**Verse 1**

לְדָוִד ׀ יְהֹוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי, מִמִּי אִירָא; יְהֹוָה מָעוֹז־חַיַּי, מִמִּי אֶפְחָד׃

> Of David.
> YHWH is my light and my rescue — of whom shall I be afraid? YHWH is the fortress of my life — of whom shall I be terrified?

The verse rhymes its confidence against its fear. Listen to the possessives: `'ō-R**IY**`, `wə-yish-ʿ**IY**`, `ḥay-Y**AY**` — my light, my rescue, my life — and then the challenge that answers them, `mim-M**IY**`, "from whom?", twice. The suffix that claims God and the interrogative that names the threat are one vowel apart. The line dismisses the enemy in the enemy's own sound.

The two fear-verbs are not interchangeable, and Malbim's lexical notes make the distinction that most translations flatten: הַיִּרְאָה הוּא מִפְּנֵי דָּבָר יָדוּעַ, וְהַפַּחַד הוּא מִפְּנֵי דָּבָר בִּלְתִּי יָדוּעַ וּמְקוֹרוֹ בְּעֶצֶם הַמְפַחֵד, "fear is of a known thing; dread is of an unknown thing, and its source lies in the essence of the one dreading." So the pairing is not decorative repetition but a sweep: אִירָא covers the enemy you can see and count, אֶפְחָד the one you cannot — the dread that generates itself. Both threat-types will materialize: an army in v. 3, and something far less visible in v. 12.

That second verb is nearly a hapax in this inflection. The form אֶפְחָד occurs only twice in the Bible, and its twin is Isaiah 12:2 — a verse that turns out to hold almost the entire vocabulary of Psalm 27's confidence in a single breath: הִנֵּה אֵל יְשׁוּעָתִי אֶבְטַח וְלֹא אֶפְחָד כִּי־עׇזִּי וְזִמְרָת יָהּ יְהוָה, "Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust and not dread, for my strength and song is Yah, YHWH." Salvation (cf. יִשְׁעִי), trust (cf. בּוֹטֵחַ, v. 3), dread, strength (cf. מָעוֹז), and song (cf. אֲזַמְּרָה, v. 6) — five of Psalm 27's load-bearing words in one line. This is not quotation in either direction; it is a shared idiom, a formula of the confident song, and it tells us that verse 1 opens in a register its first hearers would have recognized instantly.

אוֹרִי, "my light," is nonetheless the psalm's oddity. Nowhere else does anyone in the Psalter attach the possessive **י** to אוֹר and hand the result to God. The usual idiom keeps its distance: אוֹר פָּנֶיךָ, "the light of Your face." And light here is not a lamp for the road. Job 33:28 uses it for life itself restored: פָּדָה נַפְשׁוֹ מֵעֲבֹר בַּשָּׁחַת וְחַיָּתוֹ בָּאוֹר תִּרְאֶה, "He redeemed his soul from crossing into the pit, and his life shall look on the light." Isaiah 5:30 uses its absence for the collapse of hope: וְהִנֵּה־חֹשֶׁךְ צַר וָאוֹר חָשַׁךְ בַּעֲרִיפֶיהָ, "and behold, darkness and distress, and the light has darkened in its clouds." The epithet is thus already reaching toward verse 13's אֶרֶץ חַיִּים, "land of the living." Ibn Ezra, more prosaically, distributes the pair across the day: אוֹרִי — בַּלַּיְלָה שֶׁאֵין שָׁם אוֹר, כִּי הַנֶּפֶשׁ תִּפְחַד, "my light — at night, when there is no light, for the soul dreads."

The Septuagint supplies the psalm with a biography the Hebrew withholds. Its superscription reads πρὸ τοῦ χρισθῆναι, "before he was anointed" — placing the whole poem in David's fugitive years, before the crown, when the confidence of verse 1 was an assertion against all visible evidence. It is an interpretation, not a translation, and it has shaped every reader who came to the psalm through Greek or Latin.

**Verse 2**

בִּקְרֹב עָלַי ׀ מְרֵעִים לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי, צָרַי וְאֹיְבַי לִי; הֵמָּה כָשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ׃

> When evildoers close in on me to eat my flesh — my foes and my enemies — they are the ones who stumbled and fell.

Three words for opponent in one verse — מְרֵעִים, "evildoers"; צָרַי, "my foes"; אֹיְבַי, "my enemies" — and then two verbs of collapse in the perfect tense, as though the falling had already happened. The approach is still in progress (בִּקְרֹב is the infinitive of an action underway) while the downfall is reported as finished business. The grammar puts the outcome behind him before the assault has arrived.

Malbim's word-notes re-read the opening preposition and change the picture. He takes בִּקְרֹב not as enemies drawing near *to me* but as conspirators drawing near *to one another*: שֶׁיִּתְקָרְבוּ זֶה לָזֶה לְדַבֵּר בַּסֵּתֶר, "that they draw close to each other to speak in secret" — and עָלַי as בַּעֲבוּרִי, "concerning me." The verse then opens not with a charge but with a huddle. Given that the psalm's actual danger turns out to be a courtroom (v. 12), the whispered conference is the more frightening image, and the more likely one.

The eating idiom is the verse's engine. לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי, "to eat my flesh," reads as pure predation, and the biblical field for devouring is dense with beasts and swords: Jeremiah 12:9 summons them — אִסְפוּ כָּל־חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה הֵתָיוּ לְאָכְלָה, "gather all the beasts of the field, bring them to devour"; 2 Kings 10:25 kills לְפִי־חָרֶב, "by the mouth of the sword," a blade imagined as a mouth. But Akkadian legal idiom knows a second sense: *akālu karṣī*, literally "to eat pieces," is the standard phrase for slander before a tribunal, surviving into Aramaic as אֲכַל קוּרְצָא, the expression Daniel 3:8 uses for informers denouncing the Jews. Both senses are live here, and the psalm will not let us choose: the mouths that eat flesh in verse 2 are the mouths that breathe violence in verse 12.

**Verse 3**

אִם־תַּחֲנֶה עָלַי ׀ מַחֲנֶה, לֹא־יִירָא לִבִּי; אִם־תָּקוּם עָלַי מִלְחָמָה, בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ׃

> If a camp encamps against me, my heart will not fear; if war rises up against me, in this I am trusting.

The first clause is a pun with a straight face: `**t**a-ḥa-NEH` and `**m**a-ḥa-NEH` differ by one letter. Verb and noun come from the same root חנה, so the Hebrew says, in effect, "if a camping camps against me." English cannot do it without sounding silly, which is roughly the point — the most imposing formation in the ancient world is compressed into a single repeated syllable and thereby made small. The military threat is deflated by phonetics before the heart is asked to be brave.

Note also that fear has been relocated. Verse 1 asked מִמִּי אִירָא, "of whom shall *I* be afraid?" Here it is לֹא־יִירָא לִבִּי, "my *heart* will not fear." The poet has moved the question inward, to the organ most likely to betray him, and it is that same heart he will be commanding in verse 14.

בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ, "in this I am trusting" — but in *what*? The demonstrative has no clear antecedent, and the commentators split cleanly. Rashi looks backward: בְּמַה שֶּׁאָמַר לְמַעְלָה ה׳ מָעוֹז חַיַּי, "in what he said above: 'the LORD is the stronghold of my life.'" Ibn Ezra allows both directions, noting that some take בְּזֹאת as הַשְּׁאֵלָה הַנִּזְכֶּרֶת אַחַר כֵּן, "the request mentioned afterward" — that is, the one thing of verse 4. Malbim's word-notes go further forward still, to verse 5's booth. I take the forward reading. The Hebrew זֹאת ordinarily points at what is coming, the syntax leaves the sentence hanging in a way the backward reading does not require, and the forward reading gives the psalm its architecture: the ground of confidence is not a proposition already stated but the request about to be made. He is confident because of what he is going to ask for.

**Verse 4**

אַחַת ׀ שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהֹוָה, אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ: שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהֹוָה כׇּל־יְמֵי חַיַּי, לַחֲזוֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְהֹוָה וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ׃

> One thing I have asked of YHWH, that is what I seek: to dwell in the house of YHWH all the days of my life, to gaze on the loveliness of YHWH, and to inquire in His palace.

Two tenses do quiet work here. שָׁאַלְתִּי is completed — "I have asked," a request already lodged — while אֲבַקֵּשׁ is ongoing: "I keep seeking." The asking was done once; the seeking never stops.

נֹעַם is a strange word to apply to God. It is not יֹפִי, visual beauty, but something closer to *loveliness* or *agreeableness* — used elsewhere of Wisdom's ways (דַּרְכֵי־נֹעַם, Prov 3:17), of pleasant speech (אִמְרֵי־נֹעַם, Prov 15:26), and, unforgettably, of Jonathan: נָעַמְתָּ לִּי מְאֹד, "you were very lovely to me" (2 Sam 1:26). Of God it occurs only here and at Psalm 90:17. The Ugaritic cognate *n'm* is used of the divine loveliness of Baal and Anat, so the register is old and cultic; but the Hebrew has domesticated it into something almost affectionate. He does not want to see God's power. He wants to see what is *lovely* about Him.

The last infinitive, וּלְבַקֵּר, is genuinely contested, and Rashi preserves the argument in miniature. He reports Dunash ben Labrat's reading — לֵירָאוֹת שָׁם בְּכָל בֹּקֶר וָבֹקֶר, "to appear there every single morning," deriving the verb from בֹּקֶר, morning — against Menachem ben Saruq, who connected it to Leviticus 27:33, לֹא יְבַקֵּר בֵּין טוֹב לָרָע, "he shall not inspect between good and bad," i.e. to examine or inquire. Rashi sides with Dunash. Given that the psalm's crisis turns out to be a trial with perjured witnesses, I side with Menachem: הֵיכָל is a palace as much as a temple, the room where verdicts are issued, and לְבַקֵּר there means to bring an inquiry. The Greek agrees — ἐπισκέπτεσθαι, to inspect or examine. The one thing he wants is a hearing.

**Verse 5**

כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי ׀ בְּסֻכֹּה בְּיוֹם רָעָה, יַסְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵתֶר אׇהֳלוֹ, בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי׃

> For He will store me away in His booth on an evil day; He will conceal me in the concealment of His tent; on a rock He will lift me high.

Three shelters, and they are not synonyms. Malbim's word-notes distinguish them by degree of disappearance: הַצָּפוּן הוּא שֶׁלֹּא תִרְאֵהוּ הָעַיִן לְפִי שָׁעָה … וְהַהֶסְתֵּר הוּא הַנִּסְתָּר לְגַמְרֵי, "what is *tsafun* is what the eye does not see for a while; what is *nistar* is completely hidden" — and the tent is more than the booth. So the verse escalates inward: temporarily out of sight, then wholly out of sight, then — reversing direction — up on a rock where being seen no longer matters.

The first verb is worth its own moment. צפן does not simply mean conceal; it means to hide *because the thing is precious*. It is what Jochebed does with the infant Moses — וַתִּצְפְּנֵהוּ שְׁלֹשָׁה יְרָחִים, "and she hid him three months" (Exod 2:2) — and what Rahab does with the spies (Josh 2:4). It is also what God does with stored blessings: מָה רַב־טוּבְךָ אֲשֶׁר־צָפַנְתָּ לִּירֵאֶיךָ, "how great is Your goodness that You have stored up for those who fear You" (Ps 31:19). The psalmist is not merely concealed; he is *put away safe*, like something valuable.

Which brings us to Psalm 31:21, the closest parallel in the Bible and the one that shows what Psalm 27 is doing: תַּסְתִּירֵם בְּסֵתֶר פָּנֶיךָ מֵרֻכְסֵי־אִישׁ תִּצְפְּנֵם בְּסֻכָּה מֵרִיב לְשֹׁנוֹת, "You hide them in the concealment of Your face from human plots; You store them away in a booth from the strife of tongues." Every element of our verse is there — hiding, concealment, booth, the same two roots — with one addition: in Psalm 31 the hiding place is God's *face*. Psalm 27 will split that bundle apart four verses from now and make the face the one thing that must not be hidden.

Rashi refuses to let the shelter stay metaphorical. He anchors it to a specific attic: the verse, he says following Seder Olam, speaks of Joash son of Ahaziah, שֶׁהִסְתִּירָתוֹ יְהוֹשֶׁבַע אֲחוֹתוֹ בַּעֲלִיַּת בֵּית קָדְשֵׁי הַקֳּדָשִׁים, "whom his sister Jehosheba hid in the upper chamber of the Holy of Holies" — an infant king concealed in the Temple for six years while Athaliah hunted the royal line (2 Kgs 11:3). The sanctuary as a literal hiding place for a hunted child. That is not the psalm's plain sense, but it is a startlingly good illustration of it.

And here the tradition inverted itself. Mizrachi and Sephardic communities took this verse's tent into the memorial prayer for the dead: מֶלֶךְ מַלְכֵי הַמְּלָכִים בְּרַחֲמָיו יַסְתִּירֵהוּ בְּצֵל כְּנָפָיו וּבְסֵתֶר אָהֳלוֹ לַחֲזוֹת בְּנוֹעַם יְהֹוָה וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ, "the King of kings in His mercy shall conceal him in the shade of His wings and in the concealment of His tent, to gaze on the loveliness of YHWH and to inquire in His palace." A man asking to be hidden while alive became the formula for where the dead are kept — and, as we will see at verse 10, the psalm's own vocabulary quietly licenses the move.

The African-American spiritual "No Hiding Place," first collected in print in 1907 but far older in oral circulation, is the exact photographic negative of this verse. Where the psalmist has a rock that lifts him, the spiritual's singer runs to the rocks and finds them hostile:

> There's no hiding place down here,
> There's no hiding place down here.
> Went to the rock for to hide my face,
> Rock cried out, "No hiding place,"
> There's no hiding place down here.

The song personifies the landscape — the rock speaks, and what it says is a refusal. The imagery is drawn from apocalyptic (Rev 6:16, itself drawing on Hos 10:8), where the terrified hide in caves from the face of the one on the throne. But sung by people held in slavery, the line acquired a second and flatly literal meaning: there is nowhere on this continent to go. The comparison measures exactly what Psalm 27 is claiming and how much it costs. Both texts agree that survival depends on finding cover in the physical world. The psalmist asserts that the cover exists and belongs to God; the spiritual reports that the same stone, asked the same question, answered no.

**Verse 6**

וְעַתָּה יָרוּם רֹאשִׁי עַל אֹיְבַי סְבִיבוֹתַי, וְאֶזְבְּחָה בְאׇהֳלוֹ זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה; אָשִׁירָה וַאֲזַמְּרָה לַיהֹוָה׃

> And now my head is raised above my enemies around me, and I will sacrifice in His tent sacrifices of the war-shout; I will sing and make music to YHWH.

The raised head is an ancient status-marker, and its most precise biblical use is Pharaoh's cupbearer: יִשָּׂא פַרְעֹה אֶת־רֹאשֶׁךָ וַהֲשִׁיבְךָ עַל־כַּנֶּךָ, "Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore you to your post" (Gen 40:13). The head goes up when rank is restored — which is what a falsely accused man wants from a court.

Compare Psalm 13, which shares this psalm's vocabulary and reverses its assignments. There the poet asks עַד־אָנָה יָרוּם אֹיְבִי עָלָי, "how long will my enemy be raised over me?" Same verb, same preposition, opposite occupant. Psalm 27 takes the position back: יָרוּם רֹאשִׁי עַל אֹיְבַי, "my head is raised above my enemies." Across this psalm the verb רום is reserved for God and the speaker; the enemies only ever get קום, the plainer verb for standing up (vv. 3, 12). The vertical vocabulary is partitioned, and the enemy never once receives the good verb.

זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה is untranslatable in one register because תְּרוּעָה sits in two at once. It is the battle-alarm — וַהֲרֵעֹתֶם בַּחֲצֹצְרוֹת, the war-blast of Numbers 10:9 — and it is the acclamation of a king: וּתְרוּעַת מֶלֶךְ בּוֹ, "and the king's shout is among them" (Num 23:21). The Greek keeps the ambiguity honestly with ἀλαλαγμός, which means both a war-cry and a shout of joy. Malbim presses the fusion into a claim: the victorious soldier offers thank-offerings for the trumpet-blast of his battle, אֲבָל אָנֹכִי שֶׁאֲנַצֵּחַ עַל יְדֵי שֶׁאֶהְיֶה בְּאֹהֶל ה׳ אִם כֵּן יְשִׁיבַת הָאֹהֶל הִיא תְּרוּעַת הַמִּלְחָמָה וְנִצְחוֹנָהּ, "but as for me, who win by being in the tent of the LORD — then sitting in the tent *is* the war-blast and the victory." The victory whose trumpet he sounds is the act of staying indoors.

**Verse 7**

שְׁמַע־יְהֹוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא, וְחׇנֵּנִי וַעֲנֵנִי׃

> Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call; be gracious to me and answer me.

Nothing prepares us for this. Six verses of statement, and then three imperatives in eleven words. The escalation is logical — שְׁמַע, hear; חׇנֵּנִי, be gracious; עֲנֵנִי, answer — reception, then disposition, then response. The Greek collapses two of the three, rendering both "hear" and "answer me" with the same intensified verb εἰσάκουσον, "hear thoroughly," which loses the middle term and with it the plea for grace as something distinct from a reply.

The word order also shifts. קוֹלִי, "my voice," comes before its verb — the sound of the cry is put forward as the thing God is asked to attend to, before we are told there is any content to it.

This verse became the voice of a congregation. In the Ashkenazi Rosh Hashanah machzor it is stitched into a piyyut inside the cantor's repetition, where the שליח ציבור speaks for everyone: שֻׁלַּחְתִּי בְּמַלְאֲכוּת סֶגֶל חֲבוּרָה … שָׁפַכְתִּי שִׂיחַ לְבַקֵּשׁ עֲתִירָה. שְׁמַע יְהֹוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא, "I have been sent on the errand of a treasured company … I have poured out speech to seek entreaty. Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call." And in the Mizrachi/Sephardic Yom Kippur eve Selichot it stands in a chain of verses that runs straight out of the Shema: שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ יְהוָה אֶחָד. שְׁמַע־יְהוָה וְחׇנֵּנִי … שְׁמַע־יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא, "Hear, Israel: YHWH our God, YHWH is one. Hear, YHWH, and be gracious to me … Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call." Israel's command to hear becomes Israel's request to be heard, on the same verb.

**Verse 8**

לְךָ ׀ אָמַר לִבִּי בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי, אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ יְהֹוָה אֲבַקֵּשׁ׃

> On Your behalf my heart has said, "Seek My face." Your face, YHWH, I seek.

The syntax has defeated a great many readers, and one of them said so in print. The publisher of a nineteenth-century edition of Radak appended a note at this verse: מְאֹד נִשְׁתּוֹמַמְתִּי עַל הַמַּרְאֶה כִּי אֵין גַּם אֶחָד מִן הַפָּרְשִׁים הָרַבִּים מְפָרֵשׁ הַפָּסוּק הַזֶּה כָּרָאוּי, אֲשֶׁר הוּא בְּעֵינֵיהֶם כֻּלּוֹ מִקְשֶׁה, וְהוּא בֶּאֱמֶת זָהָב טָהוֹר — "I was astonished at the sight, that not one of the many commentators explains this verse properly; to them it is all hard, and it is really pure gold."

The difficulty is precise. בַּקְּשׁוּ is a plural imperative, "seek!" addressed to more than one person — inside a prayer spoken by one man. Rashi's solution is the elegant one: the heart is God's messenger. בִּשְׁבִילְךָ, בִּשְׁלִיחוּתֶיךָ אוֹמֵר לִבִּי: בַּקְּשׁוּ כֻּלְּכֶם, יִשְׂרָאֵל, אֶת פָּנַי; וַאֲנִי שׁוֹמֵעַ לוֹ, "on Your behalf, as Your agent, my heart says: 'Seek, all of you, Israel, My face' — and I obey it." The plural is public liturgy, the standing summons issued to the congregation; the heart repeats it internally, and the man answers it in the singular. God's word to Israel arrives at this person by way of his own chest. Rashi supports the reading grammatically, noting that לְךָ here means "in Your stead," as in Job's אִם לָאֵל תְּרִיבוּן, "will you contend for God?"

The Septuagint had a different Hebrew in front of it, or gave up: σοὶ εἶπεν ἡ καρδία μου, Ἐξεζήτησά σε, ἐξεζήτησεν τὸ πρόσωπόν μου, "to You my heart said, 'I have sought You'; my face has sought [You]." The plural imperative is gone, the divine summons is gone, and what remains is a man simply reporting that he looked. The Greek has smoothed away precisely the dialogue that makes the Hebrew verse remarkable — the moment in which the command and the compliance happen in the same breath, in the same organ.

And this is the second of the psalm's only two occurrences of אֲבַקֵּשׁ, a form that appears nowhere else in the Psalter. The first was verse 4: אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ, "that is what I seek," of the house and the gazing. The rare verb, used twice, equates them. The one thing and the face are the same thing.

**Verse 9**

אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ ׀ מִמֶּנִּי, אַל תַּט־בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ; עֶזְרָתִי הָיִיתָ, אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי וְאַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי׃

> Do not hide Your face from me; do not turn Your servant aside in anger. You have been my help — do not cast me off and do not abandon me, God of my rescue.

Four negative petitions, and embedded among them one flat statement in the past tense: עֶזְרָתִי הָיִיתָ, "You have been my help." That is the lament's standard move — argue from the record — but its placement matters. It sits between the pleas like a stone in a current.

The two verbs of the last plea are not a doublet. Malbim's word-notes: הַנְּטִישָׁה הִיא יוֹתֵר מִן הָעֲזִיבָה, "casting off is more than abandoning" — so the psalmist rules out the worse case first and then closes the smaller one behind it. He is sealing exits.

Radak's reading of אַל תַּט־בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ is unexpected enough to be worth the room. Rather than "do not turn away in anger," he takes the verb as transitive with the psalmist as object: אַל תַּטֵּנִי וְאַל תַּטְרִידֵנִי בְּעִסְקֵי הָעוֹלָם הַזֶּה שֶׁהֵם אַף וְכַעַס לַמִּתְעַסֵּק בָּהֶם, "do not turn me aside and do not preoccupy me with the affairs of this world, which are anger and vexation to whoever is busy with them." The אַף of the verse becomes not God's anger but the aggravation of ordinary life — and the plea becomes a request to be left alone to pray. That is very likely not the plain sense. It is, however, a reading that takes verse 4's "one thing" with complete seriousness: the enemy of the contemplative is the calendar.

The opening four words are shared verbatim with Psalm 102:3, and the continuation there shows what Psalm 27 does differently: אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי בְּיוֹם צַר־לִי הַטֵּה־אֵלַי אׇזְנֶךָ, "do not hide Your face from me on the day of my distress; incline Your ear to me." Psalm 102 pairs the hidden face with an inclined ear — asking for compensation, another sense-organ. Psalm 27 asks for nothing in its place. It wants the face.

R. S. Thomas, the Welsh poet-priest who spent five decades in remote hill parishes and wrote about God chiefly as an absence, gives the verse its modern counter-statement in "The Empty Church," from *Frequencies* (1978):

> They laid this stone trap
> for him, enticing him with candles,
> as though he would come like some huge moth
> out of the darkness to beat there.
>
> ...
>
> He will not come any more
> to our lure. Why, then, do I kneel still
> striking my prayers on a stone
> heart?

Thomas is describing a church building as a device for catching God — and reporting that the device has failed. The enjambment across "stone / heart" performs the disappointment: the line ends on stone, and the next word tells you the stone is *his own*. What makes the comparison worth having is that Thomas and the psalmist share the same premise and differ only in outcome. Both assume the relationship is meant to be face-to-face and reciprocal; both take the withdrawal of the face as the worst thing that can happen; both keep kneeling. The psalmist commands God not to hide, in the confidence that a command is the right form of address. Thomas, three thousand years later, kneels in a building constructed on exactly that confidence, and asks himself why.

**Verse 10**

כִּי־אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי, וַיהֹוָה יַאַסְפֵנִי׃

> For my father and my mother have abandoned me, but YHWH will gather me in.

Eight words, and the sharpest antithesis in the psalm. Note that the verb for what the parents did, עֲזָבוּנִי, is the same root as the plea in the previous verse, אַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי, "do not abandon me." What he asks God not to do is what has already been done to him.

יַאַסְפֵנִי is the word to watch. אסף means to gather, and among its uses is legal reception into a household: Boaz says of Ruth's gleaning, and Judges 19:15 uses it of the traveler whom אֵין־אִישׁ מְאַסֵּף־אוֹתָם הַבַּיְתָה, "no man was gathering into the house" for the night. That is the sense that makes the verse a promise of adoption. But the same verb is the standard biblical euphemism for dying: וַיִּגְוַע וַיָּמָת אַבְרָהָם … וַיֵּאָסֶף אֶל־עַמָּיו, "and Abraham expired and died … and was gathered to his kin" (Gen 25:8). Jacob uses it of himself on his deathbed (Gen 49:29, 33). The Greek shut the second door: προσλαμβάνω, "take to oneself, welcome," is purely positive and cannot mean death. The Hebrew leaves both open, and I think deliberately — the man who has already lost his parents is promised that he will be *gathered*, which is what one is when taken into a family and what one is when taken to join the dead. That latent second sense is very likely why the memorial liturgy of verse 5 felt at home in this psalm.

Rashi, following Vayikra Rabbah, takes the verse back before birth, and the reading is more uncomfortable than its reputation: בִּשְׁעַת תַּשְׁמִישׁ לַהֲנָאָתָן נִתְכַּוְּנוּ; כֵּיוָן שֶׁגָּמְרוּ הֲנָאָתָן, זֶה הוֹפֵךְ פָּנָיו אֵילָךְ וְזֶה הוֹפֵךְ פָּנָיו אֵילָךְ. וַה׳ יַאַסְפֵנִי — הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא שׁוֹמֵר אֶת הַטִּפָּה וְצָר אֶת הָעֻבָּר, "at the moment of intercourse they intended their own pleasure; once their pleasure was finished, this one turns his face away and that one turns her face away. But the LORD gathers me in — the Holy One guards the drop and forms the fetus." The abandonment is not an event in a biography; it is structural, built into how everyone arrives. Parents turn their faces away in the very act that makes a child. And the word for what they do — turning the face — is the word this psalm has just spent a verse begging God not to do.

Ibn Ezra, characteristically, deflates: the parents abandoned him בְּמוֹתָם, "by dying," which is simply what parents eventually do.

No verse of this psalm has had a longer afterlife in modern Jewish thought. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks returned to it across at least seven books, always for the same reason — as the guarantee that a person's worth does not depend on being wanted. Writing on Reuben's failure to save Joseph, he put it plainly: "God heeds those not heard. He loves those whom others do not love." And in a Rosh Hashanah essay: "Human parents may lose faith in a child, but God never does." A congregant once asked him why the psalm should say such a thing at all — which Jewish parent ever rejected a child? Sacks's answer was Ishmael, sent into the desert with a water-skin and hidden under a bush by a mother too pained to watch him die. It is the reading that the season supports: this psalm is recited daily from Elul through Sukkot, and Ishmael's rescue is the Torah reading of the first day of Rosh Hashanah.

Gillian Welch's "Orphan Girl," the opening track of *Revival* (1996), reaches the same theology by the shortest possible route — a catalogue of absences, then a pivot:

> I have no mother, no father,
> No sister, no brother.
> I am an orphan girl.

The song is written in the voice of someone with no living kin at all, and the verses that follow move exactly as the psalm does: from the enumerated losses to a family waiting elsewhere. Welch's contribution is to strip the claim down until only its function remains. The rhetoric of divine adoption is not, in her hands or the psalmist's, a metaphysical proposition first. It is what a person says when the alternative is having no one.

**Verse 11**

הוֹרֵנִי יְהֹוָה דַּרְכֶּךָ, וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי׃

> Teach me, YHWH, Your way, and lead me on a level path, because of those who watch me.

The verse reworks Psalm 5:9 closely enough that the two must belong to a shared prayer-template: יְהוָה נְחֵנִי בְצִדְקָתֶךָ לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי הַיְשַׁר לְפָנַי דַּרְכֶּךָ, "YHWH, lead me in Your righteousness because of those who watch me; make Your way level before me." The same three elements — the verb נחה, "lead"; the noun דֶּרֶךְ, "way"; and the phrase לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי — recur in the same order. That matters because שׁוֹרְרָי is a rare and disputed word: three occurrences in the Bible, and the lexicons hesitate between a root meaning "to watch, lie in wait" and one meaning "to be hostile." Psalm 5 settles it by context, and Radak makes the settlement explicit: הֵם הָאוֹיְבִים הַמַּבִּיטִים אוֹתוֹ בְּעַיִן רָעָה, כְּמוֹ וַיְהִי שָׁאוּל עוֹיֵן אֶת דָּוִד, "these are the enemies who look at him with an evil eye, as in 'and Saul kept eyeing David'" (1 Sam 18:9). Then he adds the sentence that changes the verse: וְהֵם חוֹשְׁבִים כִּי אֵין לוֹ חֵלֶק בַּיהוה, "and they think that he has no portion in the LORD."

That is the real threat. The watchers are not waiting to ambush him physically; they are waiting for a stumble that will confirm their theological verdict — that God has dropped him. Which is exactly what the accusers say aloud in the psalm's neighbor: אֱלֹהִים עֲזָבוֹ רִדְפוּ וְתִפְשׂוּהוּ כִּי־אֵין מַצִּיל, "God has abandoned him; pursue and seize him, for there is no rescuer" (Ps 71:11). A מִישׁוֹר, a level plain, is ground where a man cannot be seen to trip.

הוֹרֵנִי comes from ירה, the root behind תּוֹרָה — so the plea for guidance is etymologically a request for instruction. The Greek makes the connection audible where the Hebrew leaves it latent: νομοθέτησόν με, κύριε, τῇ ὁδῷ σου, "give me law, Lord, in Your way." The translator heard תורה inside the verb and legislated accordingly.

**Verse 12**

אַל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי, כִּי קָמוּ־בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, וִיפֵחַ חָמָס׃

> Do not give me over to the gullet of my foes, for false witnesses have risen against me — and one who breathes out violence.

Here the psalm finally names its crisis, and it is a courtroom. עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר is technical vocabulary: the Decalogue's לֹא־תַעֲנֶה בְרֵעֲךָ עֵד שָׁקֶר, "you shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exod 20:16), and the procedure of Deuteronomy 19:18–19, where a witness proven false receives the punishment he intended for the accused. This is a capital proceeding, and it retroactively reframes the whole psalm — the "one thing" of verse 4, the request for a level path, the seeking of the face. He has been asking for a verdict.

נֶפֶשׁ here is not "soul." It is the throat — the physiological sense, well attested where appetite is in view. Isaiah 5:14 is the terrifying instance: לָכֵן הִרְחִיבָה שְּׁאוֹל נַפְשָׁהּ וּפָעֲרָה פִיהָ לִבְלִי־חֹק, "therefore Sheol has widened its gullet and opened its mouth beyond measure." The nearest parallel to our phrase is Psalm 41:3: וְאַל־תִּתְּנֵהוּ בְּנֶפֶשׁ אֹיְבָיו, "and do not give him over to the gullet of his enemies." So the verse closes a circuit opened in verse 2, where the enemies came לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי, "to eat my flesh." The predation frame spans the psalm: they arrived with mouths, and they are still mouths.

וִיפֵחַ חָמָס, "and one who breathes out violence," completes the picture with a physiology of perjury. The root נפח/פוח means to blow or puff, and Proverbs uses it four separate times for exactly this figure: יָפִיחַ כְּזָבִים עֵד שָׁקֶר, "a false witness breathes out lies" (Prov 6:19; cf. 14:5, 19:5, 19:9). Testimony is exhalation. The lie is a physical thing pushed out of a body — which puts breath, gullet, and mouth in a single verse, all belonging to the enemy, and sets them against the one thing the psalmist has that is made of the same material: קוֹלִי, "my voice" (v. 7).

Aeschylus knew this convergence. In the *Eumenides* (458 BCE), the third play of the *Oresteia*, the Furies who pursue Orestes are described by the Pythia as she stumbles out of the temple where they lie sleeping:

> ῥέγκουσι δ᾽ οὐ πλατοῖσι φυσιάμασιν·
> ἐκ δ᾽ ὀμμάτων λείβουσι δυσφιλῆ λίβα.
>
> "They snore with breath that drives one back;
> and from their eyes they drip a loathsome ooze."

The Furies are prosecutors — that is their actual office in the play, which culminates in a trial — and Aeschylus characterizes them almost entirely through emissions: the breath that repels, the fluid that drips. He had reached, independently, for the psalmist's insight: that relentless accusation is experienced by its target not as an argument but as a bodily assault, something exhaled at you. The difference is instructive. Aeschylus resolves the horror by inventing a court — Athena empanels a jury and the Furies are institutionally retired. The psalmist already has a court, and it is not helping; the false witnesses are standing in it. He appeals past the tribunal to the judge.

**Verse 13**

לוּלֵא הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת בְּטוּב־יְהֹוָה בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים׃

> Had I not trusted that I would see the goodness of YHWH in the land of the living —

The sentence has no second half. לוּלֵא is a counterfactual particle — "had it not been that" — and it always takes a consequence: לוּלֵא חֲרַשְׁתֶּם בְּעֶגְלָתִי לֹא מְצָאתֶם חִידָתִי, "had you not plowed with my heifer, you would not have solved my riddle" (Judg 14:18); לוּלֵי יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת הוֹתִיר לָנוּ שָׂרִיד כִּמְעָט כִּסְדֹם הָיִינוּ, "had YHWH of hosts not left us a small remnant, we would have been like Sodom" (Isa 1:9). Here the "then" clause never comes.

The Masoretes flagged it. Every letter of לוּלֵא carries the *puncta extraordinaria*, and Minchat Shai records the detail that makes this instance unique: וְנָקוּד לְמַעְלָה וּמִלְּמַטָּה כֵּן הוּא בִּסְפָרִים מְדֻיָּקִין … דֵּין נָקוּד לְמַעְלָה וּלְמַטָּה וּשְׁאָרָא כִּי אִם לְעֵיל, "and it is dotted above and below — so it is in the accurate manuscripts … this one is dotted above and below, and all the rest only above." Fifteen dotted passages in the Bible; this is the only one in the Writings, and the only one anywhere marked on both sides. Whatever the scribes meant by it — a suspected intrusion, or a warning that something here is not ordinary — they preserved the difficulty rather than repairing it.

The Greek repaired it, by the simplest available method: it deleted the word. πιστεύω τοῦ ἰδεῖν τὰ ἀγαθὰ κυρίου ἐν γῇ ζώντων — "I believe that I shall see the good things of the Lord in the land of the living." The Peshitta agrees. Either both had a Hebrew text without the particle, or both did what any translator does when handed a sentence that cannot be finished. The result is a serene confession of faith where the Hebrew has a man losing his footing mid-clause.

The Talmud read the dots as a confession of a different kind. Berakhot 4a, cited by the Torah Temimah: אָמַר דָּוִד לִפְנֵי הקב"ה: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, מֻבְטָח אֲנִי בְּךָ שֶׁאַתָּה מְשַׁלֵּם שָׂכָר טוֹב לַצַּדִּיקִים לֶעָתִיד לָבֹא, אֲבָל אֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ אִם יֵשׁ לִי חֵלֶק בֵּינֵיהֶם אִם לָאו — שֶׁמָּא יִגְרֹם הַחֵטְא, "David said before the Holy One: Master of the universe, I am confident in You that You pay good reward to the righteous in the world to come — but I do not know whether I have a share among them or not, lest sin cause otherwise." The derivation is precise about which word carries it. The verse's dotted particle is *conditional*: the whole clause hangs on an "if," and the sages read the dots as marking that conditionality and applying it not to God's reliability but to David's own standing. God will certainly pay; what is uncertain, and what the dots register, is whether this particular man is on the list. It is not skepticism about the doctrine. It is a man declining to assume he is included in it.

Rabbi Jonathan Sacks named the device by its Greek term and put the verse alongside Lear:

> I will have such revenges on you both
> That all the world shall — I will do such things —
> What they are yet, I know not; but they shall be
> The terrors of the earth!

Lear breaks off twice because rage has outrun his vocabulary. The psalmist breaks off because dread has. Sacks's observation — that the effect is powerful precisely because the poet "leaves the next words unsaid, as if overcome by a sense of the unspeakable despair that would follow" — is exactly right, and it explains why the tradition would not let the Greek's tidy solution stand.

Modern history took the fragment at its word. During the Second World War the psalm was recited in clandestine minyanim in the Warsaw, Łódź and Vilna ghettos through the month of Elul, and this verse in particular — the unfinished one — was used as an epitaph and a motto of endurance. There is a certain fitness in that. A generation that could not say how its own sentence would end adopted the one verse in Scripture that stops.

Strip away the dots, the particle, the versions, the Talmud, and what is left is a man who has been claiming for twelve verses that he is not afraid, opening his mouth to say what would have become of him without his faith — and finding that he does not have the word.

**Verse 14**

קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהֹוָה, חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ, וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהֹוָה׃

> Wait for YHWH; be strong, and let your heart be firm; and wait for YHWH.

The psalm's last verse is the only one addressed to a "you," and the change is abrupt: thirteen verses of first-person speech, and then someone is being told what to do. Radak identifies the addressee without hesitation — אֲנִי אוֹמֵר כְּנֶגֶד עַצְמִי כָּל הַיּוֹם: קַוֵּה אֶל ה׳, "I say to myself all day long: wait for the LORD." The heart that was told not to fear in verse 3 and that relayed God's summons in verse 8 is now given an order.

חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ is not generic encouragement. It is the commissioning formula, and it belongs to one specific scene: Moses installing Joshua. חֲזַק וֶאֱמָץ כִּי אַתָּה תָּבוֹא אֶת־הָעָם הַזֶּה אֶל־הָאָרֶץ, "be strong and courageous, for you shall bring this people into the land" (Deut 31:7); repeated by God at Deut 31:23; four times to Joshua in the first chapter of Joshua; and by David to Solomon at the commissioning for the Temple (1 Chr 28:20). It is what you say to a man about to lead an army across a river. Psalm 27 applies it to sitting still.

And the borrowing brings something with it. Twice in its native context the formula continues with a specific guarantee: לֹא יַרְפְּךָ וְלֹא יַעַזְבֶךָּ, "He will not fail you and He will not forsake you" (Deut 31:6; 1 Chr 28:20). The verb is עזב — the psalm's own word, the one his parents did to him in verse 10 (עֲזָבוּנִי) and the one he begged God not to do in verse 9 (אַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי). The psalmist ends by quoting a formula whose scriptural continuation is precisely the promise he has spent five verses asking for. He does not print the promise. He quotes the words that summon it.

קוה carries an image the English "wait" loses. The noun קָו is a measuring cord, and the verb's sense of expectation seems to run through the idea of something drawn taut — hope as tension, not as passivity. The Greek chose ὑπόμεινον, "remain under," the word for bearing a weight without moving. Both are more strenuous than waiting.

The Talmud drew the practical conclusion, and Rashi puts it in six words at this verse: וְאִם לֹא תִתְקַבֵּל תְּפִלָּתְךָ, חֲזוֹר וְקַוֵּה, "and if your prayer is not accepted, go back and hope again." Berakhot 32b makes the ruling explicit: אִם רָאָה אָדָם שֶׁהִתְפַּלֵּל וְלֹא נַעֲנָה — יַחֲזוֹר וְיִתְפַּלֵּל, "if a person sees that he prayed and was not answered, let him go back and pray." The derivation is the doubling itself. If one קַוֵּה sufficed, the second would be redundant; since the verse says it twice with an interval between, the sages read the second as the instruction for what to do when the first has produced nothing. The psalm's most conspicuous formal feature — the repetition that frames the verse — becomes a rule about unanswered prayer.

The Czech immunologist and poet Miroslav Holub, who spent his working life in a laboratory and wrote poems in the plainest available Czech, built "Dveře" ("The Door," 1961) on the identical structure — the imperative repeated at both ends, with the worst possibilities catalogued between:

> Jdi a otevři dveře.
> I kdyby tam byla jen
> tikající tma,
> i kdyby tam bylo jen
> duté vanutí,
> i kdyby tam
> nic
> nebylo,
> jdi a otevři dveře.
>
> "Go and open the door.
> Even if there is only
> the ticking dark,
> even if there is only
> a hollow gust,
> even if there is
> nothing
> there,
> go and open the door."

Holub wrote it in Prague in the early 1960s, in a decade when the honest answer about what lay behind most doors was *nothing good*. His poem's engineering is exactly the psalm's: the command, then an inventory of what may make the command pointless, then the command again, unaltered. Neither poet argues. Both understand that a reason would be beside the point — that at the end of a bad enough case, the only thing left to say is the thing you already said, said again. The psalmist has one advantage Holub declines to claim, and it is the whole of verse 13: he believes there is goodness behind the door. He simply cannot finish the sentence that says so.
