**Verse 1**

לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־הַגִּתִּית; לְאָסָף.

> For the leader, on the gittith; of Asaph.

The word הַגִּתִּית heads only three psalms, 8, 81, and 84. Its meaning is uncertain. Rashi gives the usual medieval answer, כְּלִי שִׁיר הַבָּא מִגַּת, "a musical instrument that comes from Gath," the Philistine city where David's ark-keeper Obed-edom the Gittite came from. The Septuagint read the consonants as גַּת, "winepress," and translated ὑπὲρ τῶν ληνῶν (*hyper tōn lēnōn*), "for the winepresses." That reading suits this psalm better than it suits the other two. The festival the psalm calls for falls at the autumn ingathering, and the oracle ends with a table set with wheat and honey. A midrash on Psalm 84 makes the winepress an image of judgment, citing Isaiah 63, "I trod the peoples in My anger." In Psalm 81 the grapes are not trampled. The winepress image does come back in another form, though: in v. 13 Israel is left to sit like an unsealed vat.

The name לְאָסָף, "of Asaph," places the psalm within a guild of Levitical singers who, according to 1 Chronicles 25:1, "prophesied with lyres." Their psalms (50, 78, 82) are the ones in which a choir's song is interrupted by a divine verdict. Psalm 81 follows that pattern. The Asaphites were Levites, which matters for v. 8.

**Verse 2**

הַרְנִינוּ לֵאלֹקִים עוּזֵּנוּ; הָרִיעוּ לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב.

> Sing out to God our strength; raise a shout to the God of Jacob.

The exact form הַרְנִינוּ appears in only one other place in the Bible, the last verse of the Song of Moses: הַרְנִינוּ גוֹיִם עַמּוֹ, "Sing out, O nations, His people" (Deut 32:43). So the psalm opens with the word that ends Moses' poem. That is worth remembering: in its last verse the psalm will take its food from that same Song, the wheat-fat and the honey from the rock of Deuteronomy 32:13–14. The first line also echoes the Song of the Sea. עוּזֵּנוּ, "our strength," together with זִמְרָה, "song," in the next verse, forms the pair עָזִּי וְזִמְרָת יָ‑הּ, "my strength and my song is Yah" (Exod 15:2), and the timbrel of v. 3 is Miriam's. The festival opens in the voice of the exodus songs.

The second verb has a stranger later history. הָרִיעוּ, "raise a shout," is from the same root as תְּרוּעָה, the blast of Yom Teruah. The Aramaic Targum of this verse translates it יַבְּבוּ, from the root יבב, and that same root, in the Aramaic rendering of the Torah's verse, is what the Talmud (Rosh Hashanah 33b) relies on to determine how the shofar should actually sound. The Torah calls the day יוֹם תְּרוּעָה (Num 29:1); the Targum renders it יוֹם יַבָּבָא; and the only other יבב in Scripture is Sisera's mother waiting for a son who will not come home: בְּעַד הַחַלּוֹן נִשְׁקְפָה וַתְּיַבֵּב אֵם סִיסְרָא, "through the window she looked out and wailed, Sisera's mother" (Judg 5:28). The rabbis then argued over whether her cry was a groan or a sob, and Rabbi Abbahu in Caesarea instituted both. The result is that the festival "shout" this verse calls for, which its Targum renders with the same root, is sounded today in the synagogue as a mother's crying. The psalm itself goes in the same direction, beginning in jubilation and ending in the longing of v. 14.

The Alshich notices that the verse has two verbs and two names for God, and he reads them as two congregations. Those who are righteous may הַרְנִינוּ, ring out in joy. Those who can only claim descent, "the God of Jacob," should הָרִיעוּ, give the broken, humbled cry. On his reading the opening verse is already sorting the crowd.

**Verse 3**

שְׂאוּ־זִמְרָה וּתְנוּ־תֹף, כִּנּוֹר נָעִים עִם־נָבֶל.

> Lift up a song and strike the timbrel, the sweet lyre with the harp.

The verbs are odd. You do not usually "lift" a song or "give" a drum; Hebrew lifts a voice and strikes a timbrel. Ibn Ezra explains that וּתְנוּ also governs the strings: "give" the timbrel, and give the lyre and harp too. The lyre and harp come with no verb of their own, as if the list were going too fast to bother.

The Talmud (Arakhin 11a) finds law in the first verb. To show that the Temple offerings had to be accompanied by Levitical song, one sage cites this verse. A baraita goes further, to Numbers 7:9, where the Kohathites carried the holy objects בַּכָּתֵף יִשָּׂאוּ, "on the shoulder they shall carry." Since "on the shoulder" already implies carrying, it argues, יִשָּׂאוּ must mean something else, and אֵין יִשָּׂאוּ אֶלָּא לְשׁוֹן שִׁירָה, וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר: שְׂאוּ זִמְרָה, "yisa'u means nothing but song, and so it says: lift up a song." In this reading the Levite's shoulder carries music. The next shoulder in the psalm, in v. 7, carries Pharaoh's bricks.

Rashi enjoys the harp, נָבֶל. He asks why it has that name and answers שֶׁהוּא מְנַבֵּל כָּל מִינֵי זֶמֶר, "because it puts all other kinds of music to shame," a pun on נבל, "to disgrace." The joke has an edge in this psalm. The exact instruments listed here, lyre, harp, and timbrel, are the ones Isaiah lists at the drinking-feasts of people who "do not regard the work of the LORD" (Isa 5:12). Amos has God say, הָסֵר מֵעָלַי הֲמוֹן שִׁרֶיךָ וְזִמְרַת נְבָלֶיךָ לֹא אֶשְׁמָע, "Take away from Me the noise of your songs; the melody of your harps I will not hear" (Amos 5:23). There the harp is played and God declines to listen. Psalm 81 reverses Amos: the harps play, God listens, and the people are the ones who will not hear.

**Verse 4**

תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר, בַּכֶּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ.

> Blow the shofar at the new moon, at the full moon, for the day of our feast.

The difficult word is בַּכֶּסֶה. The Masoretic note says it appears nowhere else spelled with ה. Its only relative is in Proverbs 7:20, where the adulteress assures the young man that her husband is far away: לְיוֹם הַכֶּסֶא יָבֹא בֵיתוֹ, "on the day of the keseh he will come home." Phoenician and Syriac use the related word for the full moon, and I translate it that way here. The parallelism then sets the two ends of the month side by side, the new moon of Tishrei 1 and the full moon of Sukkot on Tishrei 15. The Talmud derived the opposite sense from the root כסה, "cover": the festival "on which the moon is covered," which can only be Rosh Hashanah. Ibn Ezra mentions both and settles on a neutral "fixed time." The Septuagint's ἐν εὐσήμῳ ἡμέρᾳ (*en eusēmō hēmera*), "on a well-marked day," shows the Greek translator avoiding a decision.

The psalm does not need to choose, and a tide table shows why. Spring tides, the highest of the month, come twice in each lunar month, at the new moon and at the full moon, when sun, earth, and moon are in line. As NOAA puts it, "During full or new moons — which occur when the Earth, sun, and moon are nearly in alignment — average tidal ranges are slightly larger." The two phases look opposite, one dark and one bright, but they have the same effect. The psalm sounds its horn at both, at the two points of the month when the pull is strongest.

The Proverbs verse had its own afterlife. In Sanhedrin 96b, Ammon and Moab encourage Nebuchadnezzar to attack Jerusalem by reading "the husband is not at home" as God being away, and they assure him that even repentance cannot bring God back before the time fixed for his return: אֵין כֶּסֶא אֶלָּא זְמַן, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר בַּכֵּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ, "keseh means nothing but an appointed time, as it says, at the keseh for our feast-day." In Proverbs an absent husband comes home on the keseh. In this psalm, on the keseh, someone who has seemed absent is about to speak.

The verse ends on חַגֵּנוּ, "our feast." That is the last time the congregation calls anything its own.

**Verse 5**

כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא, מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב.

> For it is a statute for Israel, a judgment of the God of Jacob.

This is where the hymn ends, and it ends with the language of law. The two nouns are close synonyms, and Malbim separates them along a line that runs through all his work: חֹק is a law whose reason is not known, מִשְׁפָּט one whose reason is. For Israel the shofar is a chok, done because it is commanded. For the God of Jacob it is a mishpat, and he knows the reason. On this view the verse says that the people blow without understanding, and the one who does understand is about to explain. The oracle that follows supplies that explanation.

The Jerusalem Talmud (Rosh Hashanah 1:3) reads the verse much more boldly. If the human court decides to postpone the new moon by a day, God tells his angels to put off the session of heaven's court: הֶעֱבִירוּ בִימָה לְמָחָר… שֶׁנִּמְלְכוּ בָנַיי לְעָבְרָהּ לְמָחָר, "remove the dais until tomorrow… for My children have decided to put it off until tomorrow." The proof is this verse, read as a conditional: אִם אֵינוֹ חוֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל כִּבְיָכוֹל אֵינוֹ מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "if it is not a statute for Israel, then, as it were, it is not a judgment for the God of Jacob." The heavenly court sits when Israel's calendar says it sits. It is a fitting reading of the verse that hands the festival over to divine speech: before God speaks, the rabbis give the people's calendar authority over when God may judge.

There is also a buried phrase in the verse. "Set a חֹק" is Joseph's own action in Egypt: וַיָּשֶׂם אֹתָהּ יוֹסֵף לְחֹק, "Joseph set it as a statute" (Gen 47:26), the decree that one fifth of the harvest goes to Pharaoh. The next verse names Joseph.

**Verse 6**

עֵדוּת בִּיהוֹסֵף שָׂמוֹ, בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם; שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע.

> He set it as a testimony in Jehoseph when he went out over the land of Egypt; a language I did not know I hear.

The phrase בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם is not about leaving Egypt. It is nearly word for word what Genesis says when Joseph is made vizier: וַיֵּצֵא יוֹסֵף עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "and Joseph went out over the land of Egypt" (Gen 41:45), going out on tour as its ruler. The preposition עַל, "over," gives this away. Here the psalm is not recalling the exodus but the moment when a Hebrew prisoner became the second man in the kingdom, and the Talmud (Rosh Hashanah 11a) dates Joseph's release from prison to Rosh Hashanah on the strength of this verse. The psalm calls him יְהוֹסֵף, with a ה inserted, a spelling found nowhere else. The Talmud (Sotah 10b) explains it as a reward: יוֹסֵף שֶׁקִּדֵּשׁ שֵׁם שָׁמַיִם בַּסֵּתֶר — זָכָה וְהוֹסִיפוּ לוֹ אוֹת אַחַת מִשְּׁמוֹ שֶׁל הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, "Joseph, who sanctified the name of Heaven in secret, merited that one letter of the name of the Holy One was added to his." His virtue, refusing Potiphar's wife, was done בַּסֵּתֶר, "in secret." Two verses later God answers בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "from the hiding-place of thunder." A hidden act in this verse, a hidden voice in v. 8.

The last half-line, the hinge the introduction discussed, has one more reader worth hearing. Exodus Rabbah 5:9 describes the voice at Sinai splitting as it went out: אמר רבי יוחנן היה הקול יוצא ונחלק לשבעים קולות לשבעים לשון כדי שישמעו כל האומות, "Rabbi Yohanan said: the voice went out and divided into seventy voices, in seventy languages, so that all the nations would hear." To Israel it came in a different way: היאך הקול יוצא אצל כל ישראל כל אחד ואחד לפי כחו, "how did the voice go out to Israel? to each one according to his strength," the elders by theirs, the young men by theirs, the nursing infants by theirs. The midrash's divine voice is the opposite of "a language I did not know." It adjusts itself to every ear. The psalm's voice does not. It arrives in an unfamiliar idiom, and the listener has to adapt to it.

Developmental linguistics shows how hard that adapting is. In Janet Werker and Richard Tees's 1984 study, English-learning infants of six to eight months could hear consonant contrasts from Hindi and from Nthlakampx, a Salish language of British Columbia, that their parents could not hear, but "by 10 to 12 months the infants were performing as poorly as the young children and adults." An ear trained on its own speech loses the ability to hear the distinctions of another. "A language I did not know" and, in v. 12, "my people did not listen" may describe one condition: hearing narrowed by long habit.

A small note looking ahead. Zephaniah, describing the day God will change the nations' speech, joins the same two words this verse and the next one join: אֶהְפֹּךְ אֶל־עַמִּים שָׂפָה בְרוּרָה… לְעָבְדוֹ שְׁכֶם אֶחָד, "I will turn to the peoples a pure language… to serve Him with one shoulder" (Zeph 3:9). A new language, and a shoulder under a new master. Verse 7 turns to the shoulder.

**Verse 7**

הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ; כַּפָּיו מִדּוּד תַּעֲבֹרְנָה.

> I removed his shoulder from the burden; his palms passed free of the basket.

This is the first time God says "I," and he says it with a bodily image. He does not lift the load off the shoulder; he lifts the shoulder out from under the load. Isaiah keeps to the expected order, יָסוּר סֻבֳּלוֹ מֵעַל שִׁכְמֶךָ, "his burden shall depart from your shoulder" (Isa 10:27). The psalm's wording turns the rescued body into the object of the verb.

The second half depends on a single letter. The Masoretic text has תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "they passed away from" the basket. The Greek has αἱ χεῖρες αὐτοῦ ἐν τῷ κοφίνῳ ἐδούλευσαν (*hai cheires autou en tō kophinō edouleusan*), "his hands served in the basket," which reads תַּעֲבֹדְנָה, with ד in place of ר. One reading frees the hands; the other leaves them enslaved. The variant is more interesting for what the Hebrew psalm otherwise lacks. The root עבד, "serve, slave," does not occur anywhere in the Masoretic Psalm 81. That is notable, because both formulas the psalm draws on in vv. 10–11 contain it: the Decalogue's מִבֵּית עֲבָדִים, "from the house of slaves," and its לֹא תָעָבְדֵם, "you shall not serve them." The psalm drops the word both times. The one place it could enter, in this verse, the Hebrew has the other letter. Israel is called "my people," a people that listens, and never "my servants." Genesis has a twin of this variant in Joseph's story: Joseph הֶעֱבִיר, "moved," the Egyptians into the cities (Gen 47:21), where the Greek and the Samaritan have "enslaved" them. Joseph, who once moved a people from place to place, now carries a testimony that lets a people go.

The word דּוּד names a vessel, either a basket or a pot. Rashi goes with the pot, מֵעֲבוֹדַת עַבְדוּת לְבַשֵּׁל קְדֵרוֹת, "from the slave's work of cooking pots" for the masters. That gives the verse a kitchen alongside its brickyard, and it matters when the oracle ends with God doing the feeding. The shoulder here also looks back to v. 3, where the baraita heard song in the verb for the Levites' carrying "on the shoulder." Egypt's shoulder carried bricks.

**Verse 8**

בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ וָאֲחַלְּצֶךָּ; אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם; אֶבְחׇנְךָ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה, סֶלָה.

> In distress you called and I rescued you; I answered you from the hiding-place of thunder; I tested you at the waters of Meribah. Selah.

Three verbs in a row end in ־ךָ, "you: אֲחַלְּצֶךָּ, אֶעֶנְךָ, אֶבְחׇנְךָ, I rescued you, I answered you, I tested you." They rhyme, and they move from gift to demand. The rescue was unconditional, the answer came out of the storm, and the test came last, at the waters whose name is מְרִיבָה, "quarrel," the root of the רִיב, the covenant lawsuit. The psalm's only Selah falls on the test. Whatever the musicians did at a Selah, here they did it after the word "Meribah."

It is easy to assume that "I tested you" reverses Exodus 17, where Israel tested God at Massah and Meribah, and Psalm 95:9, אֲשֶׁר נִסּוּנִי אֲבוֹתֵיכֶם בְּחָנוּנִי, "when your fathers tested Me, tried Me." The reversal is older than this psalm, though. In Moses' blessing of Levi, God is the tester: אֲשֶׁר נִסִּיתוֹ בְּמַסָּה תְּרִיבֵהוּ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה, "whom You tested at Massah, with whom You strove at the waters of Meribah" (Deut 33:8). The exact phrase עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה with God as the subject of the verb is that verse's. The Asaphites were Levites. When their psalm has God say "I tested you at Meribah," it may be using the tradition in which the one tested was their own tribe. The singers may be hearing their own tribe's story.

Rashi, following the Mekhilta, adds a painful layer: God rescued and answered Israel even though "it was revealed and tested before Me that you were destined to provoke Me at the waters of Meribah." On this reading the rescue was given to people God already knew would fail.

The lawsuit later changed direction. Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev, the Hasidic master, was remembered for a Yiddish song-prayer in which he takes God to court on Israel's behalf. It begins, in English: "Good morning to You, Master of the world! I, Levi Yitzchak son of Sarah of Berditchev, have come to You with a lawsuit on behalf of Your people Israel. What do You want of Your people Israel?" Psalm 81 is a רִיב in which God argues with his people at Meribah. Levi Yitzchak keeps the same court, the same parties, and the same phrase, "Your people," and switches plaintiff and defendant. Both speakers claim the people as theirs, and both are sure the other side owes them an answer.

**Verse 9**

שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ; יִשְׂרָאֵל, אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי.

> Hear, my people, and I will testify against you; Israel, if you would listen to me!

The first half is nearly a quotation of another Asaphite psalm: שִׁמְעָה עַמִּי וַאֲדַבֵּרָה יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, "Hear, my people, and I will speak; Israel, and I will testify against you" (Ps 50:7). In Psalm 50 that line opens an indictment of sacrifice offered without integrity. Here it opens a recitation of the first commandments. The verb אָעִידָה picks up עֵדוּת, "testimony," from v. 6: the festival statute God placed in Joseph becomes the testimony God now brings against Joseph's descendants. Metzudat Zion's explanation of the verb belongs to a courtroom: a warning is given in front of witnesses so that the accused cannot later deny having been warned.

The second half begins a sentence and does not complete it. "If you would listen to me," and then what? The verse leaves the "then" unsaid. Meiri sees two kinds of hearing in the verse's two verbs: the first שְׁמַע is "hearing with the ear," and the second, אִם תִּשְׁמַע לִי, is "accepting the words." Five verses pass with the missing consequence still unstated. When it comes, in vv. 15–17, it comes in the grammar of something that did not happen.

**Verse 10**

לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ קֵל זָר, וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְקֵל נֵכָר.

> There shall be no strange god in you, and you shall not bow down to a foreign god.

Malbim's lexical note separates the two adjectives: נֵכָר is worse than זָר. More usefully, he notes that the verse divides the sin between inside and outside. לֹא יִהְיֶה בְךָ means there should be no idol in the heart, "no thought of idolatry"; לֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה means no bowing in the body. The commandment covers the whole person, inside and out.

Here the Greek does show something. It renders קֵל זָר as θεὸς πρόσφατος (*theos prosphatos*), "a newly arrived god." That is how the Song of Moses describes the gods Israel turned to, חֲדָשִׁים מִקָּרֹב בָּאוּ, "new ones, lately come" (Deut 32:17). Through its translation the Septuagint pulls the verse into the Deuteronomy 32 orbit the psalm moves in from beginning to end. The Greek also runs the end of v. 9 into this verse: "if you hear me, there shall be no new god in you." The prohibition becomes a consequence. If you listen, the foreign god will have nowhere to live. The Hebrew keeps a command. The Greek has turned it into a description of what a listening heart is like.

Remember the word נֵכָר, "foreign." It comes back in a related psalm when we reach v. 16.

**Verse 11**

אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם; הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ.

> I am the LORD your God, who brought you up from the land of Egypt; open your mouth wide and I will fill it.

The traditional readers of the last phrase fall into three groups. Rashi and Radak take it as an invitation to ask: לִשְׁאוֹל מִמֶּנִּי כָּל תַּאֲוַת לִבְּךָ, "to ask of Me whatever your heart desires." The Jerusalem Talmud (Ta'anit 3:6) turns that into a rule of prayer, "if you have prayed, do not keep your mouth narrow." The Babylonian Talmud (Berakhot 50a) restricts it: one may ask for limitless blessing only in matters of Torah. The Alshich offers the most surprising reading. He reads "open your mouth" against the midrash on the Song of Songs in which each of the Ten Commandments went to each Israelite and asked whether he accepted it, and each said yes, and מִיָּד הָיָה הַדִּבּוּר נוֹשְׁקוֹ עַל פִּיו, "at once the Word kissed him on the mouth," as in יִשָּׁקֵנִי מִנְּשִׁיקוֹת פִּיהוּ, "let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth." On his reading, the open mouth at Sinai is the mouth that said "yes" and was then filled with holiness.

The plain image is simpler, and it comes from the nest. Altricial chicks, the ones hatched blind and helpless, survive by gaping: they open their beaks as wide as they can and show the parent a brightly colored throat. Experiments with songbirds show that parents give more food to the redder mouths. The gape is not a request in words. It is the whole body made into a space waiting to be filled, and the parent's response to it is close to automatic. The psalm asks Israel for nothing more than that, and in the next verse Israel will not give even that.

The offer has a legal shape too. In *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* (1892), an English court held that a public advertisement promising £100 to anyone who used the smoke ball and still caught influenza was a binding promise to the whole world. Lord Justice Bowen explained, "this advertisement is an offer to pay £100 to anybody who will perform these conditions, and the performance of the conditions is the acceptance of the offer." An offer like that does not need anyone to say "I accept," and it does not expire because no one has yet replied. "Open your mouth and I will fill it" is such an offer. This is why the psalm can end without the people speaking and still leave the offer open: the only way to accept it is to open the mouth.

**Verse 12**

וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי, וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה לִי.

> But my people did not listen to my voice, and Israel was not willing for me.

The second verb is the more serious charge. אָבָה means "to be willing," and it almost always appears with a negative, as if the Bible mainly needed it to describe refusal. Isaiah separates it from hearing: אִם־תֹּאבוּ וּשְׁמַעְתֶּם, "if you are willing and you listen" (Isa 1:19). Willingness comes first. The failure in this verse is not deafness. It is not wanting. There is also a quiet irony. Deuteronomy joins the verb to "listen" once of God himself, וְלֹא־אָבָה ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ לִשְׁמֹעַ אֶל־בִּלְעָם, "the LORD your God was not willing to listen to Balaam" (Deut 23:6), and once in the law about someone who tries to lure you to other gods: לֹא־תֹאבֶה לוֹ וְלֹא תִשְׁמַע אֵלָיו, "you shall not be willing for him and you shall not listen to him" (Deut 13:9). Israel treated God the way it had been told to treat the tempter, and the way God had treated Balaam.

Melville's Bartleby, the scrivener, works the same way: he answers every request with "I would prefer not to." Bartleby is not defiant and does not argue, and he cannot be argued with, because he has stopped at the level of preference, before reasons begin. What makes the story unbearable is how quietly he refuses. The Hebrew verse, without shouting, describes the same kind of refusal: לֹא־אָבָה לִי.

The Qur'an tells the Sinai scene with a pun on this verse's two failures together: "And [recall] when We took your covenant and raised over you the mount, [saying], 'Take what We have given you with determination and listen.' They said [instead], 'We hear and disobey.' And their hearts absorbed [the worship of] the calf because of their disbelief" (2:93). In Arabic, *samiʿnā wa-ʿaṣaynā*, "we hear and we disobey," plays on Israel's own answer at Sinai, "we will do and we will hear." The Qur'an's image for the calf is drink: their hearts "were made to drink" it in. A mouth opened wide, but filled with the foreign god, is the dark counterpart of v. 11.

Malbim distinguishes the two names in the verse: שֵׁם יִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא שֵׁם מַעֲלָתָם מִצַּד עַצְמָם, וְשֵׁם עַמִּי הוּא שֵׁם הַקֶּשֶׁר שֶׁיֵּשׁ לָהֶם עִם אֱלֹקִים, "the name Israel is the name of their own dignity; the name 'my people' is the name of the bond they have with God." As "my people" they owed obedience. As "Israel" they should have wanted God for their own sake. They failed on both counts. The Chida explains the failure by pointing back to Sinai. If Israel had gone on hearing directly from God, the evil inclination would have had no power over them, but they said to Moses, "you speak with us and we will hear." He reads וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי strictly: they did not hear from Me. In his account, the people's fear of the voice is what started their deafness to it.

Set beside this verse, the Yom Kippur piyyut that Israel sings to God sounds almost like a reply: כִּי אָנוּ עַמֶּךָ וְאַתָּה אֱלֹקֵינוּ, אָנוּ בָנֶיךָ וְאַתָּה אָבִינוּ, "for we are Your people and You are our God; we are Your children and You are our Father." In the psalm God says "my people" and the people do not respond. In the liturgy the people say "Your people" every year on the Day of Atonement, as though they finally wanted to say it.

**Verse 13**

וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם; יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם.

> So I let him go in the stubbornness of their heart; let them walk in their own counsels.

The pronouns change number inside the sentence. God lets "him" go, singular, the people as one body, into the stubbornness of "their" heart, plural. Meiri explains this as the normal way Scripture talks about a community. I think it also describes what happens: once God lets go, the single "him" breaks into many hearts, each going its own way.

Rashi's gloss on the rare word שְׁרִירוּת is מַרְאִית לִבָּם, "the sight of their heart," from the root שׁוּר, "to look," as in לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי, "because of those who watch me" (Ps 5:9). On this reading the punishment is to be left walking by what the heart sees, guided by its own picture of things. Ibn Ezra derives it from שָׁרִיר, "firm," and so gets "hardness," and Radak too understands it as the heart's hardness. Both readings point to the same thing: the heart treated as its own authority.

The *Odyssey* makes this verse's argument, without the pain that follows it here. At the start of the *Odyssey*, Zeus complains to the assembled gods: "Look how mortals blame the gods! They say their evils come from us, yet they themselves, by their own recklessness, suffer griefs beyond their lot." He gives Aegisthus as his example. The gods had sent Hermes to warn him not to kill Agamemnon and not to marry his wife, and he did both. The structure is the psalm's: warnings sent, not heeded, and ruin that comes from the person's own choices. But Zeus is defending heaven's good name, and once he has made his case he moves on. The God of Psalm 81 makes the same case and cannot let it go. The next verse begins with "if only."

George Herbert shows the same scene with a different outcome. In "The Collar," the speaker rages against his religious obligations, page after page of self-will, until:

> But as I rav'd, and grew more fierce and wild

> At every word,

> Me thoughts I heard one calling, "Child";

> And I replied, "My Lord."

Herbert's God steps in at the height of the rebellion, with a single word, and the speaker answers. The God of Psalm 81 deliberately does not step in. וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ, "I let him go," is the hardest verb in the poem because it holds back exactly the call that rescues Herbert.

Remember the winepress in the superscription. A vintner does not need to poison wine to ruin it. He only needs to leave the vat unsealed. Acetic-acid bacteria are everywhere, and wine exposed to air turns to vinegar by following its own course. Judgment by release works the same way. No new force is needed, only the removal of the one that was holding things in place.

**Verses 14–15**

לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַ לִי; יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ.

> If only my people were listening to me, if Israel would walk in my ways,

כִּמְעַט אוֹיְבֵיהֶם אַכְנִיעַ; וְעַל צָרֵיהֶם אָשִׁיב יָדִי.

> in a moment I would subdue their enemies, and turn my hand against their foes.

Here at last is the "then" that v. 9 left unsaid, but now it is in the unreal mood. The grammar of hearing has moved through three stages: אִם, a real condition (v. 9); a plain negative, לֹא, a recorded failure (v. 12); and now לוּ, a wish that knows it has not come true. The verb is a participle, שֹׁמֵעַ, "were listening," a state that continues, not a single act. God is not asking for one moment of attention but for an ear that stays open.

The verbs of walking are set against each other. In v. 13 Israel יֵלְכוּ, walks in the simple form, in its own counsels. Here they would יְהַלֵּכוּ, the intensive form, walking habitually, going about their lives, in God's ways. Their own route is a single walk; God's is a way of life.

The rabbis believed God could weep. Lamentations Rabbah (Proem 24) shows him at the destruction: הָיָה הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא בּוֹכֶה וְאוֹמֵר אוֹי לִי מָה עָשִׂיתִי, הִשְׁרֵיתִי שְׁכִינָתִי לְמַטָּה בִּשְׁבִיל יִשְׂרָאֵל, וְעַכְשָׁיו שֶׁחָטְאוּ חָזַרְתִּי לִמְקוֹמִי הָרִאשׁוֹן, "the Holy One wept and said: Woe is Me, what have I done? I made My Presence dwell below for Israel's sake, and now that they have sinned I have gone back to My former place." The midrash takes the step this verse prepares. Once God has let Israel go, his "if only" is the sound of the one who let go regretting it.

Machine learning gives an exact name for what these verses do. Counterfactual Regret Minimization, the algorithm at the core of the poker programs that beat professional players, learns by working out, at every decision point, how much better each action *not* taken would have done. Its authors call this "counterfactual regret." Verses 14–15 are such a calculation spoken out loud: had they listened, the result would have been this, enemies brought down "in a moment." The difference is telling. The algorithm uses its regret to change its own future play. God's regret concerns choices that belong to another player, and he will not make those choices for them.

The second verse, v. 15, keeps a promise made at the start of the oracle. In v. 7 Israel's palms were freed from the basket. Now God's own hand appears: אָשִׁיב יָדִי, "I would turn my hand." Rashi sees something the English hides: אָשִׁיב מַכָּתִי מֵעֲלֵיכֶם לָתֵת אוֹתָהּ עֲלֵיהֶם, "I would turn My blow away from you, to lay it on them." For a hand to be turned against the enemy, it must now be somewhere else, and Rashi implies that it is currently on Israel. Isaiah uses the same phrase against Israel, וְאָשִׁיבָה יָדִי עָלַיִךְ, "I will turn My hand against you" (Isa 1:25), though to refine them, not destroy them. Malbim reads כִּמְעַט more gently than "in a moment": as soon as they began to listen even a little, מְעַט, the enemies would fall. On his reading the promise does not require perfect listening, only a beginning.

**Verse 16**

מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳ יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ, וִיהִי עִתָּם לְעוֹלָם.

> Those who hate the LORD would cringe before him, and their time would be forever.

The verb כחשׁ usually means to deny or to lie, and in this idiom it describes defeated enemies coming before the victor and faking submission. BDB's definition is close to a scene description: "cringe… originally act deceptively, feigning obedience." The closest parallel is in David's victory song: לְשֵׁמַע אֹזֶן יִשָּׁמְעוּ לִי, בְּנֵי־נֵכָר יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לִי, "at the hearing of the ear they obey me; foreigners come cringing to me" (Ps 18:45). This is the word נֵכָר, "foreign," from v. 10. In Psalm 18 foreigners obey as soon as they hear. In Psalm 81, God's own people, told to have nothing foreign in them, refuse to hear at all. Even the false submission of God's enemies would have been more than Israel offered.

The verb has a farming meaning as well. Habakkuk uses it for a failed crop: כִּחֵשׁ מַעֲשֵׂה־זַיִת, "the yield of the olive fails" (Hab 3:17), as if the tree had lied about its fruit. Ibn Ezra offers a second sense along those lines, "grow lean," as in וּבְשָׂרִי כָּחַשׁ מִשָּׁמֶן, "my flesh has grown lean, without fat" (Ps 109:24). Read that way, the enemies waste away just before the psalm sets out the fat of the wheat.

The second half is undecided. Whose "time" lasts forever? Rashi says the enemies', פּוּרְעָנוּתָם, "their punishment." Radak says Israel's, "the time of their blessing, once begun, would last forever without interruption." The pronoun suffix ־ם has referred to Israel throughout vv. 13–15, which supports Radak. Meiri reads it the same way and turns the enemies' denial toward Israel as well: out of fear they would deny to Israel that they had ever harmed it, and Israel's time would last without break, שֶׁלֹּא יִגָּלוּ עוֹד בַּגּוֹיִם, "so that they would not go into exile again among the nations." The Alshich, as he often does, hears a wish for Israel: מִי יִתֵּן וִיהִי עִתָּם זֶה לְעוֹלָם, "if only this time of theirs could last forever," their time of love when they came out of Egypt and the pure, good heart they had at Sinai. He finds in the verse the same divine "if only" that Deuteronomy 5:26 puts in God's mouth: מִי־יִתֵּן וְהָיָה לְבָבָם זֶה לָהֶם, "if only they had such a heart as this always."

The name of God here is in the third person, in the middle of God's own speech: מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳, "those who hate the LORD," where we would expect "those who hate me." The verse does not need fixing. The voice from the hinge in v. 6 may have been audible behind God's voice all along. Here, for one verse, it may come through.

**Verse 17**

וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה; וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָּ.

> And he would feed him with the fat of the wheat, and with honey from the rock I would satisfy you.

Traditional readers disagree about what the rock is. Malbim reads it as a miracle of plenty: even the bare rock would drip honey, as in the Song of Moses, וַיֵּנִקֵהוּ דְבַשׁ מִסֶּלַע, "He suckled him with honey from the crag" (Deut 32:13). Ibn Ezra relates it to the wilderness: the manna was sweeter than the finest wheat, and the water from the rock sweeter than honey. Radak's reading is the most down-to-earth and fits the previous verses best. צוּר, he says, is a "strong place," a fortress, and the honey is שְׁלַל גְּדוֹלִים וּמַעֲדַנֵּיהֶם, "the spoil of great men and their delicacies." The enemies of v. 15 would be subdued, and their stored delicacies would end up on Israel's table.

An archaeological find makes the line more striking. In 2007, at Tel Rehov in the Beth-Shean valley, Amihai Mazar's team found the oldest known apiary: rows of unfired clay cylinders, about thirty hives, from the tenth and ninth centuries BCE, with remains of bees whose closest relatives are an Anatolian subspecies, which suggests the colonies were imported at great cost. Honey in Iron Age Israel was a managed industry that required labor and equipment. "Honey from the rock" is honey nobody kept bees for, given to hands that had just been freed from the basket. The psalm's last gift requires no work.

The Latin church took this verse out of its setting and changed its meaning. *Cibavit eos ex adipe frumenti, alleluia: et de petra, melle saturavit eos*, "He fed them with the fat of the wheat, and with honey from the rock He satisfied them," is the traditional Introit of the Mass of Corpus Christi. Following the Greek, the Latin makes both verbs third person and past tense, so it loses the sudden "you" the Hebrew ends with, and it leaves out the refusal and the "if only" that come before. In the Mass the verse is praise for food already given. In the psalm the food is an offer still waiting for the mouth of v. 11.

George Herbert's "Love (III)" ends with exactly what the psalm offers and does not get: a guest who argues his way out of being fed and then gives in.

> "Truth, Lord, but I have marr'd them; let my shame

> Go where it doth deserve."

> "And know you not," says Love, "who bore the blame?"

> "My dear, then I will serve."

> "You must sit down," says Love, "and taste my meat."

> So I did sit and eat.

Herbert's guest refuses out of unworthiness; the psalm's Israel refuses because it is unwilling. In both, the host does not withdraw the food. Herbert's poem ends with a mouth that finally opens. The psalm ends one step before that, on אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָּ, "I would satisfy you." The scribes left an open space after it, the petuchah, the blank that marks the end of a section. The table is set and the place is empty.
