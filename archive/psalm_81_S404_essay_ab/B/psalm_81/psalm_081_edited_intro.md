Psalm 81 opens with a remarkable amount of noise for the Psalter. In three verses it calls for five things: shouting for joy, raising a battle-cry, striking up a song, beating the timbrel, and blowing the shofar, with lyre and harp thrown in. Then, partway through the sixth verse, an unidentified voice says שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "a language I did not know I hear." From that point to the end, no member of the congregation speaks again. The psalm sets up a festival so that it can stop it. The noise it orders is there to clear a space for a voice the singers did not summon and cannot direct. That voice turns out not to be thunder. It is longing, and it holds out an offer that it refuses to take back.

The shape is simple. Verses 2–6b are the congregation's hymn: imperatives, instruments, and a calendar date, with the reason given in law, כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא, "for it is a statute for Israel." Half a line in verse 6 then serves as a hinge. After it God speaks, without interruption, through the end of the poem. He recounts the past: Egypt, rescue, thunder, the test at Meribah. He repeats the first commandments. He states that Israel refused, and he says how he answered the refusal. He says what he wishes, and he says what he would give. The pronouns show the change. The congregation says "our" twice, אֱלֹקִים עוּזֵּנוּ, "God our strength," and לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ, "for our feast-day," and never says it again. God says עַמִּי, "my people," three times. The proper name ה׳, which the hymn never uses, is first heard when God introduces himself, and it comes back once more in verse 16. The psalm's last word is אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָּ, "I would satisfy you." The people who began as the speakers end as the ones spoken to.

### The noise was a summons, and something answered

The festival, it emerges, is Sinai performed again. The shofar is the instrument of Exodus 19, where "the sound of the shofar grew ever louder, Moses spoke, and God answered him with a voice." The congregation is told הָרִיעוּ, to raise the תְּרוּעָה, the shout. God's reply in verse 8 comes בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "from the hiding-place of thunder." The Hebrew ear hears רוּעַ in the shout and רַעַם in the thunder, as if the people's cry had been answered in the same sounds, only enormously louder. Hiddenness runs through this passage. The rabbis took the hard word בַּכֶּסֶה in verse 4 as the festival "when the moon is covered," the new moon of Rosh Hashanah. The voice speaks a language the listener did not know. The answer comes out of a hiding-place. Malbim reads the whole sequence as God speaking from concealment, once behind Pharaoh's decree releasing Joseph and once behind the noise of the storm: בְּהֶסְתֵּר יָצָא דִבּוּר זֶה מֵאֵת ה׳, "in hiddenness this speech went out from God." Rashi turns the image around, and I find his version more moving: "You called to Me in secret, between Me and you, and I answered you with a voice of thunder." Israel's quiet prayer received a public reply.

Who says "a language I did not know I hear"? The Septuagint did not like a first-person voice appearing without warning and changed it to "he heard." The Talmud attaches the words to Joseph, the subject of the half-verse before, telling how the angel Gabriel taught him the seventy languages of Pharaoh's court overnight. Some modern readers hear a Levitical prophet, one of Asaph's guild, who according to Chronicles "prophesied with lyres." I think the line is better left without a fixed owner. Its effect matters more than its speaker: a person in a crowd, in the middle of a song, suddenly picks up a voice from outside the song. Rilke's "Archaic Torso of Apollo" describes the same reversal. A man looks at a broken statue, an object of worship, and finds it looking back at him, until the poem ends: "for there is no place / that does not see you. You must change your life." In both texts something people had been staring at, or singing toward, turns around and gives an order.

Early Jewish practice seems to have noticed exactly where the psalm turns. The Jerusalem Talmud records that on Rosh Hashanah the Levites began the psalm at הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ, "I removed his shoulder from the burden," which is the first verse where God says "I." Only when the festival fell on a Thursday, the psalm's own weekday, did they sing it from the top in the morning, and even then they kept הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ for the afternoon. The later liturgy went the other way. The Shofarot section of the Rosh Hashanah Musaf opens by announcing that the shofar is Sinai's own, וּבְקוֹל שׁוֹפָר עֲלֵיהֶם הוֹפָעְתָּ, "and with the sound of the shofar You appeared to them," and then quotes Psalm 81:4–5 and stops there. It keeps the instruction to blow and leaves out the voice that answers.

### Everyone hears except the people

The leading word of the second half is שָׁמַע, "hear." Count how it is used. In verse 9 it is a command and a condition, שְׁמַע עַמִּי… אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי, "Hear, my people… Israel, if you would listen to me." The sentence trails off, with no "then" clause. In verse 12 it is negated, וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי, "but my people did not listen to my voice." In verse 14 it is a wish contrary to fact, לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַ לִי, "if only my people were listening to me." The one plain, actual act of hearing in the psalm is the אֶשְׁמָע of the hinge, and the one doing it does not know the language. Hearing does happen in the poem, but God is the one who does it: בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ וָאֲחַלְּצֶךָּ, "in distress you called and I rescued you." The people called and were answered. When God calls, they do not answer.

Psychologists have a name for the one signal that gets through noise. In Neville Moray's dichotic-listening experiments of 1959, people concentrating on a message in one ear missed nearly everything played into the other, yet some of them caught their own name. God uses that remedy. He calls through the festival noise by name, עַמִּי… יִשְׂרָאֵל, "my people… Israel," three times over, and it still does not reach them.

What he asks them to hear is the Decalogue, rearranged. The prohibition comes first and has been moved inward, לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ קֵל זָר, "there shall be no strange god in you," not "for you" as in Exodus. The self-introduction follows, and where a command should come next there is an invitation instead: הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "open your mouth wide and I will fill it." The verb comes from the same causative pattern as the opening commands. Israel was first told הַרְנִינוּ and הָרִיעוּ, to make sound. Now it is told הַרְחֶב, to make room. Over the course of the psalm the mouth sings, calls out in distress, opens wide, and is fed. Israel's part gets smaller with each step, and God's grows: he removes the burden, testifies, subdues enemies, turns his hand against them, feeds, and satisfies.

The burden he removed was a real one: כַּפָּיו מִדּוּד תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "his palms passed away from the basket." In the tomb of the vizier Rekhmire at Thebes, painted around 1450 BCE, foreign workers mix mud, mould bricks, and carry loads slung from yokes over their shoulders, while overseers watch. Egypt recorded this with pride. The psalm recalls the same scene from the other end, as shoulders that were let go. A northern audience may have heard something closer to home too. סֵבֶל, "burden," is a rare noun, and one of its few other occurrences is in 1 Kings 11:28, סֵבֶל בֵּית יוֹסֵף, "the corvée of the house of Joseph," the forced labor that Jeroboam oversaw and then turned into a revolt. The psalm has just called Israel "Joseph."

### The punishment is release, and then the wish

Israel refuses, and God's response is surprisingly restrained: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם, "so I let him go, in the stubbornness of their heart." The verb is the exodus verb, שִׁלַּח from שַׁלַּח אֶת־עַמִּי, "let my people go." God finally does exactly that. He lets them go, into their own self-will, יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם, "let them walk in their own counsels." The "strange god in you" of verse 10 now has its location: the heart of verse 13. The rabbis made that connection when they read verse 10 as the evil inclination, the foreign god that lives inside a person's body.

Prophetic literature knows how sentences like this usually end. Jeremiah 11 runs through almost the same sequence as the psalm. It has "listen to my voice," the land of milk and honey, הָעֵד הַעִדֹתִי "I warned and warned," "on the day I brought them up from Egypt," and then וְלֹא שָׁמְעוּ… וַיֵּלְכוּ… בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם הָרָע, "they did not listen… they walked in the stubbornness of their evil heart." Jeremiah closes by bringing on them "all the words of this covenant," meaning its curses. Psalm 81 follows the same steps and then says לוּ, "if only." The Talmud hears pleading even in a plainer word. Reading the אִם of Leviticus 26:3, אִם בְּחֻקֹּתַי תֵּלֵכוּ, "if you walk in my statutes," it says אֵין אִם אֶלָּא לְשׁוֹן תַּחֲנוּנִים, "'if' is nothing but a word of pleading," and it quotes this verse, with its לוּ, as the proof. The God who just gave his people up to themselves cannot get through another line without pleading with them.

What follows the wish shows how unusual the psalm's logic is. Deuteronomy always puts satiety before failure: "you will eat and be satisfied… lest your heart grow proud and you forget" (Deut 8:12–14). In Deuteronomy 31:20, "they will eat and be sated and grow fat, and turn to other gods." Psalm 81 puts the failure first and the feeding last: וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָּ, "he would feed him the fat of wheat, and with honey from the rock I would satisfy you." The words come from the Song of Moses (Deut 32:13–14), which goes on to wine and then to Jeshurun, who "grew fat and kicked." The psalm stops before either. Its rock is the rock of Meribah, named in verse 8, which once gave grudging water to a people quarrelling. Here the same rock gives honey to a people who have refused. The last verse also cannot keep its persons in order. It begins "he fed him" and ends "I would satisfy you." It is as if God, telling the old story about Israel in the third person, looked up and saw the crowd in front of him and spoke to them directly.

Two other works help mark what the psalm does here by doing the opposite. In Charles Ives's *The Unanswered Question*, a solo trumpet repeats the same phrase over soft, almost inaudible strings, and the flutes' attempts to answer grow more and more frantic, until the trumpet's final question gets no reply. Psalm 81 has that structure with the roles switched: the divine voice does the asking, and the human side is silent. Luke has Jesus weep over Jerusalem with the same contrary-to-fact grammar, "If you had only known on this day what would bring you peace," and then he closes the sentence: "but now it is hidden from your eyes." Psalm 81 never says "now." Its לוּ remains open, and its final verb is addressed to whoever is standing in the courtyard.

That is why the psalm has no response from the people and does not need one. In synagogue it closes the Thursday morning service, so that every week a congregation that has just finished its own singing hears אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָּ, "I would satisfy you," and goes home with the offer still unanswered. The festival was never the point. Its purpose was to let the people hear that the one they were shouting to had been talking to them all along, and that his offer still stands.

#### Full psalm

Psalm 81 is said every Thursday as the Song of the Day. The Mishnah (Tamid 7:4) lists it as the psalm the Levites sang in the Temple on the fifth day of the week: בַּחֲמִישִׁי הָיוּ אוֹמְרִים, הַרְנִינוּ לֵאלֹקִים עוּזֵּנוּ, "on the fifth day they would say: Sing out to God our strength." It closes weekday Shacharit in every rite: Nusach Ashkenaz, Nusach Sefard (the Hasidic rite), Chabad, and Edot HaMizrach. The Talmud's explanation (Rosh Hashanah 31a) is that on the fifth day God created the birds and the fish, which sing his praise. The explanation has nothing to do with what the psalm says, and that is worth knowing about how the daily psalms were assigned. The Sefard and Edot HaMizrach siddurim also include the psalm in their Rosh Chodesh order, since v. 4 names the new moon. In the Edot HaMizrach machzor the whole psalm is said before Arvit on the eve of Rosh Hashanah, after the piyyut *Achot Ketannah*, and again, under the rubric יֵשׁ נוֹהֲגִים לוֹמַר, "some are accustomed to say," in the Mincha of Rosh Hashanah.

#### Key verses

Verses 4–5 are among the defining verses of Rosh Hashanah. In the Ashkenaz and Sefard rites both verses come among the Writings verses of the Shofarot section of the Musaf Amidah; the Edot HaMizrach rite quotes v. 4 there and leaves out v. 5. In all three the sequence reads: בַּחֲצוֹצְרוֹת וְקוֹל שׁוֹפָר הָרִיעוּ לִפְנֵי הַמֶּלֶךְ ה׳, וְנֶאֱמַר, תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר, "with trumpets and the sound of the shofar shout before the King, the LORD; and it is said: Blow the shofar at the new moon." Psalm 81 never uses the word "king." The liturgy places it right after Psalm 98:6, which does, and so the psalm's shofar, which in the psalm summons a voice, is heard at an enthronement. The same two verses are said in Ashkenaz and Sefard Ma'ariv on the eve of Rosh Hashanah, in the daytime Kiddush in the Sefard siddur (where the pilgrim festivals say אֵלֶּה מוֹעֲדֵי, "these are the appointed times"), and in the Edot HaMizrach verses said before the shofar is sounded.

In Zikhronot, v. 5 is not quoted as a prooftext; it is worked into the prose of the blessing: זֶה הַיּוֹם תְּחִלַּת מַעֲשֶׂיךָ… כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, וְעַל הַמְּדִינוֹת בּוֹ יֵאָמֵר אֵי זוֹ לַחֶרֶב וְאֵי זוֹ לַשָּׁלוֹם, "This day is the beginning of Your works… for it is a statute for Israel, a judgment of the God of Jacob; and concerning the nations it is pronounced on it, which for the sword and which for peace." The plain sense of מִשְׁפָּט is "ordinance." The blessing turns it into "judgment," a verdict on the whole world.

Verse 11 is said every day in *Hodu* in Pesukei Dezimra, in every rite. It follows directly on קוּמָה עֶזְרָתָה לָּנוּ וּפְדֵנוּ לְמַעַן חַסְדֶּךָ, "Arise, help us, and redeem us for Your kindness' sake" (Ps 44:27). Placed there, God's אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ… הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "I am the LORD your God… open your mouth wide and I will fill it," becomes his answer to the people's plea. This is a new use of the line. In the psalm the invitation is the one Israel refused; in *Hodu* it is a promise being made to people who are praying.

Verse 4 also turns up in the Rosh Hashanah piyyutim and in the selichot of Erev Rosh Hashanah. In the Edot HaMizrach rite it affects practice: on the Shabbat before Rosh Hashanah the coming month is not announced, דִּכְתִיב: בַּכֶּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ, "as it is written: at the covered moon, for our feast-day." The new moon of Tishrei is kept hidden. Verse 9 is part of a string of "Hear" verses after the Shema in the Edot HaMizrach selichot of Yom Kippur night, and v. 2 is the song of the large clean beast in *Perek Shirah*.
