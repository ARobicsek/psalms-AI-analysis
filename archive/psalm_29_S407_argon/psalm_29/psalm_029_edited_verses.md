**Verse 1**

מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד; הָבוּ לַה׳ בְּנֵי אֵלִים, הָבוּ לַה׳ כָּבוֹד וָעֹז.

> A psalm of David. Ascribe to the LORD, O divine beings; ascribe to the LORD glory and strength.

The introduction traced how the prefix **לְ** ("to") on לַה׳, "to the LORD," marks God as the receiver of tribute in the opening summons before shifting to His people in verse 11. The verb that demands that tribute, הָבוּ (*hā-VŪ*), "ascribe" or "hand over," is not a gentle liturgical word. It comes from the defective root יהב, which survives in Biblical Hebrew only in the imperative. In prose it belongs to the rough speech of the marketplace and the street: Rachel demanding children from Jacob with הָבָה־לִּי בָנִים, "Give me children!" (Genesis 30:1), or the two daughters of the leech in Proverbs 30:15 crying הַב הַב, "Give! Give!" Psalm 68:35 issues the same call with the standard verb נתן: תְּנוּ עֹז לֵאלֹקִים, "Give strength to God." Our poet chose הָבוּ instead, both for its peremptory tone—a suzerain ordering vassals to hand over what is owed—and for the labial-back-vowel chime it sets running across the verse: `hā-**VŪ** lay-hō-**WĀH** ... kā-**VŌDH** **wā-ʿŌZ**`.

Who are the בְּנֵי אֵלִים (*bə-NĒY 'ē-LIYM*), "divine beings" or "sons of gods," ordered to surrender their power? In Canaanite texts from Ugarit, the exact phrase (*bn ilm*) names the pantheon, the divine council of the high god El. Because אֵלִים can also be read as the plural of אַיִל ("ram," written defectively without a *yod*) or as "mighty ones" (as in אֵילֵי מוֹאָב, "the leaders of Moab," Exodus 15:15), later readers worked hard to domesticate these gods. The Septuagint translator hedged his bets by translating the phrase twice in a single line: Ἐνέγκατε τῷ Κυρίῳ υἱοὶ Θεοῦ, ἐνέγκατε τῷ Κυρίῳ υἱοὺς κριῶν (*Enenkate tō Kyriō huioi Theou, enenkate tō Kyriō huious kriōn*), "Bring to the Lord, O sons of God; bring to the Lord young rams." Rashi, following the Talmud (b. Megillah 17b), reads בְּנֵי אֵלִים as "sons of the mighty of the land"—the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—and derives from verses 1 and 2 the opening three blessings of the daily Amidah prayer: בְּנֵי אֵלִים yields אָבוֹת ("Patriarchs"), כָּבוֹד וָעֹז yields גְּבוּרוֹת ("Mighty Deeds"), and verse 2’s כְּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ... בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ yields קְדֻשַּׁת הַשֵּׁם ("Holiness of the Name").

A sharper fight broke out among the medieval commentators over Ibn Ezra’s reading. Noting that Job 38:7 pairs the "sons of God" with the morning stars (בְּרָן־יַחַד כּוֹכְבֵי בֹקֶר וַיָּרִיעוּ כָּל־בְּנֵי אֱלֹקִים, "when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy"), Ibn Ezra identified בְּנֵי אֵלִים as הַכּוֹכָבִים, "the stars," explaining: כִּי כְּפִי מַעֲרַכְתָּם יֵרְדוּ הַגְּשָׁמִים בְּעֶזְרַת אֱלֹקֵי הָאֱלֹקִים, "for according to their astral configuration the rains descend, with the help of the God of gods." A century later in Provence, the Meiri described a similar astrological reading without naming Ibn Ezra and snapped: וְזֶה כִּכְפִירָה גְּמוּרָה, "and this is like complete heresy!" Yet Ibn Ezra’s point was polemical: even the stars have no power to alter their courses, and bow to the LORD like a slave to his master (כְּהִשְׁתַּחֲווֹת הָעֶבֶד לַאֲדוֹנָיו).

The Septuagint also preserves an ancient liturgical clue in the superscription, adding ἐξοδίου σκηνῆς (*exodiou skēnēs*), "of the going forth of the tabernacle"—built on ἐξόδιον (*exodion*), the Greek word in Leviticus 23:36 LXX for Shemini Atzeret, the eighth concluding day of Sukkot, when the autumn rain prayers begin.

**Verse 2**

הָבוּ לַה׳ כְּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ, הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ לַה׳ בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ.

> Ascribe to the LORD the glory of His name; bow down to the LORD in the splendor of holiness.

Three commands to "hand over" (הָבוּ) culminate in a fourth imperative that forces the heavenly court to its knees: הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ, "bow down" or "prostrate yourselves." What follows—בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ—is a three-way crux. In standard Hebrew, הֲדָרָה means "adornment" or "splendor," so BDB and many medieval commentators read "in holy attire" or "in the splendor of holiness." In 1950, Frank Moore Cross proposed reading הַדְרַת in light of Ugaritic *hdrt*, which stands parallel to "dream" or "vision" when the god El appears to King Kirta, yielding "at the holy appearance"—that is, when the LORD manifests Himself in the storm of verse 3. Meanwhile, the Septuagint renders the phrase ἐν αὐλῇ ἁγίᾳ αὐτοῦ (*en aulē hagia autou*), "in His holy court," reading הַדְרַת as חַצְרַת ("court"). Notice that both the Septuagint’s "young rams" in verse 1 and its "holy court" here correspond to the single line that Psalm 96:8 inserts into its parallel version of our summons: שְׂאוּ־מִנְחָה וּבֹאוּ לְחַצְרוֹתָיו, "bring an offering and come into His courts." The Greek translator of Psalm 29 has pulled the heavenly scene toward the sacrificial temple procession of Psalm 96.

In the Talmud (b. Berakhot 30b), Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi turned the phrase by exchanging two letters and changing another: אַל תִּקְרֵי בְּהַדְרַת אֶלָּא בְּחֶרְדַּת, "Do not read בְּהַדְרַת, 'in the splendor of' holiness, but בְּחֶרְדַּת, 'in the trembling of' holiness." That homiletical swap is less arbitrary than it looks. Both Psalm 96:9 (הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ לַה׳ בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ, חִילוּ מִפָּנָיו כָּל־הָאָרֶץ, "Bow down to the LORD in the splendor of holiness; tremble before Him, all the earth") and our own psalm—which echoes the consonants of קֹדֶשׁ, "holiness," in verse 8’s מִדְבַּר קָדֵשׁ, "wilderness of Kadesh," right beside the verb יָחִיל, "convulses"—place holiness next to trembling.

How much the opening of Psalm 29 depends on *who* is kneeling becomes clear in Shaul Tchernichovsky’s 1899 Hebrew poem לְנֹכַח פֶּסֶל אַפּוֹלוֹ, "Before the Statue of Apollo." Where the psalmist commandeered a pagan hymn and ordered the old gods to bow to the LORD of the desert storm, Tchernichovsky staged the rebellion of modern secular Hebrew poetry by walking up to the marble statue of the Greek sun-god and reversing the prostration:

> בָּאתִי עָדֶיךָ – הַאִם הִכַּרְתָּנִי?

> הִנְנִי הַיְּהוּדִי: רִיב לָנוּ לְעוֹלָמִים! ...

> וָאָבֹא אֵלֶיךָ. בָּאתִי עָדֶיךָ.

> מוּל פִּסְלָךְ אֶקֹּדָה. פִּסְלְךָ – סֵמֶל הַמָּאוֹר בַּחַיִּים;

> אֶקֹּד, אֶכְרָעָה לַטּוֹב וְלַנַּעֲלֶה,

> לַאֲשֶׁר הוּא נִשָּׂא בִּמְלֹא כָּל הָעוֹלָם,

> לַאֲשֶׁר הוּא נֶהְדָּר בִּמְלֹא כָּל הַבְּרִיאָה.

>

> I have come to you—do you recognize me?

> Here I am, the Jew: our quarrel is eternal! ...

> And I came to you. I have come to you.

> Before your statue I will bow. Your statue is the symbol of light in life;

> I will bow, I will kneel to the good and the sublime,

> to that which is exalted through all the world,

> to that which is splendid (*neh-DĀR*, from הדר) through all creation.

Even in rebellion against Psalm 29, Tchernichovsky cannot escape its vocabulary: the word he chooses for Apollo’s radiance across creation is נֶהְדָּר (*neh-DĀR*), the root of הַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ.

**Verse 3**

קוֹל ה׳ עַל־הַמָּיִם; קֵל־הַכָּבוֹד הִרְעִים, ה׳ עַל־מַיִם רַבִּים.

> Hark, the voice of the LORD is over the waters; the God of glory thunders, the LORD, over the mighty waters.

The first two words, קוֹל ה׳, "The voice of the LORD," have no verb. In biblical poetry, קוֹל at the head of a verbless clause often works as an interjection—"Hark! Listen!"—as in Song of Songs 2:8, קוֹל דּוֹדִי הִנֵּה־זֶה בָּא, "Hark! My beloved, here he comes," or Isaiah 13:4, קוֹל הָמוֹן בֶּהָרִים, "Hark, a tumult on the mountains!" Before the storm is a sentence, it is a sound that makes you look up. Only in the middle clause of the three-line verse does a finite verb arrive—הִרְעִים, "thunders"—flanked on either side by the waters: first the definite עַל־הַמָּיִם, "over the waters," and then the expanded עַל־מַיִם רַבִּים, "over the mighty waters."

Notice the title given to the thunderer in that middle clause: קֵל־הַכָּבוֹד, "the God (*El*) of glory." In verses 1 and 2, the "sons of *El*" (בְּנֵי אֵלִים) were commanded to hand over כָּבוֹד, "glory." Now the poem reveals why the demand could not be refused: the One thundering over the sea already *is* קֵל־הַכָּבוֹד, the true El to whom glory belongs by nature. That same title solved a liturgical puzzle for the rabbis. Because the weekday Amidah originally had eighteen blessings corresponding to the eighteen occurrences of ה׳ in this psalm, the addition of a nineteenth blessing (against sectarians, instituted at Yavneh) seemed to break the correspondence—until Rabbi Levi pointed to our clause in the Babylonian Talmud (b. Berakhot 28b–29a): כְּנֶגֶד קֵל הַכָּבוֹד הִרְעִים, "corresponding to 'the God of glory thunders.'"

Long before our psalm was composed, Canaanite scribes on the Phoenician coast were already treating the storm-god’s thunder-voice as a portable title of sovereignty. In the 1340s BCE, Abimilki, vassal king of Tyre—sitting on the Mediterranean shore right below the mountains of Lebanon—wrote to Pharaoh Akhenaten in Amarna Letter EA 147, flattering his Egyptian overlord as one "who gives forth his voice in the sky like Haddu [Baal], so that the whole land trembles at his voice." And in the physical geography of the Levant, the Mediterranean is indeed where the thunder begins. A 2011 meteorological study by Shalev, Saaroni, and colleagues mapping lightning across Israel and the surrounding region found that forty percent of annual lightning flashes occur in October alone, with the highest density concentrated over the warm Mediterranean waters before the storm fronts move ashore:

> The spatial distribution of lightning flash density indicates the highest concentration over the Mediterranean Sea, attributed to the contribution of moisture as well as sensible and latent heat fluxes from the sea surface. Other centers of high density appear along the coastal plain, orographic barriers, especially in northern Israel, and downwind from the metropolitan area of Tel Aviv, Israel.

Because the Voice makes its first appearance "over the waters," the Babylonian Talmud (b. Pesachim 112a) prescribes verses 3–9 as a remedy for someone thirsty on Tuesday or Friday night, when drinking water was thought to risk an evil spirit: וְאִם צָחֵי מַאי תַּקַּנְתֵּיהּ? נֵימָא שִׁבְעָה קוֹלוֹת שֶׁאָמַר דָּוִד עַל הַמָּיִם, וַהֲדַר נִישְׁתֵּי, "And if he is thirsty, what is his remedy? Let him recite the seven voices that David said over the water, and afterward he may drink."

**Verse 4**

קוֹל־ה׳ בַּכֹּחַ, קוֹל ה׳ בֶּהָדָר.

> The voice of the LORD is in power; the voice of the LORD is in majesty.

After the opening thunderclap over the sea, the poem pauses for a two-beat verbless couplet that describes the Voice by its qualities before showing what it breaks. When Psalm 68:34 describes the divine thunder, it calls it קוֹל עֹז, "a voice of strength." Why does our poet avoid עֹז here and write בַּכֹּחַ, "in power," and בֶּהָדָר, "in majesty"? Because, as we saw in the introduction, עֹז is reserved for the outer frame of the poem—the tribute taken from the gods in verse 1 and given to the people in verse 11—while בֶּהָדָר (*be-hā-DHĀR*), "in majesty," echoes verse 2’s בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ (*bə-hadh-rath-QŌ-dhesh*): the worshippers were told to clothe themselves in what the Voice already is.

The first noun, בַּכֹּחַ (`ba-kōaḥ`), carries the definite article inside the preposition **בְּ** (literally "in *the* power"), instead of a possessive pronoun ("in *His* power"). That grammatical detail opened the door to a midrashic reading of the revelation at Sinai, cited by Rashi here and developed by Rabbi Jonathan Sacks in his commentary on Exodus and Deuteronomy. Why did the verse say "in the power" rather than "in His power"? Because had God spoken in His own full power, no human ear could have survived it; instead, at Sinai He modulated His voice לְפִי כֹּחָן שֶׁל יִשְׂרָאֵל, "according to the capacity of Israel"—speaking to the elderly, the young, and the nursing infants each according to the receiver’s own strength. Radak offers a charmingly physical variation on the same worry, reading verses 3 and 4 as a sequence of acoustic dampening: כְּדֵי שֶׁלֹּא יָמוּתוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל מִפְּנֵי הַקּוֹל הַחָזָק הַיּוֹצֵא, הֶעֱבִירוֹ בַּמַּיִם תְּחִלָּה לְהַתִּישׁ כֹּחוֹ שָׁם, וְאַחַר כָּךְ בָּא לְיִשְׂרָאֵל בְּהָדָר שֶׁיּוּכְלוּ לְסָבְלוֹ, "So that Israel would not die from the mighty voice going forth, He passed it over the waters first to exhaust its force (כֹּחוֹ) there, and afterward it came to Israel in majesty (בְּהָדָר) so that they could bear it."

In Nusach Ashkenaz, Nusach Sefard, and Edot HaMizrach, the Friday night recitation of Psalm 29 is followed immediately by the ancient seven-line mystical prayer אָנָּא בְּכֹחַ, *Ana Bekhoah* (attributed to the sage Rabbi Nehunya ben HaKanah), whose opening line catches the word בַּכֹּחַ straight from our verse:

> אָנָּא בְּכֹחַ גְּדֻלַּת יְמִינְךָ תַּתִּיר צְרוּרָה.

>

> We beg You, with the power (בְּכֹחַ) of Your great right hand, untie the bound.

Placed directly after Psalm 29, its seven lines answer the psalm’s seven קוֹלוֹת, "voices," asking that the same כֹּחַ, "power," that shatters cedars in the next verse be used to loosen knots gently.

**Verses 5–6**

קוֹל ה׳ שֹׁבֵר אֲרָזִים, וַיְשַׁבֵּר ה׳ אֶת־אַרְזֵי הַלְּבָנוֹן.

> The voice of the LORD breaks cedars; the LORD shatters the cedars of Lebanon.

וַיַּרְקִידֵם כְּמוֹ־עֵגֶל, לְבָנוֹן וְשִׂרְיֹן כְּמוֹ בֶן־רְאֵמִים.

> He makes them skip like a calf, Lebanon and Sirion like a young wild ox.

In verse 5, the actor shifts in mid-sentence. The first half-line says that "the voice of the LORD breaks" (שֹׁבֵר, a present participle) generic cedars; the second half-line drops "the voice" and says that "the LORD Himself shatters" (וַיְשַׁבֵּר, switching to an intensive form) the famed cedars of Lebanon. The Voice is not a separate messenger or intermediary; when it strikes, it is the LORD acting in person.

Verse 6 then presents a grammatical puzzle that modern Northwest Semitic philology has addressed. In the Masoretic vocalization, וַיַּרְקִידֵם, "He makes them skip," carries a third-person plural suffix (**־ֵם**, "them"), which seems to refer back to the cedars of verse 5, leaving לְבָנוֹן וְשִׂרְיֹן, "Lebanon and Sirion," dangling awkward and unattached at the seam of the two half-lines. Older nineteenth-century critics proposed deleting "Lebanon and Sirion" as a scribal gloss. In 1936, H. L. Ginsberg—followed by Frank Moore Cross—argued that the final *mem* on וַיַּרְקִידֵם may be an archaic Canaanite enclitic *mem*: a rhythmic particle attached to the verb that carries no pronoun meaning at all. Read on that understanding, "Lebanon" becomes the direct object of the first half-line and "Sirion" of the second: "He makes Lebanon skip like a calf, / Sirion like a young wild ox." Even so, the Masoretic pointing lets the listener see both images at once, as Rashi notes: אֶת הָאֲרָזִים וְאֶת הֶהָרִים, "both the cedars and the mountains" dance together.

The Septuagint turns verse 6 into a cabinet of curiosities: Καὶ λεπτυνεῖ αὐτὰς ὡς τὸν μόσχον τὸν Λίβανον, καὶ ὁ ἠγαπημένος ὡς υἱὸς μονοκερώτων (*Kai leptynei autas hōs ton moschon ton Libanon, kai ho ēgapēmenos hōs huios monokerōtōn*), "And He will pulverize them like the calf of Lebanon, and the Beloved like a son of unicorns." Three separate transformations seem to have happened here. First, the translator appears to have read דקק ("crush, pulverize") for רקד ("skip"), turning a comic dance into dust. Second, he rendered שִׂרְיֹן, "Sirion," as ὁ ἠγαπημένος (*ho ēgapēmenos*), "the Beloved"—the standard Greek rendering of יְשֻׁרוּן, "Jeshurun" (poetic Israel, as in Deuteronomy 32:15), perhaps by transposing the letters of שרין and ישרון. Third, he translated רְאֵמִים, the wild ox or aurochs (*Bos primigenius*, Akkadian *rīmu*, frequently depicted in Mesopotamian reliefs in profile so that only a single horn is visible), as μονοκερώτων (*monokerōtōn*), "one-horned"—the Greek word that passed through the Latin Vulgate (*unicornis*) into the King James Bible and populated Western art with unicorns.

Why does the poet make the shattering of mountain peaks a proof of royal order rather than cosmic collapse? Compare a Chinese court poem from roughly the eighth century BCE in the *Book of Odes* (*Shijing*, Mao 193, "At the Junction of the Tenth Month"), which watches a thunderstorm tear into the mountains and draws the opposite political conclusion:

> Grandly flashes the lightning of the thunder;—

> There is a want of rest, a want of good.

> The streams all bubble up and overflow.

> The crags on the hill-tops fall down.

> High banks become valleys;

> Deep valleys become hills.

> Alas for the men of this time!

> How does the king not stop these things?

For the Zhou dynasty poet, when lightning shatters hilltops and makes mountains trade places with valleys, it is a terrifying sign that the human king has lost the Mandate of Heaven and cannot hold nature still. In Psalm 29, the human king is absent and the LORD Himself is the King (v. 10), so the very same upheaval—mountains skipping like calves and wild oxen—is not a failure of government, but the exuberance of creation jumping at its Master’s voice.

**Verse 7**

קוֹל־ה׳ חֹצֵב לַהֲבוֹת אֵשׁ.

> The voice of the LORD hews out flames of fire.

Every other verse of the psalm has two or three lines; verse 7 stands alone as a four-word monocolon (a single poetic line). Nineteenth-century scholars suspected that a parallel half-line had fallen out in transmission, but the acoustic shape of the verse argues that nothing is missing. In a poem that imitates a thunderstorm, a three-beat clause cut off without a partner is the prosodic equivalent of a lightning bolt. Even its final word, אֵשׁ (`'ĒSH`), "fire," rhymes directly with the final word of verse 8, קָדֵשׁ (`qā-**DHĒSH**`), binding the isolated flash into the desert convulsion that follows.

The introduction noted how חֹצֵב, "hews," borrows the verb of royal stone-quarrying and applies it to fire. In the sensory world of the poem, that produces a striking synesthesia—a mixing of the senses: a *voice* (sound) *carves* (touch and stonecraft) *flames of fire* (light). That fusion of speech, stone-carving, and fire is what led the sages of the Mekhilta (cited here by Rashi) to hear in this four-word line the engraving of the Ten Commandments at Mount Sinai: שֶׁהָיָה הַדִּבּוּר שֶׁל עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדִּבְּרוֹת יוֹצֵא בְּלַהֲבוֹת אֵשׁ מִפִּיו וְנֶחְקָק עַל הַלּוּחוֹת כְּתַבְנִיתָם, "for the utterance of the Decalogue emanated from His mouth in flames of fire and was engraved upon the stone tablets according to their form." In eleventh-century Spain, Solomon Ibn Gabirol took the same phrase in his philosophical poem כֶּתֶר מַלְכוּת, *Keter Malkhut*, to describe the angels themselves as חֲצוּבֵי לֶהָבוֹת, "beings hewn out of flames."

In visual art, Giorgione’s painting *La Tempesta* (*The Tempest*, c. 1505–1508) in the Gallerie dell'Accademia in Venice hauntingly captures the strange poise of verses 7 through 11—a lightning bolt overhead paired with childbirth and unbothered calm below. In the background, a jagged bolt of lightning splits a dark teal sky above a bridge and the white walls of a town; in the foreground, directly beneath the storm, a woman sits on the grass calmly nursing a newborn infant while a young man stands watching with a staff. Art historians have argued for centuries over what narrative Giorgione meant to illustrate, because no Greek or Roman myth holds those pieces together. Psalm 29 brings those pieces together: the Voice hews flames of fire in verse 7, brings forth new birth in verse 9, and shelters His people in quiet peace in verse 11.

**Verse 8**

קוֹל ה׳ יָחִיל מִדְבָּר, יָחִיל ה׳ מִדְבַּר קָדֵשׁ.

> The voice of the LORD convulses the wilderness; the LORD convulses the wilderness of Kadesh.

Having shattered the cedar forests of the far north in verses 5–6, the storm jumps to the opposite ecological and geographical pole: the arid wilderness of Kadesh in the south (known in the Torah’s wilderness wanderings as Kadesh-barnea, Numbers 13:26; 20:1, though some scholars of the Canaanite substratum hear an older reference to Kadesh-on-the-Orontes in Syria). Together, the northern snow-peaks of Lebanon/Hermon and the southern desert of Kadesh form a merism—a pair of extremes standing for the whole length of the land.

At the same time, the poet closes a subtle consonantal ring with verse 2. In verse 2, the divine beings were commanded to bow down בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ, "in the splendor of holiness" (spelled קדש); here the LORD convulses מִדְבַּר קָדֵשׁ, "the wilderness of Kadesh" (also spelled קדש). And in Hebrew ear-play, מִדְבָּר, "wilderness," shares its three root letters (דבר) with "speech," a resonance that helps later readers hear Sinai in the phrase. Rashi, quoting the Talmud (b. Shabbat 89a) on the five names of the Sinai desert, explains our phrase by saying: מִדְבַּר קָדֵשׁ שֶׁנִּתְקַדְּשׁוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל עָלָיו, "It was called the wilderness of Kadesh because Israel was sanctified (נִתְקַדְּשׁוּ) upon it."

The verb repeated twice in the staircase—יָחִיל (*yā-ḤIYL*), "convulses" or "makes writhe as in childbirth"—prepares the birthing image of the next verse. Joining a tree-smashing thunderstorm to the impregnation and labor of the parched earth is one of the oldest poetic instincts in the ancient world. We hear it in Mesopotamian *eršemšahunga* laments to the storm-gods Enlil and Adad ("because of his voice people are deathly still, meadowlands are broken up, steppes come into labor"), and we hear it in India in *Rig Veda* 5.83 (c. 1500–1000 BCE), the Vedic hymn to the storm-god Parjanya (translated by Ralph T. H. Griffith):

> Sing with these songs thy welcome to the Mighty, with adoration praise and call Parjanya.

> The Bull, loud roaring, swift to send his bounty, lays in the plants the seed for germination.

> He smites the trees apart, he slays the demons: all life fears him who wields the mighty weapon.

> From him exceeding strong flees e’en the guiltless, when thundering Parjanya smites the wicked.

Like Psalm 29, the Vedic hymn opens with an imperative call to praise the roaring storm-god, watches him smash the trees, and sees his terror implant new life in the barren ground. The difference is how the two hymns end: the Vedic poet has to beg Parjanya at the close of the hymn to stop raining lest he drown the world ("Thou hast poured down the rain-flood: now withhold it"), whereas Psalm 29 ends with a King who already sits enthroned above the Flood.

**Verse 9**

קוֹל ה׳ יְחוֹלֵל אַיָּלוֹת, וַיֶּחֱשֹׂף יְעָרוֹת; וּבְהֵיכָלוֹ, כֻּלּוֹ אֹמֵר כָּבוֹד.

> The voice of the LORD causes hinds to calve and strips forests bare; while in His temple, all of it cries: Glory!

The introduction defended the Masoretic reading יְחוֹלֵל אַיָּלוֹת, "causes hinds to calve," against Lowth’s emendation to "oaks." Notice how the verb form itself carries the intensification from verse 8: where verse 8 used יָחִיל (*yā-ḤIYL*), "makes writhe," for the desert’s contractions, verse 9 switches to the reduplicated form יְחוֹלֵל (*yə-ḥō-LĒL*), the same form Job 39:1 uses when God asks Job whether he presides over the birth-pangs of wild deer: הֲיָדַעְתָּ עֵת לֶדֶת יַעֲלֵי־סָלַע, חֹלֵל אַיָּלוֹת תִּשְׁמֹר? "Do you know the season when the mountain goats give birth? Do you watch the calving of the hinds?" Ecology knows a real-world counterpart to this pairing of destructive storm-fire (v. 7) and sudden birth: *serotiny*. In forests of lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*), the female cones are sealed shut with a hard resin that ordinary weather cannot open; only when a forest fire sweeps through and heats the cones to roughly 45–50 °C does the resin melt, releasing the seeds onto the ash-cleared floor. What looks like the stripping of the forest is the trigger of its reproduction.

The second clause, וַיֶּחֱשֹׂף יְעָרוֹת, "and strips forests bare," is equally precise. Modern forestry studies of lightning strikes on coniferous trees—such as Alan R. Taylor’s field research for the USDA Forest Service—document how a bolt superheats the moisture under the bark, ejecting slabs of wood and peeling the bark away in long spiral strips that leave the pale sapwood exposed, the exact image of Rashi’s gloss here from Genesis 30:37 (מַחְשֹׂף הַלָּבָן, "exposing the white" of peeled branches). Here "forests" takes the rare feminine plural יְעָרוֹת instead of the usual masculine יְעָרִים (the related feminine singular construct יַעְרַת can mean "honeycomb," as in 1 Samuel 14:27, בְּיַעְרַת הַדְּבָשׁ, "in the honeycomb"). And because חָשַׂף can also mean "to uncover, reveal" (Isaiah 52:10, חָשַׂף ה׳ אֶת־זְרוֹעַ קָדְשׁוֹ, "the LORD has bared His holy arm"), the Septuagint translated the clause καὶ ἀποκαλύψει δρυμούς (*kai apokalypsei drymous*), "and He will uncover/reveal the thickets." St. Augustine seized on that Greek verb to read the stripped forests as the dark, tangled thickets of the ancient Scriptures laid open by the voice of the Gospel.

Then comes the cut to the sanctuary: וּבְהֵיכָלוֹ, כֻּלּוֹ אֹמֵר כָּבוֹד, "while in His temple, all of it cries: Glory!" In the Masoretic cantillation, the major pause (the *atnach*) falls on וּבְהֵיכָלוֹ, "while in His temple," setting the final three words apart as their own hushed colon. And the pronoun on כֻּלּוֹ ("all of *it*," singular masculine, rather than כֻּלָּם, "all of *them*") agrees grammatically with הֵיכָלוֹ, "His temple": it is not just the choir inside the building, but the building itself—every wall, beam, and court—that answers the sevenfold thunder with a single word. (In b. Zevachim 116a, Rabbi Elazar HaModa'i takes "in His temple" to refer to the pagan kings in their respective palaces, trembling and singing "Glory!")

In electronics, there is a circuit invented by Otto H. Schmitt in 1934 called a *Schmitt trigger*: it takes a noisy, violently fluctuating analog wave and, the instant the voltage crosses a set threshold, snaps the output into a single, flat, unwavering digital signal. Verse 9c is the Schmitt trigger of Psalm 29. Across the preceding clauses of splintering wood, leaping rock, fire, and labor pains, the input has been pure acoustic turbulence; the instant the poem crosses the threshold of the הֵיכָל, all that noise snaps into a single steady word: כָּבוֹד, "Glory!"

Emily Dickinson’s storm poem *There came a Wind like a Bugle* (Fr1618 / J1593, c. 1883) follows the same three-step trajectory as verses 5 through 10—from panting trees, to the wild sound inside a sanctuary steeple, to a final astonished permanence—except that in Dickinson’s New England, no king appears on the throne at the end, only the surviving earth itself:

> There came a Wind like a Bugle—

> It quivered through the Grass

> And a Green Chill upon the Heat

> So ominous did pass

> We barred the Windows and the Doors

> As from an Emerald Ghost—

> The Doom's electric Moccasin

> That very instant passed—

> On a strange Mob of panting Trees

> And Fences fled away

> And Rivers where the Houses ran

> Those looked that lived—that Day—

> The Bell within the steeple wild

> The flying tidings told—

> How much can come

> And much can go,

> And yet abide the World!

**Verse 10**

ה׳ לַמַּבּוּל יָשָׁב, וַיֵּשֶׁב ה׳ מֶלֶךְ לְעוֹלָם.

> The LORD sat enthroned at the Flood; the LORD took His seat as king forever.

The two verbs of sitting form a tight grammatical hinge around the name of the LORD: first the completed past tense יָשָׁב (*yā-SHĀV*), "sat enthroned" at the primordial Deluge, and then the narrative tense וַיֵּשֶׁב (*way-YĒ-shev*), "and took His seat—and sits still—as king forever." What does the preposition **לְ** in לַמַּבּוּל mean? It can be temporal ("at the Flood" or "since the Flood"), locative ("over the Flood," with the celestial ocean as His throne-base), or purposeful ("for the Flood," sitting as a judge to sentence the world). Frank Moore Cross argued from Ugaritic text KTU 1.101—which describes Baal sitting (*yṯb*) like the flood/ocean (*mdb*)—that מַבּוּל here meant a conquered "Flood-dragon"; David Tsumura countered in 1988 that Hebrew מַבּוּל is never a personified monster, whether in Genesis 6–11 or here, but the cosmic deluge itself.

The Aramaic Targum refuses to choose between judgment and mercy, splitting the verse’s two verbs of sitting into two different thrones during Noah’s Flood: יְיָ בְּדַר טוּפָנָא יְתִיב עַל כּוּרְסֵי דְדִינָא לְמִתְפְּרַע מִנְהוֹן, וִיתֵיב יְיָ עַל כּוּרְסֵי רַחֲמוּתָא וְשֵׁזֵיב יַת נֹחַ וּמְלַךְ עַל בְּנוֹי לְעַלְמֵי עָלְמִין, "The LORD in the generation of the Flood sat upon the throne of judgment to exact punishment from them; and the LORD sat upon the throne of mercy and saved Noah, and reigned over his sons forever and ever."

In sixteenth-century Safed, Moshe Alshich (*Romemot El*) asked a sharper question about verse 10: why prove that the LORD is "king forever" by pointing to the Flood, the one moment in history when almost all of humanity was wiped out? Proverbs 14:28 says of an earthly ruler, בְּרָב־עָם הַדְרַת־מֶלֶךְ, וּבְאֶפֶס לְאֹם מְחִתַּת רָזוֹן, "In a multitude of people is a king’s glory (הַדְרַת, the same word as v. 2), but in the lack of a nation is a prince’s ruin." A human king without subjects ceases to be a king. Not so the LORD, says Alshich: כִּי הֲלֹא ה׳ לַמַּבּוּל יָשָׁב שֶׁלֹּא נִשְׁאַר כִּי אִם אַךְ נֹחַ, וְעִם כָּל זֶה וַיֵּשֶׁב ה׳ מֶלֶךְ לְעוֹלָם, כִּי אֵין מַלְכוּתוֹ תְּלוּיָה בְּרִבּוּי עַם כְּמֶלֶךְ בָּשָׂר וָדָם, "For the LORD sat enthroned at the Flood when none remained save Noah alone, and even so 'the LORD sat as king forever,' because His kingship does not depend on a multitude of people like a king of flesh and blood."

When the Book of Revelation (4:6) envisions the heavenly throne room—in a book that also echoes our psalm’s "seven thunders" (Revelation 10:3–4)—it takes the turbulent waters of verse 3 and the Flood of verse 10 and freezes them beneath the throne into absolute stillness: "And before the throne there was a sea of glass like unto crystal."

**Verse 11**

ה׳ עֹז לְעַמּוֹ יִתֵּן, ה׳ יְבָרֵךְ אֶת־עַמּוֹ בַשָּׁלוֹם.

> The LORD will give strength to His people; the LORD will bless His people with peace.

Where verse 1 used הָבוּ (from יהב, which has no future or imperfect forms in Hebrew) to command the gods to "give" strength upward, verse 11 switches to the finite verb יִתֵּן (from נתן) for the LORD’s giving downward. As Ibn Ezra notes—תְּפִלָּה אוֹ בְּרוּחַ הַקֹּדֶשׁ, "either a prayer or a prophetic promise"—the Hebrew verb forms יִתֵּן and יְבָרֵךְ can be read either as a jussive wish ("May the LORD give... may the LORD bless") or as an indicative assurance ("The LORD will give... the LORD will bless"). Both clauses place the subject ה׳ ahead of the verb—and the first clause fronts the object עֹז, "strength," as well—a word order that in classical Hebrew tilts strongly toward a confident promise (as the Septuagint’s future tenses δώσει, *dōsei*, "He will give," and εὐλογήσει, *eulogēsei*, "He will bless," also take it).

Why must the gift of עֹז, "strength," in the first half of the verse be followed immediately by שָׁלוֹם, "peace," in the second? In *Romemot El*, Alshich offers a piercing political and psychological answer. When a nation is poor and weak, its people are humble and do not quarrel with one another; when they are given great עֹז—power and prosperity—pride sets in and they turn their strength against each other in civil strife, which is what destroyed the Temple and sent Israel into exile. Therefore, Alshich writes, כַּאֲשֶׁר ה׳ עוֹז לְעַמּוֹ יִתֵּן, ה׳ יְבָרֵךְ אֶת עַמּוֹ בַשָּׁלוֹם, שֶׁלֹּא יִתְגָּאוּ עַל יְדֵי הָעוֹז לִבְעוֹט וְלָרִיב אֵלּוּ עִם אֵלּוּ, "when the LORD gives strength to His people, the LORD will bless His people with peace, so that they do not grow haughty through that strength to kick and quarrel with one another."

Closing a thunder-poem with a quiet benediction of peace has a modern counterpart. In 1922, T. S. Eliot titled the fifth and final movement of *The Waste Land* "What the Thunder Said," drawing on a parable in the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* (5.2.1–3, c. seventh–sixth century BCE). In the Upanishad, three classes of beings—gods, humans, and demons—ask their father Prajapati for instruction. To each group the thunder speaks only a single Sanskrit syllable, *Da*, which each group hears according to its own nature and flaw (a striking Indian cousin to the Sinai midrash on verse 4’s בַּכֹּחַ): the unruly gods hear *Damyata* ("Be self-controlled"), the greedy humans hear *Datta* ("Give"), and the cruel demons hear *Dayadhvam* ("Be compassionate"). Eliot’s poem begins that final section in a parched desert waiting for rain under distant mountain thunder:

> After the torchlight red on sweaty faces

> After the frosty silence in the gardens

> After the agony in stony places

> The shouting and the crying

> Prison and palace and reverberation

> Of thunder of spring over distant mountains

> He who was living is now dead

> We who were living are now dying

> With a little patience

> Here is no water but only rock

> Rock and no water and the sandy road

> The road winding above among the mountains

> Which are mountains of rock without water

And when the thunder has spoken its three *Da*’s, Eliot ends the entire poem by repeating the Upanishadic closing formula three times: *Shantih shantih shantih*—"the Peace which passeth understanding." Yet notice the difference between the two thunder-endings. In the Upanishad and in *The Waste Land*, the thunder delivers a moral command—control yourself, give, sympathize—and peace is what awaits if the listener obeys. In Psalm 29, the human listeners have not been given a command at all; the commands in verses 1 and 2 were addressed only to the heavenly beings. By the time the poem reaches human ears in verse 11, all the giving is done by God, and בַשָּׁלוֹם, "with peace," is not a task left for the exhausted listener to achieve, but the blessing in which they are sent home.
