### INTRODUCTION ESSAY

In 1601, the German composer Hans Leo Hassler published a secular love song about a young man’s bewildered heart. Fifty-five years later, the Lutheran poet Paul Gerhardt set sacred words to Hassler’s tune—the hymn known in English as "O Sacred Head, Now Wounded"—and Johann Sebastian Bach later made that borrowed melody the choral spine of his *St. Matthew Passion*. Music historians call this a *contrafactum*: you keep the music and its emotional force, but you change the one to whom it is sung. In 1936, the Semitist H. L. Ginsberg argued that Psalm 29 is an ancient Hebrew *contrafactum* of a Canaanite or Phoenician hymn to the storm-god Baal. Its northern landscape of Lebanon and Sirion—which Deuteronomy 3:9 notes is the Sidonian name for Mount Hermon: צִידֹנִים יִקְרְאוּ לְחֶרְמוֹן שִׂרְיֹן, "the Sidonians call Hermon Sirion"—and its archaic staircase rhythms belonged to the storm-god’s repertoire, now redirected to יְהוָה, "the LORD."

Yet Psalm 29 does something more daring than paste a new divine name over an old pagan song: it stages the transfer of power inside the poem itself. The psalm opens in the heavenly court by commanding the old pantheon—בְּנֵי אֵלִים, "O divine beings"—to hand over their glory and strength and bow down (vv. 1–2). It then follows a sevenfold thunder-voice from the Mediterranean Sea across the northern mountains and down into the southern desert, before cutting in mid-verse to a temple choir (vv. 3–9). It closes with the LORD seated as king over the Flood, bestowing strength and peace upon His people on earth (vv. 10–11). Across those eleven verses, the name יְהוָה, "the LORD," sounds eighteen times: four times in the two opening verses of summons, ten times in the central storm, and four times in the two closing verses of enthronement and blessing. That frame turns the poem into a transformer. It takes raw, terrifying cosmic power at the top, passes it through a thunderstorm and a sanctuary liturgy, and delivers it at the bottom as human peace.

### A Storm Stripped to a Voice

To see how the poem reshapes the storm-god myth without dulling its force, look first at what it leaves out. In the Louvre stands a Bronze Age limestone stele from Ugarit on the Syrian coast. It shows Baal striding forward above wavy lines of sea and mountain, a mace raised in his right hand to strike his foes and a spear sprouting lightning in his left. Psalm 29 keeps the sea at the start of the storm, עַל־הַמָּיִם, "over the waters" (v. 3), and the mountains at its center, לְבָנוֹן וְשִׂרְיֹן, "Lebanon and Sirion" (v. 6). But it edits out the stride, the battle, and every weapon in the god’s hands.

Stranger still, this famous storm-psalm removes almost all the physical weather of a storm. As the scholar Gérard Nissim Amzallag has pointed out, a poem long read as a hymn to the bringer of autumn rains never mentions rain, whether גֶּשֶׁם or מָטָר. Look closer, and the rest of the storm’s material vocabulary is missing too. There are no clouds (עָנָן or עָב), no wind (רוּחַ), no hail (בָּרָד), and not even the common Hebrew and Canaanite word for lightning (בָּרָק). Elsewhere, when Psalm 18:14 uses the same thunder-verb as our psalm, it surrounds the sound with hailstones and coals: וַיַּרְעֵם בַּשָּׁמַיִם יְהֹוָה, וְעֶלְיוֹן יִתֵּן קֹלוֹ, בָּרָד וְגַחֲלֵי־אֵשׁ, "Then the LORD thundered in the heavens, and the Most High gave forth His voice, hail and coals of fire." An Ugaritic hymn to the enthroned storm-god (KTU 1.101) likewise equips him with "seven lightnings." Psalm 29 replaces those seven bolts with a sevenfold קוֹל יְהֹוָה, "The voice of the LORD." Of the storm’s physical elements, the poet keeps only water and fire—and even the fire in verse 7 is something the Voice carves out. The visual storm has been distilled into pure sound.

Turning the tempest into a voice changes the theology of the scene before a single tree is struck. A god with a mace needs an enemy to hit, which is why ancient Near Eastern storm myths center on a battle against the unruly Sea. A voice needs only a listener. When the theophany opens in verse 3—קוֹל יְהֹוָה עַל־הַמָּיִם, אֵל־הַכָּבוֹד הִרְעִים, יְהֹוָה עַל־מַיִם רַבִּים, "The voice of the LORD is over the waters; the God of glory thunders, the LORD, over the mighty waters"—the mighty waters do not rear up or fight back. There is no combat and no corpse. Modern meteorological studies of the Eastern Mediterranean, such as Shalev and colleagues’ 2011 mapping of regional lightning, confirm how closely this opening matches a Levantine autumn storm. Storm systems known as Cyprus Lows peak in October—just when the Septuagint’s heading, ἐξοδίου σκηνῆς (*exodiou skēnēs*), "of the going forth of the tabernacle," assigns our psalm to the closing day of Sukkot—and generate their densest lightning over the warm Mediterranean waters before driving inland against the northern mountains. In Psalm 29, the cosmic sea is no rival monster. It is simply the dark sounding-board over which the Voice rolls in from the west.

### The Unbuilt Palace and the Answer of the Choir

When that Voice hits land in verses 5 through 7, its verbs and objects dismantle the prestige of earthly kings and rival gods alike. The poet uses staircase parallelism, a rhythm in which a clause starts an action, pauses to name the LORD, and repeats the verb with a sharper geographical focus. At the same time, the line crackles with hard *b*’s, *sh*’s, and trilled *r*’s: קוֹל יְהֹוָה שֹׁבֵר אֲרָזִים, וַיְשַׁבֵּר יְהֹוָה אֶת־אַרְזֵי הַלְּבָנוֹן (*QŌL yə-hō-WĀH shō-VĒR 'a-rā-ZIYM, way-shab-BĒR yə-hō-WĀH 'eth-'ar-ZĒY hal-lə-vā-NŌN*), "the voice of the LORD breaks cedars; the LORD shatters the cedars of Lebanon" (v. 5). Two verses later, in a lone three-beat line that flashes between longer couplets, the Voice "hews out flames of fire": קוֹל־יְהֹוָה חֹצֵב לַהֲבוֹת אֵשׁ (v. 7). Why pair the shattering of Lebanon’s cedars with חֹצֵב (*ḥō-TSĒV*), "hews"? In its other four biblical appearances, that exact participial form always describes construction workers hewing stone or rock-vats—above all Solomon’s eighty thousand חֹצֵב בָּהָר, "hewers in the mountain" (1 Kings 5:29; 2 Chronicles 2:1, 17), who worked alongside the cutters of Lebanon’s cedars to build the Jerusalem Temple (1 Kings 5:20; Ezra 3:7).

The poet has chosen the vocabulary of royal temple-building in order to run it in reverse. Both Near Eastern monarchs and Canaanite gods proved their rule by importing cedars from Lebanon and Sirion and quarrying stone to erect a royal house, a הֵיכָל (*hēy-khāl*), from which the king could speak. Here the LORD does not harvest Lebanon’s cedars to build His house; His voice splinters them. He does not quarry mountain stone; His voice quarries fire out of the sky. And at the center of the storm in verse 6—the single verse between verses 3 and 9 where קוֹל יְהֹוָה, "the voice of the LORD," drops out—terror tips into comedy: וַיַּרְקִידֵם כְּמוֹ־עֵגֶל, לְבָנוֹן וְשִׂרְיֹן כְּמוֹ בֶן־רְאֵמִים, "He makes Lebanon skip like a calf, Sirion, like a young wild ox." Ten-thousand-foot snow-capped ranges, along with the bull-calf and wild ox that were the storm-god’s prized animal emblems, caper like young livestock. Even the sound of בֶן־רְאֵמִים (*ven-rə-'ē-MIYM*), "young wild ox," mocks the opening בְּנֵי אֵלִים (*bə-NĒY 'ē-LIYM*), "sons of gods." It is a demotion by ear that helps explain why the Septuagint translator heard "rams" in verse 1, rendering the phrase twice in a famous doublet: first as υἱοὶ Θεοῦ (*huioi Theou*), "sons of God," and then as υἱοὺς κριῶν (*huious kriōn*), "sons of rams."

As the storm sweeps south into the wilderness of Kadesh in verses 8 and 9, its force turns from shattering to labor pains. The verb repeated in verse 8, יָחִיל (*yā-ḤIYL*), "convulses" or "makes writhe," intensifies in verse 9a into יְחוֹלֵל אַיָּלוֹת (*yə-ḥō-LĒL 'ay-yā-LŌTH*), "causes hinds to calve," before the Voice "strips forests bare" (וַיֶּחֱשֹׂף יְעָרוֹת). In 1753, Bishop Robert Lowth, finding pregnant deer out of place beside stripped forests, proposed repointing אַיָּלוֹת, "hinds," to אֵילוֹת, "oaks," an emendation adopted by several modern translations ("twists the oaks"). Yet Lowth smoothed away the line’s best paradox. The Book of Job uses the same verb and noun for deer in labor: חֹלֵל אַיָּלוֹת תִּשְׁמֹר, "Do you watch the calving of the hinds?" (Job 39:1). Mesopotamian storm-hymns to Enlil and Adad likewise say that at the god’s voice "meadowlands are broken up, steppes come into labor." Within the Hebrew Bible itself, Jeremiah 22:23 binds Lebanon’s cedars directly to the pangs of childbirth: יֹשַׁבְתְּ בַּלְּבָנוֹן, מְקֻנַּנְתְּ בָּאֲרָזִים, מַה־נֵּחַנְתְּ בְּבֹא־לָךְ חֲבָלִים, חִיל כַּיֹּלֵדָה, "You who dwell in Lebanon, nested among the cedars, how you will groan when pangs come upon you, writhing like a woman in labor!" As Ibn Ezra and Radak observe, the thunderclap startles the pregnant hind and contracts her womb to bring forth life. The storm both strips the world bare and forces it into delivery.

Then, in the last five words of verse 9, the poem executes an abrupt cut from the ravaged woods to the inside of a sanctuary: וּבְהֵיכָלוֹ כֻּלּוֹ אֹמֵר כָּבוֹד, "while in His temple all say 'Glory!'" Listen to what the sevenfold קוֹל יְהֹוָה, "voice of the LORD," has *not* done across verses 3 through 9: it has spoken not a single word. All its predicates have been physical impacts—thundering, breaking, skipping, hewing, convulsing, calving, stripping. The only verb of speech in the psalm, אֹמֵר ("says"), and the only quoted word, כָּבוֹד ("Glory!"), belong not to the thunder, but to the choir inside His palace. There, the sevenfold קוֹל (*qōl*), "voice," is answered by a single כֻּלּוֹ (*kul-lō*), "all of it."

That one word of recognition settles the cosmos onto its throne. In verse 10, the Voice vanishes and the LORD sits down: יְהֹוָה לַמַּבּוּל יָשָׁב, וַיֵּשֶׁב יְהֹוָה מֶלֶךְ לְעוֹלָם, "The LORD sat enthroned at the Flood; the LORD sits enthroned, king forever." Compare how Homer’s *Iliad* (Book 20, lines 56–64) stages a cosmic storm when the gods go to war:

> Dreadfully thundered the father of men and gods
> from on high; and from beneath Poseidon shook
> the boundless earth and the steep crests of the mountains.
> All the roots of many-fountained Ida were shaken,
> and her peaks, and the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans.
> And Aidoneus, lord of the dead below, was seized with terror,
> and in terror leapt from his throne and shouted aloud, lest above him
> Poseidon the earth-shaker should split the earth wide open,
> and his house be laid bare to mortals and immortals.

In Homer, the thunder-quake jolts a god off his throne—ἐκ θρόνου ἆλτο (*ek thronou alto*), "leapt from his throne"—and threatens to lay bare his house, because sky, sea, and underworld belong to rival powers. In Psalm 29, the mountains leap, the forests are laid bare, and the one God takes His seat. He sits enthroned not merely over the Mediterranean surf of verse 3, but over the מַבּוּל (*mab-BŪL*), a noun that occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible outside Noah’s Deluge in Genesis 6–11.

### Where the Strength Goes

Why, then, did the psalm begin by ordering the divine beings to "give" strength to a God who already rules the Deluge, and what becomes of that strength at the end? In the 1930s, the physicist Basil Schonland used a high-speed camera to photograph lightning over South Africa. He showed that what the eye takes for a single downward blow is a two-way circuit: a faint leader steps down from the cloud, and the brilliant flash races upward from the ground as a return stroke. Psalm 29 works as a circuit of the same kind, and its grammar tracks the current through the single-letter prefix **לְ** ("to").

In the opening summons of verses 1 and 2, all four occurrences of the divine name carry that prefix: הָבוּ **לַ**יהֹוָה בְּנֵי אֵלִים, הָבוּ **לַ**יהֹוָה כָּבוֹד וָעֹז. הָבוּ **לַ**יהֹוָה כְּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ, הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ **לַ**יהֹוָה בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ, "Ascribe **to** the LORD, O divine beings, ascribe **to** the LORD glory and strength. Ascribe **to** the LORD the glory of His name; bow down **to** the LORD, majestic in holiness." The repeated verb הָבוּ (*hā-VŪ*), "ascribe" or "hand over," comes from a defective root (יהב) that survives in Hebrew only in the imperative and commonly demands what is owed, as when Joseph’s starving Egyptians cry הָבָה־לָּנוּ לֶחֶם, "Give us bread!" (Genesis 47:15). Once the lesser gods have surrendered their כָּבוֹד, "glory," and עֹז, "strength," in verses 1 and 2, the name יְהֹוָה never takes a preposition again. Across all fourteen remaining occurrences in verses 3 through 11, the LORD is solely the actor or the owner of the Voice, never the receiver. Where does the **לְ** ("to") of the receiver go? It returns in the final verse, attached for the first time to human beings: יְהֹוָה עֹז **לְ**עַמּוֹ יִתֵּן, יְהֹוָה יְבָרֵךְ אֶת־עַמּוֹ בַשָּׁלוֹם, "May the LORD grant strength **to** His people; may the LORD bestow on His people wellbeing" (v. 11).

The strength surrendered upward by the gods in verse 1 is given downward by God to His people in verse 11—and the poem’s syntax quietly shows who has been bypassed along the way. Elsewhere in biblical poetry, when the LORD thunders from heaven, He channels that עֹז, "strength," to the human king, as at the climax of Hannah’s song in 1 Samuel 2:10: עָלָו בַּשָּׁמַיִם יַרְעֵם, יְהֹוָה יָדִין אַפְסֵי־אָרֶץ, וְיִתֶּן־עֹז לְמַלְכּוֹ, "Against them He thunders in the heavens; the LORD judges the ends of the earth, and He gives strength to His king." Psalm 29 carries the heading מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד, "A psalm of David," yet neither David nor any earthly monarch appears in it. The title מֶלֶךְ, "king," is kept for the LORD alone in verse 10, and the strength goes straight to עַמּוֹ, "His people." Even the grammatical marker אֶת, which flags a definite direct object and is rare in archaic verse, pairs what the storm shatters with what the King blesses. Across the entire psalm, אֶת marks only two nouns: וַיְשַׁבֵּר יְהֹוָה אֶת־אַרְזֵי הַלְּבָנוֹן, "the LORD shatters the cedars of Lebanon" (v. 5), and יְבָרֵךְ אֶת־עַמּוֹ בַשָּׁלוֹם, "may He bestow on His people wellbeing" (v. 11). Both verbs stand in the intensive conjugation, and their consonants rearrange shattering, וַיְשַׁבֵּר (*way-shab-BĒR*), into blessing, יְבָרֵךְ (*yə-vā-RĒKH*). Rashi caught the political edge of that pairing when he glossed "breaks the cedars" in verse 5 with a single word: הָאֻמּוֹת, "the nations."

Because the sevenfold Voice at the center of the psalm never speaks a word, later biblical and rabbinic readers kept returning to listen for what was inside the thunder. When the poet of Psalm 96 borrowed our opening summons for a new temple song (Psalm 96:7–9), he replaced the heavenly בְּנֵי אֵלִים, "divine beings," with מִשְׁפְּחוֹת עַמִּים, "families of peoples," and handed the storm’s own verbs over to the worshippers, calling on all the earth to "writhe" before Him (חִילוּ מִפָּנָיו כָּל־הָאָרֶץ, 96:9) and the sea itself to "thunder" in praise (יִרְעַם הַיָּם וּמְלֹאוֹ, 96:11). The sages of the Talmud went further. Hearing a wordless sevenfold קוֹל that ends with a gift of עֹז, "strength," to Israel, they recognized the thunder of Mount Sinai. In the Babylonian Talmud (Zevachim 116a, cited by Rashi on v. 10), when the Torah was given and God’s voice echoed from one end of the world to the other, the kings of the nations trembled in their palaces and gathered around Balaam to ask: מָה קוֹל הֶהָמוֹן אֲשֶׁר שָׁמַעְנוּ? שֶׁמָּא מַבּוּל בָּא לָעוֹלָם? "What is this tumultuous sound that we have heard? Perhaps a flood is coming to the world?" Balaam answered them with verse 10—God already swore after Noah’s Flood never to bring another deluge, an oath that in Genesis 9:15 uses the only other appearance in the Bible of the exact consonantal form למבול: וְלֹא־יִהְיֶה עוֹד הַמַּיִם לְמַבּוּל, "and the waters shall never again become a flood." What you hear, Balaam told the kings, is verse 11: חֶמְדָּה טוֹבָה יֵשׁ לוֹ בְּבֵית גְּנָזָיו ... וּבִיקֵּשׁ לִיתְּנָהּ לְבָנָיו, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ה׳ עוֹז לְעַמּוֹ יִתֵּן. מִיָּד פָּתְחוּ כּוּלָּם וְאָמְרוּ: ה׳ יְבָרֵךְ אֶת עַמּוֹ בַשָּׁלוֹם, "He has a precious treasure in His storehouse... and He seeks to give it to His children, as it is stated: 'The LORD will give strength to His people.' Immediately, they all opened their mouths and said: 'The LORD will bless His people with peace.'"

That rabbinic ear—hearing the wordless storm-voice become the words of the Torah, and the eighteen divine names become the eighteen blessings of the daily Amidah prayer that opens by praising God’s might and closes by praying for peace (b. Berakhot 28b; b. Megillah 17b–18a)—explains what happens every Shabbat when the Torah scroll is carried back to the Ark to the singing of Psalm 29. Both the Ashkenazi and Edot HaMizrach prayerbook rubrics call the Ark the הֵיכָל (*hēy-khāl*): כְּשֶׁמַּחֲזִירִין הַסֵּפֶר תּוֹרָה לַהֵיכָל אוֹמְרִים, "When returning the Torah scroll to the *hekhal*, they say..." As the scroll reaches the open doors of the Ark, the congregation arrives at verse 9c—וּבְהֵיכָלוֹ כֻּלּוֹ אֹמֵר כָּבוֹד, "while in His *hekhal* all say 'Glory!'"—and then closes the doors on verse 11’s בַשָּׁלוֹם, "with peace." It is the same verse Rabbi Shimon ben Halafta chose as the final sentence of the entire Mishnah (Uktzin 3:12): לֹא מָצָא הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא כְּלִי מַחֲזִיק בְּרָכָה לְיִשְׂרָאֵל אֶלָּא הַשָּׁלוֹם, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ה׳ עֹז לְעַמּוֹ יִתֵּן ה׳ יְבָרֵךְ אֶת עַמּוֹ בַשָּׁלוֹם, "The Holy One, Blessed be He, found no vessel that could hold blessing for Israel save peace, as it is written: 'The LORD will give strength to His people; the LORD will bless His people with peace.'" Raw strength, even divine strength, would shatter the hands that tried to hold it if it were not poured into the vessel of peace; the thunder was only the sound of the gift on its way down.
