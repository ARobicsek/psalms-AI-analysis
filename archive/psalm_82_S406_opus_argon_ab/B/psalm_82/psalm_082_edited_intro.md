In the fourth century BCE, King Xuan of Qi asked the philosopher Mencius a dangerous question. Was it true that the founder of the Zhou dynasty had killed his sovereign, and if so, may a minister put a king to death? Mencius answered by dismantling the premise:

> He who outrages the benevolence proper to his nature, is called a robber; he who outrages righteousness, is called a ruffian. The robber and ruffian we call a mere fellow. I have heard of the cutting off of the fellow Zhou, but I have not heard of the putting a sovereign to death, in his case.

The king was not killed; the title died before the blow fell. What Mencius did to an earthly monarch, Psalm 82 does to heaven itself. It stages a trial in which "godhood" is treated not as an immortal nature a being possesses by birth, but as a judicial office held strictly on condition of rescuing the poor. When the gods fail that office, their divinity is revoked, and they are sentenced to die like ordinary men.

The poem accomplishes this in eight compact verses that move with the speed of a coup. It opens with a narrator's stage direction: God stands inside the assembly of the high god El, surrounded by fellow deities, ready to pronounce judgment (verse 1). Without so much as an "And He said," God's voice erupts into the room. He demands to know how long these rulers will judge crookedly and favor the wicked, then issues four rapid-fire commands to defend the orphan, the destitute, and the needy (verses 2–4). At the poem's center, the speaker abruptly stops addressing the court in the second person and turns away to diagnose them in the third. They know nothing, understand nothing, and walk about in darkness while the foundations of the earth totter beneath them (verse 5). Then God turns back to the defendants to deliver the sentence: He had once declared them all gods and sons of the Most High, yet they will die like mortals and fall like any prince (verses 6–7). Finally, a human voice from earth breaks into the heavenly courtroom. It begs the one remaining God to arise, judge the earth, and claim all the nations as His own inheritance (verse 8).

Listen to the first seven words after the heading, and you can hear the whole drama compressed into a single grammatical riddle. The psalm begins: אֱלֹקִים, נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת־קֵל; בְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹקִים, יִשְׁפֹּט, "God stands in the divine assembly; among the divine beings He pronounces judgment" (verse 1). In Hebrew, the prosecutor and the accused wear the identical, uninflected noun: אֱלֹקִים (*'e-lō-HIYM*). A listener hearing the line aloud cannot tell from the noun itself which אֱלֹקִים is the one Judge and which is the crowd of gods on trial. The distinction hangs on the masculine singular participle נִצָּב, "stands," and the singular verb יִשְׁפֹּט, "He pronounces judgment." The verse forces the ear to hold two meanings of the same word in the same breath. Then the rest of the psalm resolves the ambiguity by sentencing the plural אֱלֹקִים to death, so that by verse 8 only the singular אֱלֹקִים remains.

### A Cosmic Trial Under Municipal Law

For two thousand years, readers of Psalm 82 have divided into two camps over who is standing in the dock. Classical rabbinic commentators—Targum, Rashi, Radak, Meiri, and Malbim—along with Martin Luther and John Calvin, insist that the אֱלֹקִים on trial in verse 1 are human judges in Israel. They bear the title because they administer divine law, as in Exodus 22:8: אֲשֶׁר יַרְשִׁיעֻן אֱלֹקִים, "whom the judges condemn." Modern critical scholars argue just as forcefully that the defendants are the patron deities of the foreign nations. They point to Canaanite tablets from Ugarit and to the Dead Sea Scroll *11QMelchizedek*, which casts the heavenly Melchizedek as the judge condemning Belial and his spirits.

Each camp is reading half the poem faithfully, because the poet built Psalm 82 by splicing two famous biblical texts into two separate zones of the psalm. The outer frame and the death sentence (verses 1 and 6–8) draw their vocabulary from the Song of Moses in Deuteronomy 32:8: בְּהַנְחֵל עֶלְיוֹן גּוֹיִם, בְּהַפְרִידוֹ בְּנֵי אָדָם, יַצֵּב גְּבֻלֹת עַמִּים, "When the Most High gave nations their homes and set the divisions of man, He fixed the boundaries of peoples" (a verse that in a Qumran manuscript ends "according to the number of the sons of God," and in the Septuagint, "according to the number of the angels of God"). Five signature terms from that cosmic allotment sit exclusively in verses 1 and 6–8: עֶלְיוֹן ("Most High"), the root נחל ("inherit/allot), גּוֹיִם (nations), אָדָם (man"), and the collateral stems נצב and יצב ("stand/fix"). Not one of them enters the middle of the poem.

Step inside the indictment of verses 2–4, however, and the vocabulary of cosmic myth vanishes. In its place stands Leviticus 19:15, the Torah's everyday statute for human magistrates in a town court: לֹא־תַעֲשׂוּ עָוֶל בַּמִּשְׁפָּט, לֹא־תִשָּׂא פְנֵי־דָל, וְלֹא תֶהְדַּר פְּנֵי גָדוֹל; בְּצֶדֶק תִּשְׁפֹּט עֲמִיתֶךָ, "You shall not render an unfair decision: do not favor the poor or show deference to the rich; judge your kinsman fairly." Four of our psalm's core expressions are lifted straight from that single verse of civil law: עָוֶל ("injustice"), the idiom נשא פנים ("lift the face," meaning to show partiality), דַּל ("the wretched"), and the paired roots שפט ("judge") and צדק ("righteousness/vindicate").

The ancient Near East knew this seam between divine sonship and courtroom duty well. In the thirteenth-century BCE Ugaritic *Epic of Kirta*, when the ailing King Kirta lies near death, his son weeps over the paradox of a dying god-king:

> How can you, O father, die like a mortal? … How can it be said that Kirtu is the Son of El? Progeny of the Gentle and Holy One? Gods, after all, do they die?

Yet a few columns later, Kirta's heir Yassib marches into his father's presence and demands that his father abdicate. His charge is that the king has failed to judge the case of the widow, adjudicate the cause of the wretched, drive out those who prey on the poor, and feed the orphan. Psalm 82 takes that royal logic and turns it upon the heavenly council itself. By indicting the "sons of the Most High" with the municipal statute of Leviticus 19:15, the psalmist collapses the distance between heaven and the local courthouse. Whether you sit on a cloud over Babylon or on a magistrate's bench in Jerusalem, there is only one law, and it measures your right to rule by what happens to the poor.

Look closely at how the psalmist edits Leviticus 19:15, and the poem's edge gets sharper still. Leviticus was a law of strict neutrality: a judge must neither favor the poor (לֹא־תִשָּׂא פְנֵי־דָל) nor defer to the rich. Psalm 82 cuts the warning against favoring the poor clean out of the statute. When the wicked already hold the poor in their fist—מִיַּד רְשָׁעִים, "from the hand of the wicked" (verse 4)—neutrality is complicity. The gods have taken the warm relational idiom of "lifting someone's face" and directed it toward the predators: וּפְנֵי רְשָׁעִים תִּשְׂאוּ־סֶלָה, "showing favor to the wicked? Selah" (verse 2). To answer that corruption, the poet strips out Leviticus's abstract nouns—בַּמִּשְׁפָּט ("in judgment") and בְּצֶדֶק ("in righteousness")—and replaces them with four urgent imperatives: שִׁפְטוּ־דַל וְיָתוֹם, עָנִי וָרָשׁ הַצְדִּיקוּ. פַּלְּטוּ־דַל וְאֶבְיוֹן, מִיַּד רְשָׁעִים הַצִּילוּ, "Judge the wretched and the orphan, vindicate the lowly and the poor, rescue the wretched and the needy; save them from the hand of the wicked" (verses 3–4).

In fact, across all eight verses of this courtroom psalm, there is not a single Hebrew noun for "justice" or "righteousness"—no מִשְׁפָּט, no צֶדֶק, no צְדָקָה. The only abstract moral noun in the entire poem is עָוֶל, "injustice" (verse 2), the crooked thing corrupt judging leaves behind. Justice in Psalm 82 is never an ideal to be contemplated or a badge of office to be worn. It exists only as verbs, and as the bodies of the poor enclosed inside those verbs. Even the sound of the Hebrew warns how easily a corrupt court blurs victim and perpetrator. In verse 3, the destitute man, רָשׁ (*wā-RĀSH*), is wedged directly between his oppressors in verses 2 and 4, רְשָׁעִים (*rə-shā-ʿIYM*), separated from the word for "wicked" by a single guttural consonant (**ע**). And in verse 4, the last of the five poor-terms, אֶבְיוֹן (*'ev-YŌN*, "the needy"), is a near-rhyme with the highest title in verse 6, עֶלְיוֹן (*ʿel-YŌN*, "the Most High"). Rulers who will not hear the *'evyōn* have no business calling themselves sons of *ʿelyōn*.

### When the Judge Speaks the Sufferer's Lament

If the gods are on trial, why do they never speak a word in their own defense? Instead of giving the defendants a voice, the poet gives their Judge the voice of their victims.

God's opening words in verse 2—עַד־מָתַי, "How long?"—are a shock to anyone familiar with the Psalter. Everywhere else in the Psalms, "How long?" is the cry of bruised human beings looking up at a God who seems to be doing nothing, as in Asaph's own Psalm 74:10: עַד־מָתַי אֱלֹקִים יְחָרֵף צָר, "How long, O God, will the foe blaspheme?" Here, the supreme Judge steps into the assembly and speaks the human lament Himself. Even the four plural commands of verses 3–4 (שִׁפְטוּ, הַצְדִּיקוּ, פַּלְּטוּ, הַצִּילוּ), while cast in the language of charges to human agents of justice, culminate in the verbs of rescue that desperate petitioners address to God, as in Psalm 71:4: אֱלֹקַי, פַּלְּטֵנִי מִיַּד רָשָׁע, "My God, rescue me from the hand of the wicked." God is reading the gods their mail. Every prayer that rose from an exploited peasant or an orphan cheated of his field was a duty delegated to their bench, and they let it drop.

Then comes the chill of verse 5. Without warning, the speaker stops saying "you" and switches to "they: לֹא יָדְעוּ וְלֹא יָבִינוּ, בַּחֲשֵׁכָה יִתְהַלָּכוּ; יִמּוֹטוּ, כׇּל־מוֹסְדֵי אָרֶץ, They neither know nor understand, they go about in darkness; all the foundations of the earth totter." In a courtroom, you stop addressing the defendants in the second person when you realize they are beyond reach. You turn to the gallery and speak about them as a clinical fact. In Act 3 of Shakespeare's *King Lear*, it takes a literal storm tearing the roof off the world to force an old king to see what his office was for:

> Poor naked wretches, wheresoe’er you are,

> That bide the pelting of this pitiless storm,

> How shall your houseless heads and unfed sides,

> Your looped and windowed raggedness defend you

> From seasons such as these? O, I have ta’en

> Too little care of this. Take physic, pomp.

> Expose thyself to feel what wretches feel,

> That thou may’st shake the superflux to them

> And show the heavens more just.

Lear learns the mandate of verses 3–4 only after he has been cast out into the tempest of verse 5. The rulers of Psalm 82 never learn it at all. Because the architecture of the cosmos rests on justice, their moral blindness sets off a structural earthquake: יִמּוֹטוּ כׇּל־מוֹסְדֵי אָרֶץ, "all the foundations of the earth totter."

When God turns back to face them in verses 6–7, He borrows one more human idiom. It is the formula of a speaker confessing a mistaken assumption, as in Psalm 31:23: וַאֲנִי אָמַרְתִּי בְחׇפְזִי ... אָכֵן, "I had said in my alarm ... but in fact." Here God says to the council: אֲנִי־אָמַרְתִּי, אֱלֹקִים אַתֶּם, וּבְנֵי עֶלְיוֹן כֻּלְּכֶם. אָכֵן, כְּאָדָם תְּמוּתוּן, וּכְאַחַד הַשָּׂרִים תִּפֹּלוּ, "I had taken you for divine beings, sons of the Most High, all of you; but you shall die as men do, fall like any prince." Listen to the Hebrew verbs linking the crime in verse 5 to the sentence in verse 7. Because the earth's foundations totter, יִמּוֹטוּ (*yim-MŌ-tū*), the gods themselves will die, תְּמוּתוּן (*tə-mū-THŪN*). The root מוט ("totter") hardens by a single consonant—from **ט** to **ת**—into מות ("die"). Beings who destabilize the world forfeit their own permanence.

How bold that confidence is becomes clear beside Hayim Nahman Bialik's 1903–1904 poem *In the City of Slaughter* (*Be-ʿIr ha-Haregah*), written after the Kishinev pogrom. Bialik puts the psalm's own word for the afflicted, עָנִי, into a first-person speech of God, but with the courtroom roles shattered:

> סִלְחוּ לִי, עֲלוּבֵי עוֹלָם, אֱלֹקֵיכֶם עָנִי כְמוֹתְכֶם,

> עָנִי הוּא בְחַיֵּיכֶם וְקַל וָחֹמֶר בְּמוֹתְכֶם,

> כִּי תָבֹאוּ מָחָר עַל-שְׂכַרְכֶם וּדְפַקְתֶּם עַל-דְּלָתָי –

> אֶפְתְּחָה לָכֶם, בֹּאוּ וּרְאוּ: יָרַדְתִּי מִנְּכָסָי!

> וְצַר לִי עֲלֵיכֶם, בָּנַי, וְלִבִּי לִבִּי עֲלֵיכֶם:

> חַלְלֵיכֶם – חַלְלֵי חִנָּם, וְגַם-אֲנִי וְגַם-אַתֶּם

> לֹא-יָדַעְנוּ לָמָּה מַתֶּם וְעַל-מִי וְעַל-מָה מַתֶּם,

> וְאֵין טַעַם לְמוֹתְכֶם כְּמוֹ אֵין טַעַם לְחַיֵּיכֶם.

>

> Forgive, ye shamed of the earth, yours is a pauper-Lord!

> Poor was He during your life, and poorer still of late.

> When to my door you come to ask for your reward,

> I’ll open wide: See, I am fallen from My high estate.

> I grieve for you, my children. My heart is sad for you.

> Your dead were vainly dead; and neither I nor you

> Know why you died or wherefore, for whom, nor by what laws;

> Your deaths are without reason; your lives are without cause.

In Bialik's modern nightmare, there are no subordinate deities left to blame. When the poor are not rescued from the hand of the wicked, God Himself is bankrupted, sharing both the poverty of the עָנִי and the "not knowing" (לֹא־יָדַעְנוּ) of verse 5. Psalm 82 uses the myth of the council to draw the opposite conclusion: the failure of justice in the world is real, and it is intolerable, but it belongs to delegated powers whose lease has just been canceled.

### The Unfinished Verdict

Why, then, does the psalm not end at verse 7 with the crash of falling gods? Instead, a human voice interrupts the scene in verse 8: קוּמָה אֱלֹקִים, שׇׁפְטָה הָאָרֶץ; כִּי־אַתָּה תִנְחַל, בְּכׇל־הַגּוֹיִם, "Arise, O God, judge the earth, for all the nations are Your possession." Four imperatives to the gods in verses 3–4 are answered by two imperatives to God Himself (קוּמָה, שׇׁפְטָה). Meanwhile, the plural pronoun of verse 6—אֱלֹקִים אַתֶּם, "gods are you (*'at-TEM*)"—is replaced by the singular pronoun of verse 8: כִּי־אַתָּה, "for You alone (*'at-TĀH*)."

Yet a puzzle remains at the seam between the first verse and the last. If God has already been "standing" (נִצָּב) since verse 1, why must the worshiper beg Him to "arise" (קוּמָה) in verse 8? A passage in the Babylonian Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 7a) offers a striking key. Rabbi Samuel bar Naḥmani quotes Rabbi Jonathan: כָּל דַּיָּין שֶׁדָּן דִּין אֱמֶת לַאֲמִיתּוֹ, מַשְׁרֶה שְׁכִינָה בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: אֱלֹקִים נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת־קֵל, בְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹקִים יִשְׁפּוֹט. וְכָל דַּיָּין שֶׁאֵינוֹ דָּן דִּין אֱמֶת לַאֲמִיתּוֹ, גּוֹרֵם לַשְּׁכִינָה שֶׁתִּסְתַּלֵּק מִיִּשְׂרָאֵל, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: מִשֹּׁד עֲנִיִּים מֵאֶנְקַת אֶבְיוֹנִים, עַתָּה אָקוּם, יֹאמַר ה׳, "Any judge who judges a true judgment in its truth causes the Divine Presence to dwell in Israel, as it is said: 'God stands in the divine assembly; among the judges He pronounces judgment.' And any judge who does not judge a true judgment in its truth causes the Divine Presence to depart from Israel, as it is said: 'From the plunder of the afflicted, from the groan of the needy, now I will arise, says the LORD' (Psalm 12:6)." In the Talmud's reading, "standing" is God's presence inside a functioning court. "Arising" (אָקוּם) is God rising up to leave a corrupt bench and intervene directly on behalf of the plundered poor. Verse 8 asks God to do just that: to walk out of the failed council and take over the jurisdiction of the earth Himself.

In the Laws of Cricket (Law 31.1), there is a rule that captures the strange ending of Psalm 82: "Neither umpire shall give a Batter out, even though he/she may be out under the Laws, unless appealed to by a fielder." However blatantly the batter is out, the verdict does not take effect in the game until someone on the field cries out to the judge. In Psalm 82, the trial in heaven is over by verse 7. Every regime, court, or idol that claims exalted status while leaving the poor in the grip of the wicked is already a dead man walking, stripped of its title by the Judge. Yet the psalm ends in the middle of the appeal. Between the death sentence passed in heaven and the day the earth's foundations stop shaking stands the human voice of verse 8, refusing to accept the world as it is and shouting upward into the court: *Arise.*

#### Full psalm

Psalm 82 is the Song of the Day (*Shir shel Yom*) for Tuesday, recited in the morning service (*Shacharit*) across Nusach Ashkenaz, Nusach Sefard (the Hasidic rite), Chabad, and Edot HaMizrach. In the prayer book it is introduced by the formula recalling the Temple Levites: הַיּוֹם יוֹם שְׁלִישִׁי בַּשַּׁבָּת, שֶׁבּוֹ הָיוּ הַלְוִיִּם אוֹמְרִים בְּבֵית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ: מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף, אֱלֹקִים נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת־קֵל, בְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹקִים יִשְׁפֹּט, "Today is the third day of the week, on which the Levites used to say in the Temple: A psalm of Asaph. God stands in the divine assembly; among the gods He judges." The assignment dates back to Mishnah *Tamid* 7:4, and the Talmud (*Rosh Hashanah* 31a) explains why Tuesday received this psalm: עַל שֵׁם שֶׁגִּילָּה אֶרֶץ בְּחׇכְמָתוֹ, וְהֵכִין תֵּבֵל לַעֲדָתוֹ, "because on the third day of Creation He revealed the land in His wisdom and thereby prepared the world for His assembly." That explanation quietly domesticates the psalm's myth. The heavenly council (עֲדַת־קֵל, v. 1) becomes the human community (עֲדָתוֹ) settled on the newly uncovered dry land (הָאָרֶץ, v. 8). Within the Temple's weekly cycle, Tuesday's warning that injustice makes the earth's foundations totter (יִמּוֹטוּ כׇּל־מוֹסְדֵי אָרֶץ, v. 5) is answered on Friday by Psalm 93:1: אַף־תִּכּוֹן תֵּבֵל בַּל־תִּמּוֹט, "indeed, the world stands firm; it shall not totter." The whole psalm also appears as a daily study reading in *Seder Ma'amadot*, and is recited on Hoshana Rabbah among the additional psalms for the final sealing of judgment.

#### Key verses

The opening verse (v. 1) is recited in the *baraita* of the Levites' songs at the close of *Musaf* on Shabbat, festivals, and Rosh Hashanah in Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard. It also serves as a prooftext in *Pirkei Avot* 3:6 (recited in Shabbat study), where Rabbi Halafta of Kefar Hanania turns the divine tribunal into a quorum of ten human Torah students and a group of three Torah students: עֲשָׂרָה שֶׁיּוֹשְׁבִין וְעוֹסְקִין בַּתּוֹרָה, שְׁכִינָה שְׁרוּיָה בֵינֵיהֶם, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: אֱלֹקִים נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת־קֵל... וּמִנַּיִן אֲפִלּוּ שְׁלֹשָׁה, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: בְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹקִים יִשְׁפֹּט, "When ten sit and occupy themselves with Torah, the Shekhinah dwells among them, as it is said: 'God stands in the assembly of God'... And how do we know that the same is true even of three? As it is said: 'In the midst of the judges He judges.'"

In High Holiday *piyyutim* (liturgical poems), v. 1 is bent in both directions. In the Yom Kippur morning repetition in Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard, it describes God among the angels (קָדוֹשׁ הוּא נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת כְּרוּבִים, "holy is He who stands in the assembly of cherubim"), whereas at Yom Kippur afternoon in both rites (מֶלֶךְ נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת בְּרוּכִים, "the King who stands in the assembly of the blessed") and in Rosh Hashanah *Musaf* in Nusach Sefard (אֲשֶׁר בַּעֲדַת־קֵל בְּקִרְבִּי נִצָּב, "who stands within me in the assembly of God") the assembly is the praying congregation itself. Meanwhile, the Yom Kippur Eve hymn *Ki Hinei Ka-Chomer* takes v. 3's pair עָנִי וָרָשׁ, "the lowly and the poor," out of God's charge to corrupt rulers and makes it a title of God's own mercy: כֵּן אֲנַחְנוּ בְּיָדְךָ, תּוֹמֵךְ עָנִי וָרָשׁ, "so are we in Your hand, You who uphold the poor and destitute."

#### Practical Kabbalah

In the folk-magical manual *Shimush Tehillim* (as transmitted in Godfrey Selig's 1788 edition), reciting Psalm 82 is prescribed to succeed in a business transaction and to help an agent or clerk win favor with an employer. Using a fierce indictment of judicial bribery to secure commercial favor looks paradoxical, but the link lies in v. 2's idiom פְּנֵי ... תִּשְׂאוּ, "you lift the face." Outside a courtroom, "lifting a face" is the ordinary biblical phrase for a superior receiving a petitioner or envoy with favor—just as Jacob sends gifts ahead to Esau hoping אוּלַי יִשָּׂא פָנָי, "perhaps he will lift my face" (Genesis 32:21). Folk practice lifted the phrase out of its forensic condemnation and heard it as a charm for a boss's welcoming look.
