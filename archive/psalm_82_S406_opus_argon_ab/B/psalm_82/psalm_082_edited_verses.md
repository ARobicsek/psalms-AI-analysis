**Verse 1**

מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף: אֱלֹקִים, נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת־קֵל; בְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹקִים, יִשְׁפֹּט.

> A psalm of Asaph: God stands in the assembly of El; in the midst of gods He pronounces judgment.

The introduction noted how the verse turns on the masculine singular participle נִצָּב, "stands," which—together with the singular verb יִשְׁפֹּט, "He pronounces judgment"—tells the listener that the first אֱלֹקִים is one Judge and the second is a crowd of defendants. A sister poem about corrupt divine judges, Psalm 58, plays the exact same grammatical game in reverse. It closes with the declaration אַךְ יֵשׁ־אֱלֹקִים שֹׁפְטִים בָּאָרֶץ, "Indeed, there is a God who judges on earth" (Psalm 58:12), where אֱלֹקִים is paired with the *plural* participle שֹׁפְטִים ("judging") so that the ear hears the plural "gods" being absorbed into the one Judge of the earth. Here in Psalm 82, the singular participle נִצָּב also carries a sharp courtroom posture. In ancient Near Eastern council scenes, the presiding deity sits on a throne while the prosecutor or litigant stands to bring a charge—just as Isaiah pictures God rising to prosecute human leaders: נִצָּב לָרִיב ה׳, וְעֹמֵד לָדִין עַמִּים, "The LORD stands up to plead a cause; He rises to judge the peoples" (Isaiah 3:13).

Because Psalm 82 sits inside the Elohistic Psalter (Psalms 42–83, where editors routinely replaced the personal name ה׳ with אֱלֹקִים), the opening line likely once read "YHWH stands in the assembly of El"—the exact Canaanite council phrase found on the tablets of Ugarit (*ʿdt ilm*, "assembly of the gods"). Ancient translators handled that phrase in opposite ways. The Septuagint renders בַּעֲדַת־קֵל as ἐν συναγωγῇ θεῶν (*en synagōgē theōn*), "in the assembly of gods," replacing the singular name El with an explicit plural of gods. The Aramaic Targum moves in the other direction, reading קֵל in its lexical sense of "strength" and turning the heavenly pantheon into a rabbinic study hall: בִּכְנִישַׁת צַדִּיקִין דְּתַקִּיפִין בְּאוֹרַיְתָא, "in the congregation of the righteous who are strong in the Torah."

Among the medieval commentators, Ibn Ezra breaks ranks with Rashi and Radak. Before even mentioning human judges, he states plainly that בְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹקִים ("in the midst of gods") means the celestial beings: הֵם הַמַּלְאָכִים שֶׁהֵם הָאֱלֹקִים וּצְבָא הַשָּׁמַיִם בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹקִים, כִּי עַל יְדֵי הַמַּלְאָכִים הֵם כָּל מִשְׁפַּט אֱלֹקִים בָּאָרֶץ, "they are the angels, who are the *elohim* and the host of heaven, the sons of God, for through the angels are all the judgments of God on earth." Malbim, defending the human-judges reading, notices a second meaning in the noun קֶרֶב ("midst"), which in Hebrew also means the inward parts or heart. God does not merely stand in the courtroom among the judges; בְּקֶרֶב לֵב הַשּׁוֹפְטִים נִמְצָא דְּבַר אֱלֹקִים הַמַּזְהִיר אוֹתָם, "inside the heart of the judges is found the word of God warning them." In Malbim's reading, the unintroduced outcry that follows in verse 2 is the sound of the judges' own conscience shouting at them from within.

**Verse 2**

עַד־מָתַי תִּשְׁפְּטוּ־עָוֶל; וּפְנֵי רְשָׁעִים, תִּשְׂאוּ־סֶלָה.

> How long will you judge unjustly, and lift the faces of the wicked? Selah.

No speech verb ("And God said") introduces this question. The prosecutor simply speaks, and His first words indict the defendants by the standard of the Song of Moses. Where Deuteronomy 32:4 defined El as קֵל אֱמוּנָה וְאֵין עָוֶל, "a faithful God, never false," the members of El's assembly do the opposite: תִּשְׁפְּטוּ־עָוֶל, "you judge unjustly." That exact verb form, תִּשְׁפְּטוּ ("you judge"), occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible: here, in Psalm 58:2, and in King Jehoshaphat's charge to the human judges of Judah in 2 Chronicles 19:6–7. Listen to how closely Jehoshaphat's speech anticipates our psalm: רְאוּ מָה־אַתֶּם עֹשִׂים, כִּי לֹא לְאָדָם תִּשְׁפְּטוּ כִּי לַה׳, וְעִמָּכֶם בִּדְבַר מִשְׁפָּט. וְעַתָּה יְהִי פַחַד־ה׳ עֲלֵיכֶם, שִׁמְרוּ וַעֲשׂוּ, כִּי אֵין עִם־ה׳ אֱלֹקֵינוּ עַוְלָה וּמַשֹּׂא־פָנִים, "Consider what you are doing, for you judge not on behalf of man, but on behalf of the LORD, and He is with you in the matter of judgment. Now therefore let the fear of the LORD be upon you; be careful and act, for there is no injustice or favoritism with the LORD our God." Jehoshaphat's warning clusters four of our psalm's signature elements—תִּשְׁפְּטוּ, אָדָם (which will return with lethal irony in verse 7's כְּאָדָם תְּמוּתוּן, "like a man you shall die), עַוְלָה (injustice"), and מַשֹּׂא־פָנִים ("lifting of faces"). That shared cluster helps explain why Radak tentatively suggests that Psalm 82 was composed in the days of Jehoshaphat, Malbim dates it to his judicial reform, and Meiri cites Jehoshaphat's admonition as the parallel for the psalm's warning.

Why do these judges lift the faces of the wicked? Radak gives the plain socioeconomic answer: לְפִי שֶׁהֵם חֲזָקִים וּגְדוֹלִים וַעֲשִׁירִים, "because they are strong and great and rich." The sixteenth-century Safed commentator Moses Alshich (*Romemot El*) hears a subtler psychological evasion in the pairing of the two clauses. The judges do not tell themselves that the wicked man is innocent. Rather, they recognize the wrong (עָוֶל) and whisper to one another: פְּלוֹנִי עָשׁוּק כִּי עָוֶל עָשָׂה לוֹ רֵעֵהוּ כְּפִי הַדִּין, אַךְ מַה נַּעֲשֶׂה כִּי בַּעַל דִּינוֹ רָשָׁע וְאַלָּם הוּא וְלֹא נוּכַל לוֹ, "So-and-so is wronged, for his fellow has done him an injustice according to the law—but what can we do? His opponent is wicked and violent, and we cannot prevail against him." Institutional cowardice begins by diagnosing the injustice and ends by accommodating the bully.

At the end of the verse stands the psalm's only סֶלָה ("Selah"), bound directly to the verb תִּשְׂאוּ ("you lift") by a hyphen (*maqqef*): תִּשְׂאוּ־סֶלָה. Since סֶלָה is traditionally derived from the root סָלַל, "to lift up," the line closes with a sharp acoustic pun: they "lift" the faces of the wicked, and the music "lifts" into an abrupt pause. In the silence of that pause, the court waits to hear if the accused gods will answer "How long?" They say nothing, and God fills the silence with four commands.

**Verses 3–4**

שִׁפְטוּ־דַל וְיָתוֹם; עָנִי וָרָשׁ, הַצְדִּיקוּ.

> Vindicate the wretched and the orphan; acquit the lowly and the destitute.

פַּלְּטוּ־דַל וְאֶבְיוֹן; מִיַּד רְשָׁעִים, הַצִּילוּ.

> Rescue the wretched and the needy; snatch them from the hand of the wicked.

In both verses, the Hebrew word order builds a physical shelter around the vulnerable. Each verse opens with an imperative verb (שִׁפְטוּ, "vindicate; פַּלְּטוּ, rescue") and closes with an imperative verb (הַצְדִּיקוּ, "acquit; הַצִּילוּ, snatch away"), enclosing the poor inside a ring of saving action. And as Ibn Ezra notices, the verbs escalate from verse 3 to verse 4: שִׁפְטוּ דַּל בְּמָמוֹן, וְפַלְּטוּ דַּל בְּנֶפֶשׁ, "judge the wretched in property, and rescue the wretched in life." Verse 3 uses courtroom verbs—giving the orphan a hearing and declaring the destitute man in the right (הַצְדִּיקוּ). By verse 4, a favorable verdict on paper is no longer enough; the judges must physically pry the victim out of the predator's grip (מִיַּד רְשָׁעִים, "from the hand of the wicked"). Malbim (*Beur Hamilot*) traces the same downward spiral in the five nouns for the poor. A רָשׁ ("destitute man") is someone impoverished of his inheritance, the inverse of יָרַשׁ ("inherit"), while the עָנִי ("lowly") comes to court over what is being taken from his estate; by the time we reach דַּל וְאֶבְיוֹן ("the wretched and the needy") in verse 4, the victim has been so thoroughly fleeced that he has no property suit left to bring—only his body to be rescued.

As the introduction observed, Psalm 82 edits Leviticus 19:15 by deleting its ban on favoring the poor in court. In the Babylonian Talmud (*Chullin* 134a), Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish spots the legal tension that deletion creates and asks how a judge can be ordered to "acquit the lowly and the destitute" without violating Exodus 23:3: מַאי דִּכְתִיב: עָנִי וָרָשׁ הַצְדִּיקוּ? מַאי הַצְדִּיקוּ? אִילֵּימָא בְּדִינִים, וְהָא כְּתִיב: וְדָל לֹא תֶהְדַּר בְּרִיבוֹ! אֶלָּא, צַדֵּק מִשֶּׁלְּךָ וְתֵן לוֹ, "What is the meaning of that which is written, 'Acquit the lowly and the destitute'? What does 'acquit' mean? If we say in lawsuits, is it not written, 'Nor shall you show deference to a poor man in his dispute' (Exodus 23:3)? Rather, be generous from your own property and give to him." Rashi, sticking to the plain courtroom sense, adds a careful qualifier: אִם זַכַּאי הוּא בְּדִינוֹ, אַל תַּהַפְּכוּ דִינוֹ לְטוֹבָה בִּשְׁבִיל לָשֵׂאת פְּנֵי רְשָׁעִים, "If he is right in his cause, do not reverse the verdict to condemn him in order to favor the wicked."

One stock figure is conspicuously missing from this catalogue of five poor-terms: the widow (אַלְמָנָה). In biblical law and prophecy—such as Isaiah 1:17, שִׁפְטוּ יָתוֹם, רִיבוּ אַלְמָנָה, "defend the orphan, plead for the widow"—as well as in the Ugaritic *Epic of Kirta*, the orphan and the widow are an inseparable pair. Her absence here is so surprising that Ibn Ezra, paraphrasing verse 3 in his commentary, unconsciously writes her back in: וְאַלְמָנָה שֶׁאֵין לָהּ בַּעַל הַצְדִּיקוּ, "and the widow who has no husband, acquit!" Instead of naming the widow, the poet doubles the word דַּל ("wretched") at the opening of both verses. Yet in the Temple's weekly liturgy (*Tamid* 7:4), the missing widow arrives the very next morning. Wednesday's psalm, Psalm 94, opens by echoing our psalm's closing cry (הִנָּשֵׂא שֹׁפֵט הָאָרֶץ, "Rise up, Judge of the earth," 94:2) and its opening question (עַד־מָתַי רְשָׁעִים, "How long shall the wicked," 94:3), and then names the victims left unprotected when the court fails: אַלְמָנָה וְגֵר יַהֲרֹגוּ, וִיתוֹמִים יְרַצֵּחוּ, "They kill the widow and the stranger; they murder the orphans" (94:6).

In his 1637 pastoral elegy *Lycidas*, John Milton stages a visitation much like verses 3–4, as St. Peter steps forward to indict the corrupt guardians of the flock who gorge themselves while the vulnerable starve:

> How well could I have spar'd for thee young swain,

> Anow of such as for their bellies sake,

> Creep and intrude, and climb into the fold?

> Of other care they little reck'ning make,

> Then how to scramble at the shearers feast,

> And shove away the worthy bidden guest.

> Blind mouthes! that scarce themselves know how to hold

> A Sheep-hook, or have learn'd ought els the least

Milton's two-word oxymoron—"Blind mouthes!"—captures the exact transition from verses 3–4 to verse 5. Guardians who turn an office of vision and care into an appetite lose the ability to see at all, and "scarce themselves know" the first thing about their charge.

**Verse 5**

לֹא יָדְעוּ וְלֹא יָבִינוּ, בַּחֲשֵׁכָה יִתְהַלָּכוּ; יִמּוֹטוּ, כׇּל־מוֹסְדֵי אָרֶץ.

> They neither know nor understand, in darkness they walk about; all the foundations of the earth totter.

The sudden drop from second-person commands ("rescue," "snatch away") into third-person diagnosis ("they neither know nor understand") creates an eerie grammatical ambiguity. Who is "they"? Rashi and Radak identify the subject as the corrupt judges, whose moral blindness echoes Exodus 23:8 (כִּי הַשֹּׁחַד יְעַוֵּר פִּקְחִים, "for a bribe blinds the clear-sighted") and matches Deutero-Isaiah's description of idol-makers in Isaiah 44:18 (לֹא יָדְעוּ וְלֹא יָבִינוּ, "they neither know nor understand"), while Meiri hears the shift as introducing a second class of magistrates—not those who deliberately pervert justice, but appointees ignorant of the ways of the law. Yet the nearest plural noun is רְשָׁעִים, "the wicked," at the end of verse 4. Moses Alshich (*Romemot El*) seizes on that proximity to split the verse in two: the wicked predators "neither know nor understand" the punishment awaiting them, which lessens their guilt, whereas the silent judges who refuse to stop them cause "all the foundations of the earth to totter."

The Aramaic Targum welds verse 4 to verse 5 through a single remarkable wordplay. To render the five poor-terms of verses 3–4, the Targum in verse 4 adds a fifth Aramaic noun for the needy: וַחֲשׁוֹכָא, literally "the darkened man," the person pushed into obscurity and want. Then in verse 5 it renders בַּחֲשֵׁכָה יִתְהַלָּכוּ ("in darkness they walk about") with the identical word: בַּחֲשׁוֹכָא מְהַלְכִין. In the Targum's ear, rulers who abandon the man in the dark end up walking inside the very darkness they left him in.

Two findings of modern social psychology illuminate why an exalted assembly (`עֵדָה`) falls into this specific blindness. In a 2006 experiment (*Power and Perspectives Not Taken*), Adam Galinsky, Joe Magee, M. Ena Inesi, and Deborah Gruenfeld asked participants to draw a capital letter *E* on their own foreheads with a marker. Participants primed to feel powerful were significantly more likely to draw the *E* oriented so that it read correctly to themselves from inside their own heads—and backward to anyone standing in front of them. Power impairs perspective-taking, the capacity to see the world from the vantage of the `דַּל` across the room. Compound that with John Darley and Bibb Latané's 1968 discovery (*Bystander Intervention in Emergencies*) that "the presence of other bystanders reduced the individual's feelings of personal responsibility," and the pathology of the council in verse 5 comes into sharp focus. Each member of the assembly looks out from his own exalted seat, assumes another member of the committee will handle the orphan's plea, and walks on in the dark.

Meanwhile, the floor gives way beneath them: יִמּוֹטוּ כׇּל־מוֹסְדֵי אָרֶץ, "all the foundations of the earth totter." Between 1532 and 1535, the Renaissance painter and architect Giulio Romano created a physical version of this line inside the *Sala dei Giganti* (Chamber of the Giants) at the Palazzo Te in Mantua. Romano frescoed the walls and dome without corners or seams so that massive stone columns and architraves appear to crack and crash down around the visitor, while Jupiter's assembly of gods looks down from the unbroken vault above. Psalm 82 builds the same room, but reverses the blame. Here the foundations crack not because rebels are storming heaven from below, but because the council in the vault has stopped doing justice—and in the next two verses, it is the figures in the vault who will be brought crashing down. (When the Temple Levites sang this line on the sixth day of Sukkot, the Talmud notes in *Sukkah* 55a, if Shabbat fell during the intermediate days of the festival, this snippet was bumped from the rotation: וְאִם חָל שַׁבָּת בְּאֶחָד מֵהֶם, יִמּוֹטוּ יִדָּחֶה, "and if Shabbat falls on one of them, 'they totter' is pushed aside"—as if the arrival of Shabbat physically steadies the tottering world.)

**Verses 6–7**

אֲנִי־אָמַרְתִּי, אֱלֹקִים אַתֶּם; וּבְנֵי עֶלְיוֹן כֻּלְּכֶם.

> I Myself had said, gods are you, and sons of the Most High, all of you;

אָכֵן, כְּאָדָם תְּמוּתוּן; וּכְאַחַד הַשָּׂרִים תִּפֹּלוּ.

> Yet in fact, like Adam you shall die, and like one of the princes you shall fall.

The Hebrew word כְּאָדָם ("like a man" or "like Adam") carries a double edge. Without the definite article, the exact form כְּאָדָם occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible: here, in Job 31:33 (כִּסִּיתִי כְאָדָם פְּשָׁעָי, "if I covered my transgressions like Adam"), and in Hosea 6:7 (וְהֵמָּה כְּאָדָם עָבְרוּ בְרִית, "But they, like Adam, transgressed the covenant"). Hearing כְּאָדָם as an allusion to the first man's transgression, the rabbis of the Talmud (*Avodah Zarah* 5a, followed by Rashi's gloss כְּאָדָם הָרִאשׁוֹן, "like the first man, Adam") read verses 6–7 as the story of Israel at Mount Sinai. When Israel accepted the Torah, God declared them deathless like the angels (אֱלֹקִים אַתֶּם); when they shattered the covenant at the Golden Calf, they were sentenced to die like Adam. In that Talmudic passage, Reish Lakish opens with a startling claim that the ensuing discussion refines (concluding that later generations would still have been born, yet would have been as though they had not come into the world): בּוֹאוּ וְנַחֲזִיק טוֹבָה לַאֲבוֹתֵינוּ, שֶׁאִלְמָלֵא הֵן לֹא חָטְאוּ — אָנוּ לֹא בָּאנוּ לָעוֹלָם, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: אֲנִי אָמַרְתִּי אֱלֹקִים אַתֶּם וּבְנֵי עֶלְיוֹן כֻּלְּכֶם, חִבַּלְתֶּם מַעֲשֵׂיכֶם — אָכֵן כְּאָדָם תְּמוּתוּן, "Come and let us be grateful to our ancestors, for had they not sinned, we would not have come into the world, as it is stated: 'I said, you are godlike beings, and all of you sons of the Most High'; you ruined your deeds—'yet like a man you shall die.'"

Set that rabbinic reading beside early Christian theology, and you witness one of the strangest reversals in the history of biblical interpretation. In John 10:34, Jesus quotes the Septuagint of verse 6—Ἐγὼ εἶπα, θεοί ἐστε (*Egō eipa, theoi este*), "I said, you are gods"—and from the second century onward, Church Fathers such as Irenaeus of Lyons (*Against Heresies* 3.6.1) and Athanasius detached verse 6 from verse 7's death sentence to make it the scriptural cornerstone of *theosis* (deification). Where the psalm and the Talmud read verses 6–7 as a **fall** from divine immortality into human death, patristic theology read verse 6 as the charter for humanity's **ascent** from mortal dust to divine immortality. That tug-of-war left its fingerprints on the ancient Aramaic translations. The Jewish Targum cautiously softens verse 6 into a simile: הֵי כְּמַלְאֲכַיָּא אַתּוּן חֲשִׁיבִין, "like angels you are reckoned." The Christian Syriac Peshitta does the reverse: after translating the אֱלֹקִים of verse 1 safely as "angels," it suddenly restores the literal plural "gods" in verse 6, where the Gospel of John had declared that "Scripture cannot be broken."

In the history of biology, there is an episode that enacts the exact logic of אֲנִי־אָמַרְתִּי ... אָכֵן ("I had declared ... yet in fact"). From 1912 to 1946, the Nobel laureate Alexis Carrel maintained a culture of embryonic chick-heart cells at the Rockefeller Institute that seemed to divide forever, making it scientific dogma that cells are intrinsically immortal and only die when the whole body fails. In 1961, Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead (*The Serial Cultivation of Human Diploid Cell Strains*) overturned that dogma. They demonstrated that normal cells divide roughly fifty times and then die, and that Carrel's famous "immortal" culture had almost certainly been kept going by fresh living cells inadvertently introduced each time the culture was fed. The gods of Psalm 82 suffer a Hayflick limit of their own: what they mistook for an innate, ontological immortality turns out to have been sustained from outside by the word of the Most High (`אֲנִי־אָמַרְתִּי`), and when that word is withdrawn, they age and die.

The parallel clause in verse 7b—וּכְאַחַד הַשָּׂרִים תִּפֹּלוּ, "and like one of the princes you shall fall"—is equally two-layered, as Rashi's two comments show. On earth, Rashi first reads them as earlier human princes who died—and in Ezekiel 11:10, God tells the corrupt שָׂרֵי הָעָם ("princes of the people) בַּחֶרֶב תִּפֹּלוּ ... אֶשְׁפּוֹט אֶתְכֶם, by the sword you shall fall ... I will judge you," as Prince Pelatiah drops dead while Ezekiel is still speaking. In heaven, Rashi's alternative midrashic reading takes הַשָּׂרִים as heavenly princes, whom Moses Alshich (*Romemot El*) specifies as the celestial patron-princes of the seventy nations (a title echoed in Daniel 10:13, where in a struggle against the patron-prince of Persia, Israel's own angelic prince Michael is called אַחַד הַשָּׂרִים הָרִאשֹׁנִים, "one of the chief princes"). Rashi's midrashic prooftext, Isaiah 24:21 (יִפְקֹד ה׳ עַל־צְבָא הַמָּרוֹם בַּמָּרוֹם, "the LORD will punish the host of heaven in heaven"), comes from a passage (Isaiah 24:18–21) that marches step-by-step through the second half of our psalm: the foundations of the earth shaking (מוֹסְדֵי אָרֶץ), the earth tottering (מוֹט הִתְמוֹטְטָה אָרֶץ), falling (וְנָפְלָה), and God judging both the celestial host above and the kings of the earth below.

In classical literature, the shock of a dying deity is felt vividly in Plutarch's dialogue *On the Obsolescence of Oracles* (*De defectu oraculorum* 17, c. 100 CE). A passenger named Epitherses recounts how his ship was becalmed one evening off the islands of Paxi when a voice from the shore called out three times to the Egyptian pilot, Thamus:

> Then the voice said aloud to him, When you are arrived at Palodes, take care to make it known that the great God Pan is dead. Epitherses told us, this voice did much astonish all that heard it, and caused much arguing whether this voice was to be obeyed or slighted. Thamus, for his part, was resolved, if the wind permitted, to sail by the place without saying a word; but if the wind ceased and there ensued a calm, to speak and cry out as loud as he was able what he was enjoined. Being come to Palodes, there was no wind stirring, and the sea was as smooth as glass. Whereupon Thamus standing on the deck, with his face towards the land, uttered with a loud voice his message, saying, The great Pan is dead. He had no sooner said this, but they heard a dreadful noise, not only of one, but of several, who, to their thinking, groaned and lamented with a kind of astonishment.

In Plutarch, the death of a god arrives as a mysterious rumor across glassy water, met by a chorus of grieving groans and scholarly bewilderment at Rome. In Psalm 82, no one groans for the fallen gods, and no one wonders why they died. The verdict has been read aloud, and their fall clears the stage for the final verse.

**Verse 8**

קוּמָה אֱלֹקִים, שׇׁפְטָה הָאָרֶץ; כִּי־אַתָּה תִנְחַל, בְּכׇל־הַגּוֹיִם.

> Arise, O God, judge the earth; for You shall take possession among all the nations.

Listen to the two verbs that sit side by side across the boundary of verses 7 and 8: תִּפֹּלוּ ("you shall fall") and תִנְחַל ("You shall inherit / take possession"). In biblical land law, those two roots—נפל and נחל—form the standard idiom for dividing territory by lot, as in Numbers 34:2: הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר תִּפֹּל לָכֶם בְּנַחֲלָה, "the land that shall fall to you as an inheritance." When the patron gods "fall" at the end of verse 7, their allotted jurisdictions fall back into the hands of the One Judge in verse 8. That also reverses the crisis of an earlier Asaph psalm, Psalm 79, the only other psalm in the Bible that begins with the sequence מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף אֱלֹקִים: there, foreign nations invaded God's inheritance (בָּאוּ גוֹיִם בְּנַחֲלָתֶךָ, "the nations have come into Your inheritance," 79:1); here, God steps in to inherit among all the nations (תִנְחַל בְּכׇל־הַגּוֹיִם).

In 2011, the architecture of internet security experienced a collapse that mirrors the mechanism of verses 6–8. Browsers delegate authority to trusted intermediate "certificate authorities" to vouch for the identity of websites worldwide. That summer, investigators discovered that a Dutch certificate authority named DigiNotar had issued fraudulent cryptographic certificates—including for Google—that were used to intercept the communications of three hundred thousand citizens in Iran. Once it was clear that the delegate had certified impostors to prey on the vulnerable, the browser and software vendors (Mozilla, Microsoft, Google) withdrew trust in DigiNotar's certificates; within weeks the company was clinically dead, and its domains reverted to the root.

Notice how differently Rome handled the patron gods of foreign nations. When Scipio Aemilianus besieged Carthage in 146 BCE, he performed the Roman ritual of *evocatio* (preserved by Macrobius in *Saturnalia* 3.9.7–8), calling on the city's unnamed patron deity to switch sides in exchange for a bribe:

> Whether you are a god or a goddess of these people who defend this city of Carthage, and you Most High, take back your favor in defense of this city and these people whom I attack. I pray, I beseech, I ask your indulgence, that you withdraw and desert these people and this city of Carthage, and that you relinquish the temples and sacred precincts of this city, go away without them, and incite these people and their city into fear of oblivion. Come then to favor Rome by crossing over to me and my army, and with our city tried and accepted as the location for your sacred precincts and holy rites, be propitious to me and the people of Rome, and my soldiers. If you make this happen, with clear and recognizable signs, I vow to erect temples for you and to initiate games in your honor.

Rome takes possession of a nation by bribing its god to abandon the people; the God of Psalm 82 takes possession of the nations by convicting their gods of abandoning the poor.

Why does the Hebrew say תִנְחַל **בְּ**כׇל־הַגּוֹיִם, "You shall inherit *in/among* all the nations," with the preposition **בְּ**, rather than "inherit all the nations"? Ibn Ezra notes that the **בְּ** can mark the direct object, or—quoting the poet Rabbi Judah Halevi—that it means: אַתָּה תִּהְיֶה נַחֲלָה לַצּוֹעֲקִים בְּכָל הַגּוֹיִם, "You will be an inheritance for those who cry out among all the nations." Radak and Meiri read the **בְּ** as singling out some from among the nations, with equal beauty. Not everyone in every nation is God's inheritance; rather, as Radak writes, אַתָּה תִנְחַל הָעֲנִיִּים הָעֲשׁוּקִים בְּאֵיזֶה עַם שֶׁיִּהְיֶה, כְּלוֹמַר הֵם עַמְּךָ וְנַחֲלָתְךָ, כִּי בְּרוֹב הָעֲנִיִּים וְהָעֲשׁוּקִים הֵם דַּכְּאֵי רוּחַ וְאַנְשֵׁי אֱמֶת, "You inherit the oppressed poor in whatever nation they may be—that is to say, they are Your people and Your inheritance, for in the main the poor and oppressed are contrite in spirit and people of truth." In a psalm that never once mentions the name Israel, Halevi's and Radak's readings invite comparison with the landless tribe of Levi (to whom Asaph himself belonged), whose kinsman Aaron God told in Numbers 18:20, using this exact verb form: בְּאַרְצָם לֹא תִנְחָל ... אֲנִי חֶלְקְךָ וְנַחֲלָתְךָ, "In their land you shall have no inheritance ... I am your portion and your inheritance." Wherever a person is stripped of land and justice on earth, that person stands in the place of the landless Levite—and becomes God's own claim on that nation.

Both synagogue and church heard verse 8 as the hinge of world history. In the Eastern Orthodox Easter Vigil on Holy Saturday morning, as the choir chants the Septuagint of this verse—Ἀνάστα, ὁ Θεός, κρῖνον τὴν γῆν (*Anasta, ho Theos, krinon tēn gēn*), "Arise, O God, judge the earth"—the dark vestments of Passion Week are changed for white and bells ring through the church, hearing קוּמָה ("Arise") as the resurrection. And in the daily Jewish prayer book, the second paragraph of the *Aleinu* prayer (traditionally ascribed to the school of the third-century Babylonian rabbi Rav, though likely composed in the land of Israel for the Rosh Hashanah kingship service) turns verse 8 into the closing plea of every service, asking God to remove the false gods from the earth in order לְתַקֵּן עוֹלָם בְּמַלְכוּת שַׁקַּי, "to repair the world under the sovereignty of the Almighty":

> עַל כֵּן נְקַוֶּה לְּךָ ה׳ אֱלֹקֵינוּ לִרְאוֹת מְהֵרָה בְּתִפְאַרֶת עֻזֶּךָ,

> לְהַעֲבִיר גִּלּוּלִים מִן הָאָרֶץ וְהָאֱלִילִים כָּרוֹת יִכָּרֵתוּן,

> לְתַקֵּן עוֹלָם בְּמַלְכוּת שַׁקַּי, וְכָל בְּנֵי בָשָׂר יִקְרְאוּ בִשְׁמֶךָ,

> לְהַפְנוֹת אֵלֶיךָ כָּל רִשְׁעֵי אָרֶץ.

> יַכִּירוּ וְיֵדְעוּ כָּל יוֹשְׁבֵי תֵבֵל כִּי לְךָ תִּכְרַע כָּל בֶּרֶךְ, תִּשָּׁבַע כָּל לָשׁוֹן.

>

> We therefore put our hope in You, LORD our God, to soon behold the glory of Your might,

> to banish idolatry from the earth, and the false gods will be utterly cut off,

> to repair the world under the kingdom of Shaddai, and all flesh will invoke Your Name,

> to turn back to You all the wicked of the earth.

> They will recognize and know, all the inhabitants of the world, that to You every knee must bend, every tongue must swear.

Where the gods of verse 5 "neither know nor understand" (לֹא יָדְעוּ) while the foundations of the earth totter, *Aleinu* prays for the day when the false gods are cut off, the tottering world is set right (לְתַקֵּן עוֹלָם), and all the inhabitants of the earth finally know (יַכִּירוּ וְיֵדְעוּ) the one Judge who answers the cry of the poor.
