### P4 (flagged)

**Before:**

Its first hearers knew whose hymn this had been. The phrase "sons of gods" is the Ugaritic bn ilm, the council of El. Lebanon and Sirion are the pair of mountains from which Baal's palace cedars are fetched in the Ugaritic epic. Baal's "holy voice" makes the earth shake. Canaanite kings used the storm god's voice as a title they could move from one master to another. Abimilki of Tyre, a city under the shadow of Lebanon, wrote to Pharaoh in the fourteenth century BCE praising him as one who gives his cry in the heavens like Baal, so that the whole land trembles. Psalm 29 does the same thing with the same title and gives it to YHWH. Of its eighteen divine names, every one is YHWH.

### P5 (flagged)

**Before:**

What the psalm leaves out matters as much as what it takes over. There is a famous limestone stele from Ugarit, now in the Louvre, in which Baal strides forward over wavy lines of sea and mountain with a club raised over his head and a spear in his other hand whose shaft sprouts like lightning. Psalm 29 keeps the sea and the mountains and drops the club, the stride, and the battle. Its God never strikes anything. Even the waters, which in the neighboring psalms roar, lift their voice, and writhe, are never once the subject of a verb here. They appear only after a preposition: עַל־הַמָּיִם, "over the waters," and at the end לַמַּבּוּל, "over the Flood." They are not defeated, because they never get to act at all. The Ugaritic text that authorizes Baal's palace announces his storm as a set: his rain in its season, his voice in the clouds, his lightning flashing to earth. Psalm 29 has no word for rain, cloud, wind, or hail, and avoids the ordinary word for lightning. Baal's characteristic gift is rain. This storm will give something else.

### P24 (flagged)

**Before:**

Ibn Ezra makes the most daring move. The בְּנֵי אֵלִים are the stars, because "according to their configuration the rains descend, with the help of the God of gods." Meiri recoils at this and calls it וזה ככפירה גמורה, "this is like outright heresy." He objects because Ibn Ezra has turned the heavenly beings into astrological causes. The quarrel is worth noticing: eight centuries ago, two Jewish readers were arguing over the same unease the psalm itself handles, which is how much power the lesser heavenly beings really have. Malbim, writing in the nineteenth century, gives the boldest reading of all. For him the "sons of gods" are the forces of nature, and the summons means "make yourselves ready to be His messengers." The flood, the hail, and the fire are the elim. As they march into the holy land they come to the place of His presence, where "they fall on their faces… and become stillness." Keep this reading in mind for verse 9.

### P34 (flagged)

**Before:**

The epithet קֵל־הַכָּבוֹד, "the God of glory," is the only divine name in the psalm that is not YHWH. Both Talmuds noticed it, because they had derived eighteen Amidah blessings from the psalm's eighteen YHWHs and were then left with a nineteenth blessing, the one against sectarians. Rabbi Levi assigns it here: כְּנֶגֶד קֵל הַכָּבוֹד הִרְעִים, "corresponding to 'the God of glory thunders'" (Berakhot 28b). The Talmud is doing arithmetic, but modern scholars read this same epithet as the psalm's own polemic, since it is an El-title attached to Baal's thunder. The rabbis' polemical blessing and the poet's polemical epithet ended up at the same place.

**After:**

The epithet קֵל־הַכָּבוֹד, "the God of glory," is the only divine name in the psalm that is not YHWH. Both Talmuds noticed it, because they connect the Amidah's blessings with the psalm's eighteen YHWHs and had to account for the blessing against sectarians.
