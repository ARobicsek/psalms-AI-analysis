#### Full psalm

Psalm 76 is the Psalm of the Day for the first day of Sukkot in the Edot HaMizrach rite and in communities that follow the custom of the Vilna Gaon. The obvious reason is a word in v. 3, וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ, "His booth was in Salem." The fit goes deeper than the word. Sukkot is the festival at which, in Zechariah's vision, the survivors of the nations that attacked Jerusalem come up every year to keep the Feast of Booths, and that chapter is read as the haftarah of the festival's first day. The psalm's ending, with the peoples around God bringing tribute, is the same scene. Some Ashkenazi and Hasidic communities also recite the psalm on the seventh day of Passover, the day the Song at the Sea is read in the Torah. The link there is v. 7's "chariot and horse" stunned into sleep, which recalls Pharaoh's cavalry.

#### Key verses

The opening line, נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ, "God is known in Judah; in Israel His name is great" (v. 2), appears twice in Nusach Sefard, the Hasidic rite. On Simchat Torah it is part of the chain of verses recited during the hakafot, the processions with the Torah scrolls. It comes just before the fifth circuit, after a plea that God "rebuild the Temple speedily in our days" and restore "the voice of the Levite… with harp and lyre." A psalm about a sanctuary becomes a line chanted while the congregation circles its own sanctuary. The same words, recast in the second person, open an alphabetical selichah for the first Monday of the pre–Rosh Hashanah penitential season: אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל נוֹדָעְתָּ, אַתָּה יְהוָה אָבִינוּ אָתָּה, "God, great in Israel, You have made Yourself known; You, LORD, are our Father." The psalm's boast becomes the basis of a confession: You have been known among us, and we have not deserved it (לֹא בְצִדְקוֹתֵינוּ, "not by our righteousness").

In v. 8, וּמִי־יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ, "who can stand before You?", is taken into Abraham ibn Ezra's piyyut *Lecha Eli Teshukati*, recited on the eve of Yom Kippur in the Edot HaMizrach rite: וּמִי יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ וּמִי יִהְיֶה תְמוּרָתִי, "who can stand before You, and who will stand in my place?" The line leads straight into the alphabetical confession (אָשַׁמְתִּי וְאָרַבְתִּי וּבָגַדְתִּי, "I have trespassed, lain in wait, betrayed…"). The psalm's question was asked of a defeated army. The liturgy puts it to the worshipper.

The last verse's יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "He cuts off the spirit of princes" (v. 13), appears in the Ashkenazi Rosh Hashanah Shacharit, in the yotzer piyyut *Melekh Azur Gevurah*: מֶלֶךְ חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים, בְּדָרְכוֹ בוֹגְדִים, יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "the King in crimsoned garments, as He treads down the treacherous, cuts off the spirit of princes." A Nusach Sefard Shabbat zemer sets the same phrase in a stanza built from Isaiah 63: בְּבוֹאוֹ מֵאֱדוֹם חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים… בְּכֹחוֹ הַגָּדוֹל יִבְצוֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "when He comes from Edom in crimsoned garments… in His great power He cuts off the spirit of princes." Both poets heard the grape-harvest in the verb. The note on v. 13 takes this up.

#### Practical Kabbalah

Shimush Tehillim prescribes the psalm "to be saved from fire and from water" (להנצל מאש וממים). The language of the psalm explains both. Fire comes from v. 4, where God breaks רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת, the "flames" (or "Reshephs") "of the bow." Water comes from v. 7's גְּעָרָה, the divine "rebuke," which in almost every other biblical use is the blast that drives back the sea (Psalms 104:7, 106:9; Nahum 1:4). A psalm whose two mythic words are a broken fire-bolt and a sea-rebuke became a charm against flame and flood. Later manuals also recommend it to travelers menaced by robbers, in the hope that attackers, like the warriors of v. 6, will "not find their hands."

### VERSE COMMENTARY

**Verse 1**

לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת; מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף שִׁיר.

> For the conductor, with strings: a psalm of Asaph, a song.

The heading is conventional, but one of its phrases has a notable second home. The only place outside the Psalter where לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינוֹת, "for the conductor, with strings," appears is the last line of Habakkuk 3 (לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינוֹתָי). That chapter is a theophany in which God marches out as a warrior, and it shares an unusual amount with our psalm. Resheph walks at God's heels there (וְיֵצֵא רֶשֶׁף לְרַגְלָיו, Hab 3:5); here Resheph's arrows are broken (v. 4). Habakkuk's God rides against "horses" and "chariots" (3:8); here horse and chariot fall asleep. Habakkuk speaks of הַרְרֵי־עַד, "the everlasting mountains" (3:6), which is exactly the phrase the Greek translators read in place of our psalm's "mountains of prey" (v. 5). The two poems were probably not written together. They are drawing on one stock of storm-and-war language, and the shared musical tag suggests they were performed in the same way.

The root of לַמְנַצֵּחַ also means "to prevail." In a psalm where the only victor is God, the conductor's title carries a buried pun: the song is handed "to the one who prevails." Whether any ancient singer heard it that way cannot be known.

The commentators split over the date. Malbim says Asaph "composed it in the days of Hezekiah on the miracle of Sennacherib's fall." Meiri, who assumes Asaph was David's contemporary, has David foresee the event and praise God "as if it had already come about." The first reading makes the psalm a memory, the second makes it a prophecy. The psalm's own verbs allow both, as the introduction showed.

**Verse 2**

נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים; בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ.

> God has made Himself known in Judah; in Israel His name is great.

Judah comes first, and the commentators notice. Meiri says it is placed first "for the honor of the kingship," since the Davidic throne was in Judah. Malbim's word-notes find a gradation in the parallel: to be "known" (נוֹדָע) is to be known in oneself, and that happens in Judah, "where the Shekhinah is"; in Israel there is only שְׁמוֹ, "His name," meaning His reputation. On this reading the verse is not a simple repetition of one idea. It moves from the center, where God is present, to the wider circle, where He is talked about.

The Alshich preserves a sharper midrash. When the ten northern tribes were exiled and Judah was spared, the nations (or, in another version, the exiled tribes themselves) said that God was showing favor to "the people of His inn," the tribe He lodged with. When Judah was later exiled too, God was "known in Judah" as a judge without favorites, and His name became great among the tribes who had doubted Him. The midrash hears נוֹדָע, "made known," as a verdict God gives about Himself. That is not the plain sense, which celebrates a deliverance rather than an exile. But it takes the passive grammar seriously: nobody discovers God here. He discloses Himself.

The pairing of the roots "known" and "great" recurs where the Bible describes nations assaulting Zion. Psalm 48 opens with גָּדוֹל יְהוָה, "great is the LORD," and continues נוֹדַע לְמִשְׂגָּב, "known as a stronghold." Ezekiel's oracle against Gog ends וְהִתְגַּדִּלְתִּי… וְנוֹדַעְתִּי לְעֵינֵי גּוֹיִם רַבִּים, "I will make Myself great… and I will make Myself known in the eyes of many nations" (Ezek 38:23). Radak read our whole psalm as the war of Gog, and this opening line gave him a reason.

**Verse 3**

וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סוּכּוֹ; וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ בְצִיּוֹן.

> His booth was in Salem, and His lair in Zion.

The introduction discussed the lion's den behind these two nouns. Two further readers add something. Malbim notes that a סֻכָּה is temporary and a מָעוֹן permanent, and maps the difference onto the city: Salem is Jerusalem at large, "where the masses dwelt," and there God has a festival hut; Zion is the citadel "where the king and the Sanhedrin and the priests sat," and there He has a fixed home. The line becomes a map of how near God is to each part of the city.

The second reader is a Masoretic note, preserved by Minchat Shai. The word סכו appears three times in the Bible "in reading," each spelled differently: "He has left his lair like a young lion" (Jer 25:38), spelled without vav; "His booth was in Salem," spelled here with a vav, סוּכּוֹ; and a third spelled with a שׂ, in Lamentations 2:6: וַיַּחְמֹס כַּגַּן שֻׂכּוֹ שִׁחֵת מוֹעֲדוֹ, "He stripped His booth like a garden; He destroyed His place of meeting." The scribes' list places the booth of our psalm between a lion leaving its lair and a Temple torn down. Whether the Masoretes meant anything by this is doubtful; they were counting spellings. But the list says that a booth can be a fortress, a lair, or a ruin, and Isaiah had already used the image of fragility for Zion: "like a booth in a vineyard… like a besieged city" (כְּסֻכָּה בְכָרֶם… כְּעִיר נְצוּרָה, Isa 1:8). Psalm 76 takes the most breakable dwelling in Israel's vocabulary and makes it the one no army can enter, because of who lives in it.

Ted Hughes's zoo jaguar is a creature whose enclosure cannot contain what it is:

> By the bang of blood in the brain deaf the ear—
> He spins from the bars, but there's no cage to him
> More than to the visionary his cell:
> His stride is wildernesses of freedom:
> The world rolls under the long thrust of his heel.

Hughes's cage is a real prison that the animal's nature cancels. The psalm's lair is a chosen home, and it seems to confine God only in the way a lion "confines" itself to a thicket. The poem has just placed the infinite God in one small city, and what Hughes shows is the danger of mistaking a location for a limit. Verse 9 will reveal that the one who lives in the booth issues His verdict "from heaven."

**Verse 4**

שָׁמָּה שִׁבַּר רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת; מָגֵן וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה, סֶלָה.

> There He broke the flaming shafts of the bow, shield and sword and war. Selah.

Rashi settles what רִשְׁפֵי means by pointing to a single dot. The word here has a soft פ (no dagesh), whereas "flames of fire" in the Song of Songs, רִשְׁפֵּי אֵשׁ (Song 8:6), has a hard one. Minchat Shai confirms that the Masoretic tradition reads it soft. For Rashi the difference in sound is a difference in word. This רֶשֶׁף belongs with the "sons of resheph" who "fly upward" (Job 5:7), with the cattle given "to the reshaphim" (Ps 78:48), and with Deuteronomy's רֶשֶׁף, which the Aramaic translation renders "birds." So the word means things that fly, and here it means arrows, "the arrows the bow makes fly." Besides, says Rashi, "sparks of fire have nothing to do with a bow."

A modern reader hears a third meaning that Rashi's list contains without naming. Resheph was a Canaanite god of plague and archery, worshipped at Ugarit and in Egypt. A Phoenician inscription from Cyprus calls him "Resheph of the arrow." In Habakkuk (3:5) he marches as a servant in God's train. Here his arrows lie snapped on the ground. Whether or not the poet intended the god, a hearer who knew the name would have heard a rival deity reduced to broken equipment.

The emphatic שָׁמָּה, "there," has a probable source. Isaiah's oracle to Sennacherib promises: וְלֹא־יוֹרֶה שָׁם חֵץ וְלֹא־יְקַדְּמֶנָּה מָגֵן, "he shall not shoot an arrow there, nor come before it with a shield" (Isa 37:33; 2 Kgs 19:32). The psalm answers with the same three things, "there," arrow and shield. The prophet's "he will not shoot there" becomes the singer's "there He broke the arrows." Prophecy has become report.

The Alshich reads the verse against itself. God's "arrows of wrath," which would have destroyed Israel, were spent on Zion: God "poured out His fury on wood and stones" when the Temple burned, and the building served as Israel's מָגֵן, a shield that takes the blows meant for its bearer. That is homiletics driven by the destruction of the Second Temple, far from the plain sense. But it notices that "shield" is the one defensive item in an offensive list, and asks whose shield it was.

The verse ends on the abstract noun and then a pause. Ilya Kaminsky's *Deaf Republic* imagines a town that answers soldiers' violence by collectively refusing to hear:

> The sound we do not hear lifts the gulls off the water.

Kaminsky's silence is a human act of resistance. The psalm's Selah comes after the resistance is over and was never human. Both treat silence as something that falls over a battlefield like a verdict.

**Verse 5**

נָאוֹר אַתָּה; אַדִּיר מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף.

> Resplendent are You, majestic from the mountains of prey.

The first word is the problem. נָאוֹר looks like a passive participle of אוֹר, "light," so "illuminated, resplendent." But the form is odd, and the psalm is full of נוֹרָא, "awesome" (vv. 8, 13), which is the same consonants rearranged. Rashi takes another root entirely: נֵאַר, "to spurn, to destroy," as in נִאֵר מִקְדָּשׁוֹ, "He has spurned His sanctuary" (Lam 2:7). God is then "the Destroyer," named for what He does, "as He is called 'Gracious' because He grants grace." Ibn Ezra keeps the light and gives a philosopher's reason: "nothing visible is glorious except that which is not a body… only light." The Aramaic Targum, as the Alshich reports, translates דְּחִיל אַתְּ, "You are feared," which suggests it read or understood נוֹרָא. The Greek has "You shine forth wondrously."

Malbim reconciles the two by recalling the time of night. Sennacherib's army died in the dark, so God goes out from Zion "like a lion from the mountains of prey" at night, and is called נָאוֹר because "it is not dark for You when You go to the enemy camp." Light and terror describe one event, the terror felt as a glare.

The second half has its own problem. The Greek "from the everlasting mountains" presupposes הַרְרֵי עַד, a known phrase (Hab 3:6; Gen 49:26). The Hebrew "mountains of prey" is harder and stranger, and it has a close parallel in the Song of Songs' pairing of lions' dens with the leopards' mountains, noted in the introduction. Radak takes the Hebrew as it stands: "as the lion goes out from the mountains of prey to tear, so You tear all the enemies." Rashi takes מִן as comparative: God is "mightier than the predatory giants, tall as mountains." The Hebrew keeps the lion alive, and the argument of vv. 3–6 depends on it.

**Verse 6**

אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ אַבִּירֵי לֵב, נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם; וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ כָל־אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל יְדֵיהֶם.

> The stout of heart were stripped bare; they slept their sleep; and all the men of might did not find their hands.

The first verb begins with an א where one expects a ה. Rashi explains it by comparison with אֶתְחַבַּר for הִתְחַבֵּר in 2 Chronicles 20:35. It is an Aramaic habit that turns up in late Hebrew. The commentators disagree over which root שׁלל is here. Radak derives it from שָׁלָל, "plunder": the warriors who came for spoil "become spoil for Israel." Rashi derives it from שׁוֹלָל, the "folly" or "madness" of Job 12:17 (מוֹלִיךְ יוֹעֲצִים שׁוֹלָל, "He leads counselors away stripped," or "into madness"): the warriors "went mad, like fools who err." Malbim's word-notes offer a third sense, "forgot," and his commentary follows it: how could these stout-hearted men forget that the greatest predator of all would come down on them from Zion that night, and lie down to sleep? Plunder, madness, forgetfulness. Each reading takes something different from the "stout-hearted": their loot, their minds, their vigilance.

Meiri hears finality in the possessive of נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם, "they slept *their* sleep." It is the sleep that was already theirs, the sleep of death, as in Jeremiah's word to Babylon: וְיָשְׁנוּ שְׁנַת־עוֹלָם וְלֹא יָקִיצוּ, "they shall sleep an everlasting sleep and not wake" (Jer 51:39).

The "hands" that cannot be found have a counterpart in Lorenzo da Ponte's libretto for Mozart's *Don Giovanni* (1787), where the stone guest asks the fearless libertine for his hand:

> COMMENDATORE: Dammi la mano in pegno!
> DON GIOVANNI: Eccola! ... Ohimè!
> Che gelo è questo mai!
>
> COMMENDATORE: Give me your hand as a pledge!
> DON GIOVANNI: Here it is! ... Alas!
> What ice is this!

The bravest man on stage gives his hand and loses it to cold. Da Ponte's hero at least has his hand long enough to offer it. The psalm's warriors reach for theirs and find nothing, so the defeat happens before any gesture is possible.

The night setting was taken up by a synagogue poet. Yannai's *Az Rov Nissim*, sung in the Ashkenazi Passover Haggadah with its refrain וַיְהִי בַּחֲצִי הַלַּיְלָה, "and it came to pass at midnight," lists God's night-time rescues, and one of them is this one:

> יָעַץ מְחָרֵף לְנוֹפֵף אִוּוּי, הוֹבַשְׁתָּ פְגָרָיו בַּלַּיְלָה,
> כָּרַע בֵּל וּמַצָּבוֹ בְּאִישׁוֹן לַיְלָה.
>
> The blasphemer plotted to shake his fist at the desired city; You withered his corpses in the night.
> Bel and his pedestal bowed down in the dead of night.

Yannai files Sennacherib with the Exodus under one heading, *night*. It is the same grouping the psalm makes when it follows these sleeping warriors with Pharaoh's "horse and chariot."

**Verse 7**

מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב; נִרְדָּם וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס.

> At Your rebuke, God of Jacob, both chariot and horse sank into deep sleep.

The verb is singular, and there is a ו before רֶכֶב that seems to serve no purpose. Rashi calls it redundant, like the ו in "these are the sons of Zibeon: *and* Aiah and Anah" (Gen 36:24). Ibn Ezra lets the singular verb reach back into the previous verse: "each one of the men of might" was stunned, "and chariot and horse" too. Radak finds a separate subject for the singular: נִרְדָּם is the king himself, the head of the "stout of heart," and after him chariot and horse. On Ibn Ezra's reading, the verse makes every warrior sleep alone, one by one. On Radak's, the commander is put under first and his army after him.

Why "God of Jacob" here, in a Zion psalm? Ibn Ezra's answer is structural: it picks up "Israel" from v. 2. Another answer lies in Jacob's blessing of Joseph, where the patriarch's bow stays firm and "the arms of his hands" are made strong מִידֵי אֲבִיר יַעֲקֹב, "by the hands of the Mighty One of Jacob" (Gen 49:24). Bow, hands, אביר, Jacob: in vv. 4–7 the psalm has broken the bow, taken the hands from the אַבִּירֵי לֵב, and now names God by Jacob's name. If the echo is meant, it is pointed. The enemies bear the consonants of a divine title, and the psalm then gives "Jacob" back to its owner.

Randall Jarrell's gunner goes from one sleep to another inside a modern war-machine:

> From my mother's sleep I fell into the State,
> And I hunched in its belly till my wet fur froze.
> Six miles from earth, loosed from its dream of life,
> I woke to black flak and the nightmare fighters.
> When I died they washed me out of the turret with a hose.

The chariot was the ancient world's bomber, the weapon an empire used to show what it was. Jarrell's airman wakes into the machine and dies there. The psalm's charioteers never wake, and the machine sleeps with them. Jarrell's poem accuses the state that used the boy up. The psalm's accusation falls on the machine itself, and the verdict is one breath.

**Verse 8**

אַתָּה נוֹרָא אַתָּה; וּמִי־יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ מֵאָז אַפֶּךָ.

> You, awesome are You! And who can stand before You from the moment of Your anger?

The pronoun appears twice around one word. Ibn Ezra calls the second אַתָּה unnecessary and gives a parallel: "LORD, a refuge You have been to us" (Ps 90:1), where one reference would have been enough. The doubling is how the psalm sounds when it turns to face God: `'at-TĀH NŌ-rā' 'AT-tāh`. The adjective is fenced in on both sides by "You." What the grammar treats as surplus is how the line sounds when it is spoken in awe.

מֵאָז אַפֶּךָ has a range of meanings. Rashi reads "from the moment You grow angry." Ibn Ezra hears מֵאָז as in Genesis 39:5, "from the time that he appointed him." Malbim gives it a history. Assyria was שֵׁבֶט אַפִּי, "the rod of My anger" (Isa 10:5), so "from the time You sent Your anger" upon the earth in the form of Assyria, no kingdom could stand before it. The terror the nations felt at Assyria was God's own terror, on loan. On Malbim's reading the rhetorical question applies twice: no one could stand before the rod, and now the rod itself cannot stand.

The question was a set form: "Who can stand before His indignation?" (Nah 1:6), and "who can endure the day of His coming?" (Mal 3:2). Ibn Ezra's Yom Kippur piyyut, discussed above, puts it to the worshipper.

**Verse 9**

מִשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּ דִּין; אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה.

> From heaven You made judgment heard; the earth feared and grew still.

The introduction gave Hezekiah's question from the Talmud and its answer. The Chida, who is usually brief, stays with that answer, because on the surface it merely repeats the verse. He cites a teacher who explains that fear never becomes stillness directly. Between them come dread and trembling, and only when these are exhausted does quiet arrive. The Talmud's "at first it feared, and in the end it was still" means that the verse names only the first and last stages and skips everything between. That is also how the psalm works: it skips the battle and shows only the terror and the aftermath.

The same verse carries a second rabbinic reading, in Avodah Zarah 3a. Resh Lakish taught that God made creation conditional: if Israel accepted the Torah, the world would stand; if not, it would return to chaos. So "the earth feared" while the offer was pending, and "grew still" when Israel said yes. On this reading the verdict "from heaven" was Sinai. The rabbis had a reason in the text. Deuteronomy says of Sinai, מִן־הַשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמִיעֲךָ אֶת־קֹלוֹ, "from heaven He made you hear His voice… and on earth He showed you His great fire" (Deut 4:36). It has the same verb, "made heard," the same "from heaven," and the same pairing of heaven and earth. A few verses earlier that chapter speaks of God's מוֹרָאִים גְּדֹלִים, "great terrors" (Deut 4:34), which is the noun this psalm will use as a divine title in v. 12.

The word שָׁקָטָה completes a phrase begun in v. 4. The book of Joshua closes its conquest narratives with וְהָאָרֶץ שָׁקְטָה מִמִּלְחָמָה, "and the land had rest from war" (Josh 14:15; 11:23). Psalm 76 splits the formula. Its last word, מִלְחָמָה, is broken in v. 4; its first words, אֶרֶץ… שָׁקָטָה, return here. In Joshua the land rests because Israel has won. Here it rests because war has been dismantled. And אֶרֶץ now means "earth" as well as "land": Rashi reads Eretz Israel, freed from its fear of Sennacherib, and Radak reads "the lands of the nations," which stop making war on Israel. Ibn Ezra divides the verse by character: "the earth feared" means the wicked, and "grew still" means the righteous.

Octavio Paz imagines silence as a thing with an edge:

> Así como del fondo de la música
> brota una nota
> que mientras vibra crece y se adelgaza
> hasta que en otra música enmudece,
> brota del fondo del silencio
> otro silencio, aguda torre, espada...
>
> Just as from the depths of music
> a note springs forth
> that while it vibrates grows and thins
> until it falls silent in another music,
> there springs from the depths of silence
> another silence, a sharp tower, a sword...

Paz's sword of silence rises out of silence. In the psalm, silence rises out of the broken sword. The earth's stillness is not calm. It is the weight of a verdict that has just been read out.

**Verse 10**

בְּקוּם־לַמִּשְׁפָּט אֱלֹהִים; לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, סֶלָה.

> When God rose to judgment, to save all the lowly of the earth. Selah.

The introduction dealt with the absence of "us" in this line. What remains is why the earth's silence was relief as well as terror, and Malbim, commenting on vv. 8–10 together, supplies the logic. The earth stilled its fear because it understood what kind of judge had stood up: "divine judgment does not come to strengthen the tyrant who oppresses and robs, but only to save the humble crushed beneath his hand. So when the earth heard that God had come to judgment, it grew quiet from its fear." For the powerful, the judge's rising is terrifying. For everyone else it means the terror is over.

The phrase עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ is Isaiah's, in his portrait of the ideal king: וְהוֹכִיחַ בְּמִישׁוֹר לְעַנְוֵי־אָרֶץ, "he shall decide with equity for the lowly of the earth" (Isa 11:4). There too the king's weapon is his breath: "with the breath of his lips he shall slay the wicked." Zephaniah uses the phrase as a call to seek God: בַּקְּשׁוּ אֶת־יְהוָה כָּל־עַנְוֵי הָאָרֶץ, "seek the LORD, all you lowly of the earth… perhaps you will be hidden on the day of the LORD's anger" (Zeph 2:3). In each case the lowly are defined by their condition, not their nation.

The seventeenth-century Marathi poet-saint Tukaram, a low-caste grocer whose songs are still sung by pilgrims to Pandharpur, gives a test for where God is found:

> जे का रंजले गांजले । त्यासी म्हणे जो आपुले ॥
> तोचि साधु ओळखावा । देव तेथेचि जाणावा ॥
>
> Those who are tormented and oppressed —
> he who calls them his own,
> know him to be the true saint;
> know that God is right there.

Tukaram locates holiness in whoever claims the afflicted as family. The psalm makes the same claim about God, and in the same direction. The judge's rising is justified by its purpose, לְהוֹשִׁיעַ, "to save." The Selah falls here and not after the terror of v. 9, which fits: the purpose is what the pause asks the listener to take in.

**Verse 11**

כִּי־חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ; שְׁאֵרִית חֵמֹת תַּחְגֹּר.

> For human fury will praise You; with the remnant of furies You gird Yourself.

This is the psalm's hardest line, and Rashi gives two readings of its second half. In both, the first half is a paradox that history has proved. The fury of the wicked makes people praise God: when their rage comes to nothing, "everyone praises Him, and they themselves praise Him." His example is Nebuchadnezzar, who threw three young men into the furnace and ended by blessing their God (Dan 3:28). For the second half he first offers a sense from Mishnaic Hebrew, where חגר means "to catch, to hold back": a nick in a slaughtering knife disqualifies it if it is deep enough "that the fingernail catches (תַּחְגֹּר) in it." So the remnant of the wicked are "held back" from showing their fury. Rashi adds that he heard that a Gaon, Rabbi Eleazar son of Isaac, used this very verse to explain that Mishnah, so the verse and the legal text each clarified the other. Then Rashi gives the plain sense, "girding": "it befits You to gird on wrath… since human wrath is nothing, all the belts of wrath are left for You."

Malbim, in his word-notes, makes the grammar do the work. תַּחְגֹּר can be third person feminine as well as second person masculine. Then its subject is חֲמַת אָדָם itself: human wrath restrains the remnant of God's wrath. Sennacherib's fury spent itself on Jerusalem, and because of it God held back and sent no further destruction on the nations. Ibn Ezra reads "gird" as "bind": the remaining furies are tied up and held in God's hand, "to be released as You please."

Other solutions remove the difficulty by changing the words. Some read אֱדֹם, "Edom," for אָדָם and חֲמָת, the Syrian city of Hamath, for "fury." The Greek, as the introduction noted, heard a festival verb in place of "gird." The Hebrew as it stands is stranger than any of these repairs, and the strangeness serves the point: the fury aimed at God ends up as something He wears, or holds, or restrains. Joseph's words to his brothers state the same principle in prose: "you intended harm to me; God intended it for good" (Gen 50:20).

**Verse 12**

נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם; כָּל־סְבִיבָיו יֹבִילוּ שַׁי לַמּוֹרָא.

> Vow and pay to the LORD your God; let all around Him bring tribute to the Awesome One.

Here, for the only time in the psalm, the covenant name יהוה appears. Everywhere else the psalm says אֱלֹהִים, as the psalms of this collection usually do. The vow formula may have resisted substitution ("when you make a vow to the LORD your God," Deut 23:22). Whatever the reason, the effect is that God is called by His own name at the moment the congregation is told what to do.

Ask who the tribute-bearers are, and the answers widen. Rashi has them already doing it: "all who hear of this salvation, and so they did," citing Isaiah's five cities in Egypt that "swear to the LORD of hosts" (Isa 19:18). Radak makes סְבִיבָיו geographical: God dwells in the land, so the nations "around the land of Israel" are "around Him." The only other two verses in the Bible with the rare word שַׁי, "tribute," both describe foreign gifts arriving at Zion: לְךָ יוֹבִילוּ מְלָכִים שָׁי, "to You kings will bring tribute" (Ps 68:30), and Isaiah's people from beyond the rivers of Cush, whose gift is brought "to the place of the name of the LORD of hosts, Mount Zion" (Isa 18:7). Minchat Shai notes a small spelling difference: our psalm writes יֹבִילוּ defectively, without the vav it has in Psalm 68.

Malbim gives לַמּוֹרָא its most pointed reading. The noun, he notes, has the מ prefix that marks a place or source. The nations had been paying tribute to Assyria out of fear. But Assyria's terror was only a share of God's, lent to it. "Why bring gifts to Assyria? Bring them to the source from which the fear came." The title "the Awesome One" answers an economic question: now that the protection racket is exposed, whom do you pay? Ibn Ezra reports a reading of מוֹרָא as a divine title and supports it with Isaiah: וְהוּא מוֹרַאֲכֶם, "and He shall be your fear" (Isa 8:13). The pairing of כָּל־סְבִיבָיו with נוֹרָא recurs in Psalm 89:8, where God is נוֹרָא עַל־כָּל־סְבִיבָיו, "awesome over all around Him," and the ones around Him are the heavenly council. Psalm 76 puts the earth's kings in the place of the council.

**Verse 13**

יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים; נוֹרָא לְמַלְכֵי־אָרֶץ.

> He cuts off the spirit of princes; He is awesome to the kings of the earth.

The verb has two meanings, and the commentators take both. Rashi hears בצר as "to lessen, withhold," from Babel: לֹא־יִבָּצֵר מֵהֶם, "nothing will be withheld from them" (Gen 11:6). God "diminishes the haughty spirit of princes." Ibn Ezra hears the vintage, בָּצִיר, and calls it a figure: God "cuts off" as a vintager cuts grape clusters. Meiri holds both open. The two readings suit each other. The builders of Babel were the first rulers of whom God said nothing would be withheld; this verse is His answer to such ambition.

The liturgical poets heard the vintage and followed it to its most violent biblical source. Both the Rosh Hashanah yotzer and the Shabbat zemer discussed above set this phrase beside Isaiah 63: מִי־זֶה בָּא מֵאֱדוֹם חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים מִבָּצְרָה, "Who is this coming from Edom, in crimsoned garments, from Bozrah?" (Isa 63:1). Bozrah, whose name is built from the same letters as יִבְצֹר, is where God treads the nations like grapes. The pairing looks like word association, but Isaiah 63 shares a surprising amount with the end of our psalm. Its God is רַב לְהוֹשִׁיעַ, "mighty to save" (compare לְהוֹשִׁיעַ, v. 10). His חֵמָה, "fury," returns again and again (compare vv. 11, 11). He is dressed in what He has trampled (compare the girding of v. 11). The name Edom is written with the consonants of אָדָם. And He works alone: וְאֵין עֹזֵר, "and there was no helper" (Isa 63:5), which is the psalm's missing army. The poets who put these lines together were reading the psalm closely.

The last word of the psalm is אָרֶץ, and its last image is kings in awe. Rubén Darío, writing to Theodore Roosevelt in 1904 on behalf of a Latin America that the United States was pressing, ends his warning the same way:

> Tened cuidado. ¡Vive la América española!
> Hay mil cachorros sueltos del León Español.
> Se necesitaría, Roosevelt, ser por Dios mismo,
> el Riflero terrible y el fuerte Cazador,
> para poder tenernos en vuestras férreas garras.
> Y, pues contáis con todo, falta una cosa: ¡Dios!
>
> Take care. Spanish America lives!
> There are a thousand cubs loose from the Spanish Lion.
> Roosevelt, one would have to be God Himself,
> the terrible Rifleman and the strong Hunter,
> to hold us in your iron claws.
> And though you count on everything, one thing is missing: God!

Darío's empire has every weapon except the one that matters. His lion-cubs and his divine hunter make the same point as the psalm's lair and its "mountains of prey": the true predator is not the superpower. Darío's defiance still speaks from one small nation to one large one. The psalm ends looking at "the kings of the earth," all of them, with no one named, as though every future Sennacherib has been told already.

### REFINED READER QUESTIONS

1. In v. 4, Rashi decides what רִשְׁפֵי means from the presence or absence of a single dot in the letter פ. What does he conclude, and what third meaning of the word does his list of parallels hold without naming?

2. Verse 7's verb נִרְדָּם is singular, though "chariot and horse" are two. How do Ibn Ezra and Radak each account for this, and how does each reading change the picture of the enemy camp?

3. The Talmud reads v. 9, "From heaven You made judgment heard; the earth feared and grew still," as the moment of Sinai. Which verse of Deuteronomy makes that reading natural, and what does the Chida find between the earth's fear and its stillness?

4. Rashi offers two meanings for תַּחְגֹּר in v. 11, one of them drawn from a law about slaughtering knives. What are they? Malbim reads the verb as feminine: what, then, is its subject, and what does the verse come to mean?

5. Why, according to Malbim, should the nations bring their tribute לַמּוֹרָא, "to the Awesome One" (v. 12), rather than to Assyria?

6. Two liturgical poets set v. 13's יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים beside Isaiah 63's warrior "from Edom… from Bozrah." What connects the verb to that passage, and what else do Isaiah 63 and the last verses of the psalm share?
