When Sennacherib of Assyria came home from his campaign of 701 BCE, he lined a room of his palace at Nineveh with carved reliefs of a siege: battering rams at the walls, archers behind wicker shields, captives impaled, families trudging into exile. The city is Lachish, not Jerusalem. His annals claim that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." They never say he took the cage. The walls of the palace show which city he could not show.

Psalm 76 is the picture from the other side of that wall, and it is a strange one. The ancient Greek translators headed it "concerning the Assyrian," and Rashi insists that "this whole psalm speaks of Sennacherib's fall, for we find no enemy falling at Jerusalem but he." Yet it is a victory song in which no battle takes place. Nobody charges. No sword is drawn on the side of Zion. There is no Israelite army and no human king. By the time the poem opens its eyes, the fighting, if there was any, is already over. What the hearer walks into is a field at dawn: broken bows, men who will not wake, horses that do not stir, and a silence the poem calls fear.

### The shape of the morning after

The psalm moves in four steps, and two of them end on a Selah, the liturgical pause. The first (vv. 2–4) places God in Jerusalem under two names: וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ בְצִיּוֹן, "His booth was in Salem, His den in Zion." It then says what He did there: שָׁמָּה שִׁבַּר רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת מָגֵן וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה, "There He broke the fiery shafts of the bow, shield and sword and war." Selah. The list starts with things you can hold and ends with a thing you cannot. Arrows, shield, sword, and then "war" itself goes onto the pile of snapped equipment. The first pause falls on that word.

The second step (vv. 5–7) turns to face God directly ("You are resplendent, mighty") and shows the enemy camp by night. The third (vv. 8–10) lifts the camera to heaven. The battlefield becomes a courtroom, the earth falls silent, and God rises "to save all the lowly of the earth." Selah again. The second pause falls on the lowly. The last step (vv. 11–13) is the aftermath: human fury turns into praise, the congregation is told to pay its vows, the surrounding peoples bring gifts, and kings tremble. The poem saves its two silences for the two things it most wants heard. War is broken, and the powerless are saved. Everything else happens between those two pauses.

### What human beings are allowed to do

The most striking thing about Psalm 76 shows up only if you ask of each verb who is doing it. Every verb of force belongs to God. He broke (שִׁבַּר). He made judgment heard (הִשְׁמַעְתָּ). He rose (קוּם) to save (לְהוֹשִׁיעַ). He girds (תַּחְגֹּר). He cuts off (יִבְצֹר). The enemy gets something else entirely. אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ, "they were despoiled," is an odd reflexive form of the root for plunder, so the plunderers become plunder by the shape of the verb itself. נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם, "they slept their sleep," is a state and not an action. נִרְדָּם, "stunned into deep sleep," is passive, and it is singular though its subject is plural: וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס, "chariot and horse." The whole cavalry lies down as one inert thing. מִי יַעֲמֹד, "who can stand?", is a question whose answer is no one. And the one transitive verb any warrior is given comes negated: וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ כָל־אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל יְדֵיהֶם, "and all the men of valor did not find their hands." It is not that they could not use them. They could not find them. The hand is the organ of war, and it has simply gone missing.

After the verdict, human beings get verbs back, and every one of them is worship: fury "will praise You" (תּוֹדֶךָּ); "vow and pay" (נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ); "let them bring tribute" (יֹבִילוּ שַׁי). In the grammar of this poem, a human being may do three things only: fail, fall silent, or give thanks. The psalm does not argue for disarmament. It enacts it in its conjugations.

Malbim, who reads the psalm as the night Sennacherib's camp died, catches something about that sleep. He notes that the warriors "were not asleep." Terror put them under, "so that he was stunned, he and his chariot and his horse, *even though it is the way of the horse to be awake at night*." In his lexical notes he makes a sharper point still: God's rebuke (גְּעָרָה) works in reverse. A human rebuke wakes a sleeper; this one sends an army into sleep. The only weapon in the poem is a single exhalation. In other psalms the same word dries up the sea.

That sea is not incidental. "Horse and chariot" is the vocabulary of the Song at the Sea (Exod 15:1, סוּס וְרֹכְבוֹ), and the psalm shares with that song the words אַדִּיר, "mighty," and נוֹרָא, "awesome." But the Song at the Sea names God יְהוָה אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, "the LORD is a man of war." Psalm 76 has Him break מִלְחָמָה. The Song moves from victory toward the sanctuary ("the place You made to dwell in"). This psalm begins at the sanctuary and works backward to the victory. It reads like the Exodus told by someone who already lives in Jerusalem and has concluded that the point of the warrior God is the end of warfare.

The same move shows in a line from Hosea that the psalm borrows almost word for word. Hosea's covenant of peace promises וְקֶשֶׁת וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה אֶשְׁבּוֹר, "bow and sword and war I will break," and goes on: וְהִשְׁכַּבְתִּים לָבֶטַח, "and I will make them lie down in safety." In Hosea, disarmament lets Israel sleep. Psalm 76 keeps the formula and the sleep but moves the sleeper. Here it is the aggressor who lies down and does not wake.

### The lion's den

The imagery around Zion is not gentle. סֻכּוֹ can be a festival booth, and in Jeremiah 25:38 it is also the covert a young lion leaves when it goes out to kill. מְעוֹנָה is a dwelling, and in Nahum 2:12 it is מְעוֹן אֲרָיוֹת, the lions' den of Nineveh. The psalm confirms the reading when it calls God אַדִּיר מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף, "mighty from the mountains of prey." The Song of Songs uses the same pairing, "from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards" (מִמְּעֹנוֹת אֲרָיוֹת מֵהַרְרֵי נְמֵרִים). The Temple, in this poem, is a predator's lair.

What gives the image its moral edge is where else in the Psalter a lion crouches in its covert. In Psalm 10:9 the wicked man "lies in wait like a lion in his covert" (כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה) "to seize the poor." That psalm begs God to arise, to rebuke, to strike terror into the nations, not to forget the lowly. Psalm 76 reads as the answer. There is one lair in the world whose lion hunts the hunters, and from it God rises "to save all the lowly of the earth." Nahum has a further detail. The only other place in the Bible where the form נָמוּ, "they slumbered," appears is Nahum 3:18: נָמוּ רֹעֶיךָ מֶלֶךְ אַשּׁוּר יִשְׁכְּנוּ אַדִּירֶיךָ, "your shepherds slumber, O king of Assyria; your mighty ones lie still." Psalm 76 gives Assyria's word "mighty" (אַדִּיר) to God and leaves Assyria only the slumber.

### The other side of the story

The Egyptians remembered the same campaign differently, and their version is uncannily close. Herodotus, told the story by priests at Memphis, says that when "Sanacharib, king of the Arabians and Assyrians" marched on Egypt, a priest-king prayed in the temple and was promised help. At Pelusium, in the night, a swarm of field mice gnawed through the Assyrians' quivers, their bowstrings, and the handles of their shields, and in the morning the army fled unarmed. A statue in the temple, Herodotus adds, showed the king holding a mouse. Two peoples, two gods, one memory: an empire's weapons unmade overnight without a blow struck. The psalm's list, bow, shield, and then war, is the Hebrew form of that memory, with the mice removed and the agency given back to a single breath.

Byron, in 1815, heard the breath:

> For the Angel of Death spread his wings on the blast,
> And breathed in the face of the foe as he pass'd;
> And the eyes of the sleepers wax'd deadly and chill,
> And their hearts but once heaved, and for ever grew still!

His sleepers and his stillness are the psalm's נָמוּ and שָׁקָטָה, and his "might of the Gentile, unsmote by the sword" is the psalm's absent battle. But Byron's poem ends on spectacle: the rider distorted and pale, the widows wailing at Ashur. It lingers over the dead. The psalm spends two lines on the sleeping army and then looks away. It climbs to heaven, where the event turns out to have been a trial: מִשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּ דִּין אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה, "From heaven You made judgment heard; the earth feared and was still." The Talmud (Shabbat 88a) puts a question to this verse through Hezekiah, of all people: if the earth feared, how was it still, and if it was still, why did it fear? The answer: "At first it feared, and in the end it was still." That is the arc of the whole poem, compressed into two verbs. Terror first, then a quiet that is almost rest.

### Who was saved?

Now look for the word "us." It is not there. Psalm 76 has no "I," no "we," no "our" anywhere. Its sister Zion song, Psalm 46, uses the same epithet and joins it to a possessive: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת עִמָּנוּ מִשְׂגָּב־לָנוּ אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "the LORD of hosts is with us, a stronghold for us, the God of Jacob." Psalm 76 says מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "at Your rebuke, God of Jacob," and never claims Him. When the verdict names its beneficiaries they are כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, "all the lowly of the earth," not of the land. The commentators notice the gap and fill it. Rashi makes the saved "Hezekiah and his company," and Radak explains that Israel are "the lowly of the earth." They are supplying exactly what the poem holds back. It has room for Jerusalem's deliverance, but it will not let that deliverance belong to Jerusalem alone.

The poem's time is held back in the same way. Its verbs are past through verse 10 and future from verse 11 on. Radak first read the psalm as Sennacherib's defeat, then changed his mind and read it as the future war of Gog. To do so he had to gloss verse 3's plain past וַיְהִי, "it was," as "and then it will be." The disagreement among the commentators is built into the grammar. The psalm remembers one night so that it can promise a pattern.

Here is what it is like from the inside. To be one of the lowly is to know that you cannot win a fight. You do not have the chariot, the horse, the hands. This poem asks nothing of you in the fight. It does not tell you to be brave. It tells you that the battle was a trial, that the judge stood up, and that He stood up for you. What is left for you to do is to bring a gift.

### Salem

The psalm never says "Jerusalem." It calls the city by its oldest name, שָׁלֵם, the Salem of Melchizedek, and the Greek translators heard the word inside it and simply wrote "in peace." God's lair is in Peace, and Peace is where war is broken. The final summons, נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ, "vow, and pay," returns the same root as an answer: shalom is repaid at Shalem. Radak recalls the midrash that Shem named the place Shalem and Abraham named it Yir'eh, and God, unwilling to set aside either, joined them into Yerushalayim. The Chida notes that in this verse the yir'eh half has fallen away and only Shalem remains. The poem restores the missing half as it goes, through the root of fear (יָרְאָה, "the earth feared"; לַמּוֹרָא, "to the Awesome One"; נוֹרָא, "awesome to the kings of the earth"), until the city's full name has been spelled out across twelve verses: peace at the beginning, awe at the end.

Zechariah imagines the day when "all who are left of the nations that came against Jerusalem" will go up each year "to keep the festival of Booths." The Greek of our verse 11 may have heard the same thing: the remnant of fury "shall keep festival to You." That is why this psalm is sung on Sukkot. The one who lives in the booth is the one who breaks the bow. The warriors who could not find their hands come to Salem carrying tribute, and the psalm leaves them there.

#### Full psalm

Psalm 76 is the Psalm of the Day for the first day of Sukkot in the Edot HaMizrach rite and in communities that follow the custom of the Vilna Gaon. The obvious reason is a word in v. 3, וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ, "His booth was in Salem." The fit goes deeper than the word. Sukkot is the festival at which, in Zechariah's vision, the survivors of the nations that attacked Jerusalem come up every year to keep the Feast of Booths, and that chapter is read as the haftarah of the festival's first day. The psalm's ending, with the peoples around God bringing tribute, is the same scene. Some Ashkenazi and Hasidic communities also recite the psalm on the seventh day of Passover, the day the Song at the Sea is read in the Torah. The link there is v. 7's "chariot and horse" stunned into sleep, which recalls Pharaoh's cavalry.

#### Key verses

The opening line, נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ, "God is known in Judah; in Israel His name is great" (v. 2), appears twice in Nusach Sefard, the Hasidic rite. On Simchat Torah it is part of the chain of verses recited during the hakafot, the processions with the Torah scrolls. It comes just before the fifth circuit, after a plea that God "rebuild the Temple speedily in our days" and restore "the voice of the Levite… with harp and lyre." A psalm about a sanctuary becomes a line chanted while the congregation circles its own sanctuary. The same words, recast in the second person, open an alphabetical selichah for the first Monday of the pre–Rosh Hashanah penitential season: אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל נוֹדָעְתָּ, אַתָּה יְהוָה אָבִינוּ אָתָּה, "God, great in Israel, You have made Yourself known; You, LORD, are our Father." The psalm's boast becomes the basis of a confession: You have been known among us, and we have not deserved it (לֹא בְצִדְקוֹתֵינוּ, "not by our righteousness").

In v. 8, וּמִי־יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ, "who can stand before You?", is taken into Abraham ibn Ezra's piyyut *Lecha Eli Teshukati*, recited on the eve of Yom Kippur in the Edot HaMizrach rite: וּמִי יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ וּמִי יִהְיֶה תְמוּרָתִי, "who can stand before You, and who will stand in my place?" The line leads straight into the alphabetical confession (אָשַׁמְתִּי וְאָרַבְתִּי וּבָגַדְתִּי, "I have trespassed, lain in wait, betrayed…"). The psalm's question was asked of a defeated army. The liturgy puts it to the worshipper.

The last verse's יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "He cuts off the spirit of princes" (v. 13), appears in the Ashkenazi Rosh Hashanah Shacharit, in the yotzer piyyut *Melekh Azur Gevurah*: מֶלֶךְ חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים, בְּדָרְכוֹ בוֹגְדִים, יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "the King in crimsoned garments, as He treads down the treacherous, cuts off the spirit of princes." A Nusach Sefard Shabbat zemer sets the same phrase in a stanza built from Isaiah 63: בְּבוֹאוֹ מֵאֱדוֹם חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים… בְּכֹחוֹ הַגָּדוֹל יִבְצוֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "when He comes from Edom in crimsoned garments… in His great power He cuts off the spirit of princes." Both poets heard the grape-harvest in the verb. The note on v. 13 takes this up.

#### Practical Kabbalah

Shimush Tehillim prescribes the psalm "to be saved from fire and from water" (להנצל מאש וממים). The language of the psalm explains both. Fire comes from v. 4, where God breaks רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת, the "flames" (or "Reshephs") "of the bow." Water comes from v. 7's גְּעָרָה, the divine "rebuke," which in almost every other biblical use is the blast that drives back the sea (Psalms 104:7, 106:9; Nahum 1:4). A psalm whose two mythic words are a broken fire-bolt and a sea-rebuke became a charm against flame and flood. Later manuals also recommend it to travelers menaced by robbers, in the hope that attackers, like the warriors of v. 6, will "not find their hands."
