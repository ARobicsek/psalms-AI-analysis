#### Full psalm

Psalm 76 is recited as the psalm of the day for the first day of Sukkot in the Edot HaMizrach rite and in the custom of the Vilna Gaon. The usual explanation is the word in v. 3: וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ, "His booth was in Salem." On the festival when every Jew moves into a flimsy hut, the psalm reminds the worshiper that God keeps a sukkah of His own, and that His is the one that does not fall. The fit runs deeper than a single word. Sukkot is the festival of the nations. Its seventy bulls were understood as offerings for the seventy peoples of the world, and its prophetic readings imagine the nations streaming up to Jerusalem (Zech 14:16, the first day's haftarah). The psalm follows the same path, from God's booth in Zion to "all around Him" bringing gifts (v. 12). Some Ashkenazi and Hasidic communities also say the psalm on the seventh day of Passover, the day the Song of the Sea is read, because v. 7 has "chariot and horse" falling still at God's rebuke.

#### Key verses

v. 2, נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ, "Known in Judah is God; in Israel great is His name," appears in the Nusach Sefard (Hasidic) Simchat Torah hakafot. It is set in a chain of verses just before the fifth circuit, after a plea that God rebuild the Temple and restore the Levites' song. As the congregation dances in circles with the Torah, the verse becomes a statement about the people doing the dancing: this is where God's name is great.

The same phrase, reordered and made into direct address, opens an alphabetic selichah for the first Monday of the pre–Rosh Hashanah Selichot in Nusach Sefard: אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל נוֹדָעְתָּ, "O God, great in Israel, You have made Yourself known." The psalm's third-person announcement turns into the ground of a plea. God has shown who He is in history, and the petitioners then list how they have fallen short.

v. 8, וּמִי יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ, "who can stand before You?", is taken into Avraham ibn Ezra's piyyut *Lecha Eli Teshukati*, sung on the night of Yom Kippur in the Edot HaMizrach rite: וְשִׁמְעָךְ עֵת שְׁמַעְתִּיהוּ מְאֹד זַעְתִּי וְיָרֵאתִי: וּמִי יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ וּמִי יִהְיֶה תְמוּרָתִי, "When I heard the report of You I trembled greatly and was afraid: who can stand before You, and who will stand in my place?" The confession, אָשַׁמְתִּי, follows at once. In the psalm the question belongs to God's enemies. In the piyyut it belongs to the worshiper, who has no one to send in his stead.

v. 13, יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "He clips the spirit of princes," appears in two poems, and both set it beside Isaiah's warrior coming from Edom in blood-red garments. The Ashkenazi Rosh Hashanah yotzer *Melekh Azur Gevurah* reads: מֶלֶךְ חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים. בְּדָרְכוֹ בוֹגְדִים. יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "King in crimsoned garments, as He treads down the treacherous, He clips the spirit of princes." A Shabbat table hymn printed in Nusach Sefard siddurim goes further: בְּבוֹאוֹ מֵאֱדוֹם חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים, זֶבַח לוֹ בְּבָצְרָה… בְּכֹחוֹ הַגָּדוֹל יִבְצוֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "When He comes from Edom in crimsoned garments, a slaughter His in Bozrah… in His great strength He clips the spirit of princes." The hymn names Bozrah outright. The poet heard בָּצְרָה, "Bozrah," inside יִבְצֹר.

#### Practical Kabbalah

Shimush Tehillim prescribes the psalm "to be rescued from fire and from water" (לְהִנָּצֵל מֵאֵשׁ וּמִמַּיִם). The fire can be traced to רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת in v. 4, the flaming shafts that God snaps. The water most likely comes from גְּעָרָה, "rebuke," in v. 7, a word the Bible uses mostly for God's roar at the sea (Ps 104:7; 106:9; Nah 1:4). The only other occurrence of the exact form מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ, Ps 18:16, lays bare "the channels of the waters." Read this way, the psalm's two words of cosmic combat supplied the amulet's two dangers.

### VERSE COMMENTARY

**Verse 1**

לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת, מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף שִׁיר.

> For the conductor, with stringed instruments; a psalm of Asaph, a song.

The heading is routine, but one detail connects it to the poem that follows. Outside the Psalter, the pairing לַמְנַצֵּחַ with נְגִינוֹת appears only at the end of Habakkuk's prayer: לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינוֹתָי, "for the conductor, on my strings" (Hab 3:19). That prayer is the Bible's fullest portrait of the divine warrior. God comes from Teman with radiance and horns of light, and וְיֵצֵא רֶשֶׁף לְרַגְלָיו, "Resheph goes out at His feet" (Hab 3:5). The same plague-archer whose arrows are broken in v. 4 walks at the heel of Habakkuk's God. The two poems come from a shared musical and theological world.

The word נְגִינָה has a second meaning: a mocking song. "I have become a laughingstock to all my people, their taunt-song (נְגִינָתָם) all day long" (Lam 3:14); drunkards make a נְגִינָה of the sufferer (Ps 69:13). Nothing forces that meaning here. Still, a psalm that ends with princes cut back like vines, accompanied by plucked strings, carries a trace of the victor's song about the vanquished. The double label מִזְמוֹר… שִׁיר, "psalm… song," comes in the opposite order in the Asaph collection's last psalm (שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף, Ps 83:1), and Psalm 83 is also a psalm about a coalition of nations against Israel.

**Verse 2**

נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים; בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ.

> Known in Judah is God; in Israel His name is great.

Malbim's lexical notes find a step down between the two halves. To be "known" (נוֹדָע) is to be encountered in one's essence, and this belongs to Judah, "where the Shekhinah is." A "name" (שֵׁם) is reputation, being spoken of, which is a lesser degree of presence, and this belongs to Israel, the northern tribes. On his reading the parallel lines are unequal: Judah has the thing itself, Israel has its fame. The Asaphite psalms, with their northern roots, may have heard the line more generously, as a union of two names. But Malbim catches something the translation smooths away. The verse moves from presence to report, and the rest of the psalm travels the same road outward, until the "name" reaches "the kings of the earth."

A midrash quoted by the Alshich takes the verse in a darker direction. When the ten tribes were exiled and Judah was not, the nations said God was showing favor to the people who hosted His house. When Judah was exiled too, "God made His faithfulness known in the world." Here God becomes "known in Judah" through a judgment passed on Judah itself, and so "great in Israel" because He plays no favorites. It is an unsettling homily for a victory psalm: the knowledge of God begins with a sentence against His own city.

The opening pair of roots, "known" and "great," appears elsewhere in poems about nations assaulting Zion. Psalm 48 opens with גָּדוֹל יְהוָה, "great is the LORD," and then says אֱלֹהִים בְּאַרְמְנוֹתֶיהָ נוֹדַע לְמִשְׂגָּב, "God in her palaces has made Himself known as a stronghold" (48:2–4). Ezekiel's oracle against Gog closes with the same two roots: וְהִתְגַּדִּלְתִּי… וְנוֹדַעְתִּי לְעֵינֵי גּוֹיִם רַבִּים, "I will make Myself great… and make Myself known in the eyes of many nations" (Ezek 38:23). When Radak later reread the whole psalm as the war of Gog, he gave no lexical reason, but one was available in this first line.

Listen to the first word beside the last divine title in the psalm: נוֹדָע, `nō-DHĀʿ`, "known," and נוֹרָא, `nō-RĀ'`, "awesome" (v. 13). The two words differ by little more than one consonant. The God "known" in the first line is the God "feared" in the last.

**Verse 3**

וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ, וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ בְצִיּוֹן.

> His covert was in Salem, and His den in Zion.

The psalm never says "Jerusalem." It uses the old name שָׁלֵם, "Salem," the city of Melchizedek (Gen 14:18). Radak, commenting on this verse, cites the midrash that explains the full name. Shem called the place Shalem; Abraham called it יִרְאֶה, "He will see" (Gen 22:14). God, unwilling to set aside either righteous man, joined the two into יְרוּשָׁלַיִם. The Chida draws the conclusion for our verse: Jerusalem is "Yir'eh–Shalem," and here the Yir'eh has been removed and only Shalem remains. Where did it go? Consonantally, the letters יראה return in v. 9 (אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה, "the earth feared"), and the root of fear runs through the psalm to its last line. The two words come from different roots, "see" and "fear," so this is wordplay, not etymology. Still, the poem opens with half the city's name and spends the rest of its length supplying the other half.

The dwelling words are words for an animal's hiding place. סֻכּוֹ can mean a festival booth, but when Jeremiah says of God עָזַב כַּכְּפִיר סֻכּוֹ, "like a young lion He has left His covert" (Jer 25:38), it is a lair. Job's lions ambush בַסֻּכָּה, "in the thicket" (Job 38:40). And מְעוֹנָה is the den of Nahum's lions: אַיֵּה מְעוֹן אֲרָיוֹת, "Where now is the lions' den?" (Nah 2:12), Nahum's taunt over Assyrian Nineveh. The sharpest parallel is Psalm 10:9, where the wicked man lies in wait כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה, "like a lion in its covert," to seize the עָנִי, "the poor." Everywhere else in these texts, the lion in its thicket preys on the weak. Psalm 76 describes one lair whose lion hunts the predators and rises "to save all the lowly of the earth" (v. 10).

Malbim keeps both meanings of סֻכָּה, and he divides them. A sukkah is a temporary dwelling, and it belongs in Jerusalem, "where the masses lived." The מָעוֹן is permanent and belongs in Zion, seat of king, Sanhedrin and priests. God camps among the crowds and resides at the center.

The Masoretes add a detail. Minchat Shai reports their note that this word is pronounced the same way three times in the Bible and spelled three ways: סֻכּוֹ in Jeremiah's lion leaving his lair, סוּכּוֹ with a vav here, and שֻׂכּוֹ in Lamentations 2:6, where God "has laid waste His booth like a garden." The Masoretes grouped them only for spelling. Read in order, though, they tell a story in three words: God's covert in Zion, the lion leaving it, the booth torn down.

Ted Hughes's caged jaguar is the modern image of a predator whose enclosure does not contain him:

> By the bang of blood in the brain deaf the ear—
> He spins from the bars, but there's no cage to him
> More than to the visionary his cell:
> His stride is wildernesses of freedom:
> The world rolls under the long thrust of his heel.

Hughes gives the animal a cage and then denies that it holds him. The psalm does something similar with a sacred address. God has an address, Salem, Zion, and the vocabulary of lairs warns that residence does not mean domestication. The difference is in the direction. Hughes's jaguar is pure force with no object. The psalm's lion has a verdict to deliver and a people to protect.

**Verse 4**

שָׁמָּה שִׁבַּר רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת, מָגֵן וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה. סֶלָה.

> There He broke the flaming shafts of the bow, shield and sword and war. Selah.

Rashi argues about a single dot. Is רִשְׁפֵי related to רִשְׁפֵּי אֵשׁ, "flashes of fire" (Song 8:6)? No, he says, for two reasons. Sparks do not come from a bow. And the letter פ here is soft (רִשְׁפֵי) while in the Song it carries a dagesh (רִשְׁפֵּי). He relates the word instead to וּלְחֻמֵי רֶשֶׁף (Deut 32:24), which Onkelos renders "birds," and to בְּנֵי רֶשֶׁף, which "fly upward" (Job 5:7). These he takes to be flying things, winged demons. So רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת are "the things the bow makes fly," arrows. Without meaning to, Rashi has kept the demonic figure of Resheph in the word while arguing that it simply means arrows. Meiri retains the fire: arrows are called flames "because in their speed they glow as if they were coals," as a blade is called לַהַב, "flame."

The Alshich reads the verse against the grain of every other commentator, as an account of the Temple's destruction. God saw that if His anger fell on Israel itself, no remnant would survive. So He turned it on the stones of Zion. שָׁמָּה, "there," in His own house, He broke the arrows of wrath. Zion served as a מָגֵן, a shield taking the blows meant for its people. Even so, he adds, "sword and war" remained for later generations. In this reading the weapons God breaks are His own, and the place where they break is His sanctuary. It is a homily of the exile. It turns the song of the city that did not fall into a song for the city that did.

The line was sung in battle. In the Scottish metrical psalter of 1650 it runs:

> In Judah's land God is well known,
> his name's in Israel great:
> In Salem is his tabernacle,
> in Sion is his seat.
> There arrows of the bow he brake,
> the shield, the sword, the war.

Presbyterian tradition, and Walter Scott after it in *Old Mortality*, has the Covenanters singing this psalm as the government dragoons advanced at Drumclog in 1679. Men carrying weapons sang about God breaking weapons, and they meant God would break the other side's. It is a fair question how the verse's last word, "war," sounds in the mouths of men about to wage one.

The Italian Futurist Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, writing the siege of Adrianople in 1914, gives the opposite approach:

> ogni 5 secondi cannoni da assedio sventrare
> spazio con un accordo TAM-TUUUMB
> ammutinamento di 500 echi per azzannarlo
> sminuzzarlo sparpagliarlo all'infinito
>
> every 5 seconds siege cannons gutting
> space with a TAM-TUUUMB chord
> mutiny of 500 echoes to tear it with teeth
> mince it scatter it to infinity

Marinetti gives the guns the whole page, in capitals, in noise and in echoes without end. The psalm gives its arsenal four quiet nouns joined by "and," then a Selah. Both poets feel the grandeur of weapons, and they place it in opposite things: Marinetti in the noise, the psalmist in the silence after it.

**Verse 5**

נָאוֹר אַתָּה, אַדִּיר מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף.

> Radiant are You, majestic from the mountains of prey.

The first word is a puzzle, and the commentators split three ways. Ibn Ezra reads it as "full of light." Of everything that can be seen, he says, only light is glorious without being a body. That makes it the natural image for God. Rashi reads it from a different root, נאר, "to spurn, lay waste," as in נִאֵר מִקְדָּשׁוֹ, "He spurned His sanctuary" (Lam 2:7). So נָאוֹר is "the Destroyer," a title from God's deeds, like חַנּוּן, "Gracious," or קַנּוֹא, "Jealous." The Alshich reports that the Targum renders the word דְּחִיל, "fearsome," which is exactly what modern scholars propose when they emend נָאוֹר to נוֹרָא, the word of v. 8 and v. 13. The ancient Aramaic translator either read or heard the psalm's governing word of awe here. Malbim offers the most dramatic reading. Since Sennacherib's army fell in the night, the psalmist calls God "Radiant," as if to say that the dark was not dark to You when You went out to the enemy camp.

Then comes מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף, "from the mountains of prey," a phrase the Greek replaced with "the everlasting mountains" (ὀρέων αἰωνίων, suggesting הַרְרֵי עַד, as in Hab 3:6). The Hebrew is stranger, and a small fact suggests it is right. The form מֵהַרְרֵי occurs only three times in the Bible, and one of them sets it in exactly this psalm's pairing: מִמְּעֹנוֹת אֲרָיוֹת מֵהַרְרֵי נְמֵרִים, "from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards" (Song 4:8). A den (מְעוֹנָה, v. 3) followed by predator mountains (v. 5) is a real biblical collocation. The Song's lover calls his bride down from the haunts of big cats, and the psalm's God comes down from them. (The third occurrence, מֵהַרְרֵי קֶדֶם, "from the ancient mountains," Num 23:7, shows that the "everlasting" kind existed too, so the matter is not closed.) Isaiah describes the scene the phrase evokes: "As a lion growls over its prey… so the LORD of Hosts will come down to fight upon Mount Zion" (Isa 31:4). Rashi goes his own way. The "mountains of prey" are the enemy's giants, "tall as mountains," whose might counts for nothing before God. So the mountains may be the lion's home or the lion's quarry.

**Verse 6**

אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ אַבִּירֵי לֵב, נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם; וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ כָל־אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל יְדֵיהֶם.

> The stout of heart were stripped; they slumbered their sleep, and none of the men of might could find their hands.

The introduction dealt with the plunderers turned to spoil and the lost hands. Rashi hears a different verb in אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ. He derives it from מוֹלִיךְ יוֹעֲצִים שׁוֹלָל, "He leads counselors away שׁוֹלָל" (Job 12:17), meaning out of their wits. So "the stout-hearted went mad," wandering "like fools who err." Meiri adds a moral note: they came to see themselves as having erred in attacking Israel. In one reading the enemy loses his goods, in the other his mind. Both fit a poem where the enemy loses everything, down to his hands.

The form נָמוּ, "they slumbered," occurs in only one other place in the Bible, and it is addressed to an Assyrian king: נָמוּ רֹעֶיךָ מֶלֶךְ אַשּׁוּר, יִשְׁכְּנוּ אַדִּירֶיךָ, "Your shepherds slumber, O king of Assyria; your nobles lie still" (Nah 3:18). Nahum gives Assyria's sleeping nobles the title אַדִּירִים. Psalm 76 gives אַדִּיר to God in the verse before and leaves the enemy only the sleep. And the God who does not sleep appears elsewhere in exactly these terms: הִנֵּה לֹא־יָנוּם וְלֹא יִישָׁן שׁוֹמֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Behold, the Guardian of Israel neither slumbers nor sleeps" (Ps 121:4). Meiri makes their sleep the last one: נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם is Jeremiah's וְיָשְׁנוּ שְׁנַת־עוֹלָם, "they shall sleep an everlasting sleep" (Jer 51:39), said of Babylon's warriors.

The enemies' title, אַבִּירֵי לֵב, has another echo. In Jacob's blessing of Joseph, Joseph's bow stays firm and "the arms of his hands" are made strong מִידֵי אֲבִיר יַעֲקֹב, "by the hands of the Mighty One of Jacob" (Gen 49:24). The Masoretes point the divine title (אֲבִיר) differently from the human one (אַבִּיר), so the link is in the consonants only. But the psalm has just broken the bow, now takes away the hands, and in the next verse calls God "God of Jacob." Joseph's blessing of bow, hands and Mighty One is undone, term by term, for the people who came against Zion.

Lorenzo da Ponte's libretto for *Don Giovanni* (1787) stages the moment when a brave man's hand fails him. The stone statue of the murdered Commendatore asks for the libertine's hand:

> COMMENDATORE: Dammi la mano in pegno!
> DON GIOVANNI: Eccola! ... Ohimè!
> Che gelo è questo mai!
>
> COMMENDATORE: Give me your hand as a pledge!
> DON GIOVANNI: Here it is! ... Alas!
> What ice is this!

Giovanni's defiance ("No, no, ch'io non mi pento," "No, I will not repent") stays whole to the end. What fails is the hand. The psalm's "men of might" are like him: their courage is described as a matter of the heart, אַבִּירֵי לֵב, and they are undone at the body's extremities. The opera gives the moment a gripping statue and a burst of fire. The psalm gives no visible cause at all, only that the hands were not there to be found.

**Verse 7**

מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ, אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, נִרְדָּם וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס.

> At Your rebuke, God of Jacob, both chariot and horse sank into deep sleep.

The grammar is odd. The verb נִרְדָּם, "sank into deep sleep," is singular, and it comes before a compound subject whose first element carries an unexplained "and," וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס, literally "slept, and chariot and horse." Rashi calls the first vav "superfluous" and compares אַיָּה וַעֲנָה in the Edomite genealogy (Gen 36:24), where a stray vav also stands in front of a name. Radak finds a missing subject instead. The one who "slept" is the king himself, the head of the stout-hearted of v. 6 (for Radak, Gog), "and chariot and horse" slept too. Ibn Ezra makes each soldier the subject, one by one. The broken grammar leaves the sleeper undefined, as if the stupor spread faster than the sentence could name who was falling.

The noun behind נִרְדָּם is תַּרְדֵּמָה, God-given deep sleep. Adam sleeps it so a rib can be taken (Gen 2:21), and Abram sleeps it before a vision (Gen 15:12). One use is especially close. David walks into Saul's sleeping camp and carries off the king's own spear, "and no one saw, no one knew, no one woke, for they were all asleep, כִּי תַּרְדֵּמַת יְהוָה נָפְלָה עֲלֵיהֶם, for a deep sleep from the LORD had fallen on them" (1 Sam 26:12). That is the psalm's scene in narrative form: an army lying helpless, its weapons within reach of anyone, and not one man stirring.

The other occurrence of the exact form מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ, Ps 18:16, lays bare the channels of the sea "at Your rebuke, at the blast of the breath of Your nostrils." Ibn Ezra explains the epithet "God of Jacob" as a return to v. 2's "Israel." After the name at the start, the psalm calls God by the patriarch's name at the point of the rebuke.

Yannai, the sixth-century liturgical poet, built a whole poem on the idea that God's great blows fall at night while the enemy sleeps. Its lines are now sung at the Passover seder:

> טִיסַת נְגִיד חֲרֹשֶׁת סִלִּיתָ בְּכוֹכְבֵי לַיְלָה…
> יָעַץ מְחָרֵף לְנוֹפֵף אִוּוּי, הוֹבַשְׁתָּ פְגָרָיו בַּלַּיְלָה…
> וַיְהִי בַּחֲצִי הַלַּיְלָה.
>
> The host of the prince of Harosheth You swept away by the stars of night…
> The blasphemer plotted to shake his fist at the Beloved City; You withered his corpses in the night…
> And it came to pass at midnight.

The first line is Sisera, the נְגִיד, "prince," against whom "the stars fought from their courses" (Judg 5:20). The second is Sennacherib. Yannai places our psalm's night in a long line of nights. What he adds is the refrain: every one of these victories, repeated over and over, "at midnight." What the psalm adds is the cause. Yannai's God acts in the dark. The psalm's God only speaks, and the chariots fall asleep.

**Verse 8**

אַתָּה נוֹרָא אַתָּה, וּמִי־יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ מֵאָז אַפֶּךָ.

> You, You are awesome, and who can stand before You once Your anger is roused?

Ibn Ezra, as a grammarian, notices the second אַתָּה. One would have been enough, he says, and he points to a similar doubling: ה׳ מָעוֹן אַתָּה הָיִיתָ לָּנוּ, "Lord, You have been our dwelling place" (Ps 90:1). The extra pronoun adds nothing to the meaning. It adds breath, the speaker saying "You" twice because once did not feel sufficient.

Malbim's reading of מֵאָז אַפֶּךָ, "once Your anger," is strikingly original. He identifies the "anger" with Assyria itself, following Isaiah: הוֹי אַשּׁוּר שֵׁבֶט אַפִּי, "Woe, Assyria, rod of My anger" (Isa 10:5). "From the time the LORD sent His anger out upon the earth, no kingdom could stand before it." On this reading v. 8 describes the Assyrian conquest: no one could withstand God's rod. Then v. 9 turns, when God speaks from heaven against the rod itself. The "who can stand?" is asked first of Assyria's victims, and then answered against Assyria. Rashi keeps the plain sense: מֵאָז means "from the moment You are angry." The same מֵאָז appears in the neighboring royal psalm, נָכוֹן כִּסְאֲךָ מֵאָז, "Your throne is established from of old" (Ps 93:2), which allows a quieter hearing: "who has stood before You, from of old, in Your anger?"

The question is a formula prophets use at the brink of judgment: לִפְנֵי זַעְמוֹ מִי יַעֲמוֹד, "before His indignation who can stand?" (Nah 1:6). Avraham ibn Ezra's Yom Kippur piyyut (see the liturgical section) turns it inward. The psalm's question is about enemies, and the piyyut's is about a sinner who has no one to send in his place. Grammatically, the verb "stand" sets up the next verses. No one can עָמַד, "stand," before God, but God can קוּם, "rise" (v. 10). The judge rises and no one else stays on his feet.

**Verse 9**

מִשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּ דִּין; אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה.

> From heaven You made judgment heard; the earth feared and grew still.

The Talmud reads this verse as Sinai (b. Shabbat 88a; b. Avodah Zarah 3a). Resh Lakish taught that at creation God set a condition: if Israel accepted the Torah, the world would endure; if not, it would return to chaos. Hezekiah the Amora supplied the verse. The earth "feared," since its existence depended on the answer, and then "grew still" when Israel said "we will do." The reading is less free than it looks. Deuteronomy's account of Sinai uses the same verb, the same direction and the same pair of heaven and earth: מִן־הַשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמִיעֲךָ אֶת־קֹלוֹ… וְעַל־הָאָרֶץ, "from heaven He made you hear His voice… and upon the earth" (Deut 4:36). Two verses earlier the same chapter speaks of God's מוֹרָאִים גְּדֹלִים, "great terrors" (Deut 4:34), the word that becomes God's title in v. 12. The rabbis heard in this verse the sound of the lawgiving, and the psalm's move from verdict to awe follows Deuteronomy's order.

The Hebrew sounds heaven and hearing together: מִשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּ, `mish-shā-MA-yim hish-MAʿ-tā`. What comes "from heaven" is something heard.

"The earth grew still" repeats half of a known formula. Joshua's conquest narrative closes with וְהָאָרֶץ שָׁקְטָה מִמִּלְחָמָה, "and the land had rest from war" (Josh 14:15). The formula's last word, מִלְחָמָה, was the climax of v. 4, where God broke war. Its first words return here. The psalm has split the idiom across five verses and changed what it means. In Joshua, the land rests after Israel's war. Here the whole earth falls silent after God has broken war itself. The commentators divide along this ambiguity. Rashi's אֶרֶץ is the land of Israel, which had feared Sennacherib and now breathed again. Radak's is "the land of the nations," which feared and stopped making war on Israel. Ibn Ezra divides the verbs: the wicked "feared," the righteous "were at rest."

Octavio Paz, in a poem from the 1940s, pictures a silence with a shape:

> Así como del fondo de la música
> brota una nota
> que mientras vibra crece y se adelgaza
> hasta que en otra música enmudece,
> brota del fondo del silencio
> otro silencio, aguda torre, espada…
>
> Just as from the depths of music
> a note springs forth
> that while it vibrates grows and thins
> until it falls silent in another music,
> springs from the depths of silence
> another silence, a sharp tower, a sword…

Paz's silence is a presence, sharp enough to be a sword. The psalm's שָׁקְטָה is like this: the quiet that follows a verdict, not the quiet of a meadow. The difference is what each silence leads to. Paz's leads only to more silence. The psalm's leads to the next verse, where the verdict's purpose is spoken.

**Verse 10**

בְּקוּם־לַמִּשְׁפָּט אֱלֹהִים, לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ. סֶלָה.

> When God rose for judgment, to save all the lowly of the earth. Selah.

Malbim links this verse back to the last one as its reason. Why did the earth, which had been terrified, grow still once God came to judge? Because "divine judgment never comes to lend strength to the tyrant who oppresses and robs, only to save the lowly crushed beneath his hand." The earth heard that the Judge was coming, and relief followed the fear, because it knew whose side this Judge takes. On this reading the silence of v. 9 is the relief of people who have heard a verdict in their favor.

The phrase עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ belongs to a small group of texts about the humble in God's final justice. Zephaniah calls on them before the day of wrath: בַּקְּשׁוּ אֶת־יְהוָה כָּל־עַנְוֵי הָאָרֶץ, "Seek the LORD, all you humble of the earth" (Zeph 2:3). Isaiah's future king will decide בְּמִישׁוֹר לְעַנְוֵי־אָרֶץ, "with equity for the humble of the earth," and "with the breath of his lips slay the wicked" (Isa 11:4). That verse has a judge, the lowly, and a lethal breath together, as in vv. 7–10 here. The lament of Psalms 9–10 had asked for exactly this: קוּמָה יְהוָה, "Arise, LORD" (9:20), a prayer that the hope of the lowly not perish (9:19), and שִׁיתָה יְהוָה מוֹרָה לָהֶם, "set terror over them" (9:21). Psalm 76 reads like the report of that prayer answered: God rose, the lowly were saved, and terror (מוֹרָא) arrived.

The Alshich, as usual, reads through the long exile. Israel, lowliest among the nations, are the עַנְוֵי אֶרֶץ. God's judgment on Zion in the present keeps a remnant alive, "so that all the lowly of the earth, in every generation," may be saved. For him, "all" stretches across time rather than across peoples. The plain text leaves "all" unrestricted.

The seventeenth-century Marathi poet-saint Tukaram gave a test for holiness that fits this verse:

> जे का रंजले गांजले । त्यासी म्हणे जो आपुले ॥
> तोचि साधु ओळखावा । देव तेथेचि जाणावा ॥
>
> Those who are tormented and oppressed,
> he who calls them his own,
> know him to be a true saint;
> know that God dwells right there.

Tukaram places God where the oppressed are embraced. The psalm makes a parallel move, and a bolder one. It has just shown God's anger at its most violent, sleep, stupor, a silenced earth, and then states that all of it served one purpose, to save the lowly. For Tukaram, care for the afflicted shows that a person is holy. For the psalm, it shows what God's anger is for.

**Verse 11**

כִּי־חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ, שְׁאֵרִית חֵמֹת תַּחְגֹּר.

> For human fury shall praise You; with the remnant of furies You gird Yourself.

The introduction gave Rashi's two readings. Another question is whose fury makes up the "remnant." Malbim's lexical notes offer a reading that reverses the verse. Take תַּחְגֹּר as third person feminine, with human fury as its subject: "human fury restrains the remnant of God's fury." His main commentary explains the idea. Sennacherib's rage, meant to destroy everything, became the reason God held back the rest of His own anger and sent no further conqueror against the kingdoms of the earth. On this reading the worst human anger uses up the divine anger it provoked. Radak gives the remnant to the nations instead: those left alive after the great slaughter "will gird and bind up" their fury and never again rage against Israel. Meiri agrees and explains why God is still called the one who girds: He is the cause, so the act is credited to Him.

The Alshich adds a midrash that fits Malbim's reading. A human being is mastered by his anger. God is בַּעַל חֵמָה, "master of anger" (Nah 1:2), who holds it back when He wishes and releases it when He wishes. If human anger worked like God's, there would be nothing to praise. Because human anger strikes at once, God's restraint stands out, so "human fury praises You" in the sense that its contrast makes God's patience visible.

Modern critics propose place names: חֲמַת אֱדֹם, "the fury of Edom," and perhaps Hamath, the Syrian city whose gods the Assyrian envoy mocked (אַיֵּה אֱלֹהֵי חֲמָת, "where are the gods of Hamath?", 2 Kgs 18:34). The Edom reading brings in Isaiah 63, where God comes מֵאֱדוֹם with garments stained red, having trodden the winepress in His חֵמָה, with לְהוֹשִׁיעַ, "to save," in its first verse and "no one to help" (וְאֵין עֹזֵר) in its fifth. The later poets who set our v. 13 beside Isaiah 63 (see the liturgical section) had heard the same connection. The girding verb, finally, is royal dress. In יְהוָה… עֹז הִתְאַזָּר, "the LORD has girded Himself with strength" (Ps 93:1), the King puts on His power. Whatever the "remnant" is, it has become part of God's clothing.

**Verse 12**

נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם; כָּל־סְבִיבָיו יֹבִילוּ שַׁי לַמּוֹרָא.

> Vow and pay to the LORD your God; let all around Him bring tribute to the Fearsome One.

Here, for the only time in the psalm, the covenant name appears, at the moment of payment. Radak imagines the scene. The people "will say to one another, Vow and pay to your God who did these wonders." Ibn Ezra suggests the vows were made during the siege, as frightened people make them, and now fall due. The second verb, וְשַׁלְּמוּ, `wə-shal-lə-MŪ`, "pay," carries the same consonants as the city's name in v. 3, שָׁלֵם, `shā-LĒM`. At Shalem, one pays in full. The first Asaph psalm had asked for exactly this: וְשַׁלֵּם לְעֶלְיוֹן נְדָרֶיךָ, "and pay your vows to the Most High" (Ps 50:14).

The chronicler records something like this happening after 701: וְרַבִּים מְבִיאִים מִנְחָה לַיהוָה לִירוּשָׁלַ‍ִם וּמִגְדָּנוֹת לִיחִזְקִיָּהוּ, "and many brought offerings to the LORD in Jerusalem, and precious gifts to Hezekiah king of Judah" (2 Chr 32:23). Rashi extends it further. "All around Him" are those who heard of the deliverance, "and so they did," as Isaiah foresaw five Egyptian cities "speaking the language of Canaan and swearing to the LORD of Hosts" (Isa 19:18). The noun שַׁי, "tribute," occurs only three times in the Bible, and the other two are also about foreign peoples bringing gifts: to Zion from the land beyond the rivers of Cush (Isa 18:7), and from kings to the Temple (Ps 68:30).

Malbim reads לַמּוֹרָא as a place-noun, "the source of fear," and makes it an argument. Assyria terrified the kings of the earth, but its terror was borrowed: God had lent it "a portion of His fear." "So why bring tribute to Assyria? Bring it to the source from which fear came." The last verse makes the same argument, and Malbim reads the two together.

The collocation of fear and "all around Him" has a setting elsewhere: אֵל נַעֲרָץ בְּסוֹד־קְדֹשִׁים רַבָּה, וְנוֹרָא עַל־כָּל־סְבִיבָיו, "a God dreaded in the council of the holy ones, great and fearsome over all around Him" (Ps 89:8). There, "all around Him" are the heavenly beings of God's council. Psalm 76 uses the same words and then names, in the next line, "the kings of the earth." Earthly rulers stand where the heavenly council stood.

**Verse 13**

יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים; נוֹרָא לְמַלְכֵי־אָרֶץ.

> He clips the spirit of princes; He is fearsome to the kings of the earth.

Two senses of the verb compete, and the commentators divide on them. Rashi takes בצר as "lessen, hold back," citing Babel: וְעַתָּה לֹא־יִבָּצֵר מֵהֶם כֹּל אֲשֶׁר יָזְמוּ לַעֲשׂוֹת, "and now nothing they plan will be withheld from them" (Gen 11:6). The comparison fits well. At Babel, God saw human power with no limit and confused it. Here He puts a limit on the princes: He "reduces their arrogance." Ibn Ezra and Radak hear the vintage instead: God "cuts off" the princes' pride as a grape-gatherer cuts clusters, the sense of כִּי תִבְצֹר כַּרְמְךָ, "when you gather the grapes of your vineyard" (Deut 24:21). Malbim, glossing the verse, reaches for the last line of the Psalms 9–10 lament: God clips their spirit "so that he may no longer terrify mankind from the earth," לְבַל יוֹסִיף עוֹד לַעֲרֹץ אֱנוֹשׁ מִן הָאָרֶץ (Ps 10:18). He has recognized that the two psalms belong together, the one asking and the one answering.

The psalm ends on its third אֶרֶץ, "earth" (vv. 9, 10, 13). The earth that grew still, the lowly of the earth who were saved, and the kings of the earth who are awed make up the whole sequence of the psalm's second half, in three words.

The twelfth-century Persian poet Khaqani, standing in the ruins of the Sasanian palace at Ctesiphon, found the lesson written in stone:

> هان ای دل عبرت‌بین از دیده عِبَر کن هان
> ایوان مدائن را آیینهٔ عبرت دان
> ...
> دندانهٔ هر قصری پندی دهدت نو نو
> پند سر دندانه بشنو ز بن دندان
>
> Take heed, O heart that looks for warnings, learn from what you see!
> See the palace of Madain as a mirror of warning.
> ...
> The battlements of every palace give you counsel anew;
> hear the counsel of the battlements from the depths of your teeth.

Khaqani needs centuries and a ruin for the princes' spirit to be humbled. Time does the pruning, and a later visitor draws the moral. The psalm's verb is in the present-future, יִבְצֹר, and its agent is not time. The pruning is going on now, and those who see it are the princes themselves.

Rubén Darío, answering Theodore Roosevelt in 1905, used this psalm's own figures, the lion and the hunter, against an imperial power:

> Tened cuidado. ¡Vive la América española!
> Hay mil cachorros sueltos del León Español.
> Se necesitaría, Roosevelt, ser por Dios mismo,
> el Riflero terrible y el fuerte Cazador,
> para poder tenernos en vuestras férreas garras.
> Y, pues contáis con todo, falta una cosa: ¡Dios!
>
> Take care. Spanish America lives!
> There are a thousand loose cubs of the Spanish Lion.
> You would need, Roosevelt, to be God Himself,
> the terrible Rifleman and the strong Hunter,
> to hold us in your iron claws.
> And, though you count on everything, one thing is missing: God!

Darío's last line makes the psalm's argument in the language of modern politics: the empire has every weapon but not the one that counts. Darío's lions, though, are the small nations, and God is the one hunter the empire cannot become. In the psalm the lion is God, and the kings of the earth, in the last word, are left to be afraid.

### REFINED READER QUESTIONS

1. The psalm calls Jerusalem only "Salem" and never uses its full name. According to the midrash Radak cites on v. 3, what are the two halves of the name "Jerusalem," and where in the psalm might the missing half reappear?
2. The words for God's dwelling in v. 3, סֻכּוֹ and מְעוֹנָתוֹ, are elsewhere used for lions' lairs. How does Psalm 10:9, where a lion lies in its covert to seize the poor, change the way you hear Zion as God's "den"?
3. In v. 7 the verb "sank into deep sleep" is singular while its subject, "chariot and horse," is double and begins with an unexplained "and." How do Rashi, Radak and Ibn Ezra each deal with this, and what does the unsettled grammar suggest about the sleep itself?
4. Malbim identifies "Your anger" in v. 8 with Assyria, "the rod of My anger" (Isa 10:5). How does this reading change the question "who can stand before You?"
5. The Talmud reads v. 9 as a description of Sinai. What verse in Deuteronomy supports that reading, and what does it share with the psalm?
6. Verse 13's יִבְצֹר can mean "hold back" or "cut grapes." What does Rashi's comparison with Babel (Gen 11:6) bring out, and what do the later liturgical poets gain by setting the verse beside Isaiah's warrior coming from Bozrah?
