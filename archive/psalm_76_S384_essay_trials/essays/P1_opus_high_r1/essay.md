Two six-sided clay prisms from Nineveh, one in the British Museum and one in Chicago, preserve Sennacherib's own account of his campaign against Judah in 701 BCE. He lists forty-six walled towns taken. Of the Judean king he says: "Hezekiah himself, like a bird in a cage, I shut up in Jerusalem, his royal city." Then he gives the ending as an Assyrian king would want it remembered: "the terrifying splendor of my majesty overwhelmed him," and Hezekiah sent to Nineveh thirty talents of gold, eight hundred of silver, and his own daughters. In this version Jerusalem is a cage, the king's radiance does the conquering, and the tribute travels east.

Psalm 76 may or may not have been written about that siege. The Greek translators thought so and gave it the heading "a song concerning the Assyrian" (πρὸς τὸν Ἀσσύριον). Rashi was sure of it: "we find no enemy falling at Jerusalem except him." Radak first agreed and later changed his mind, reading it instead as the final war of Gog. Whatever war lies behind it, the psalm is remarkable on its own terms. It is a victory song in which nobody fights. No battle is narrated, no Israelite army appears, no human king is named, and no one says "we won." What remains is a field of broken weapons, a camp of men who will not wake, a sentence spoken from heaven, and a silence. By the end, the tribute travels the other way.

The shape is easy to follow. After the superscription, verses 2–4 establish a place, Judah, Salem, Zion, and report what happened "there": weapons broken. A Selah follows. Verses 5–7 turn to address God directly and show the enemy asleep. Verses 8–10 widen the view to heaven and earth: a verdict is heard, the world goes still, the lowly are saved, and another Selah follows. Verses 11–13 shift into the future tense. Human fury becomes praise, worshipers pay their vows, the nations bring gifts, and princes are cut back like vines.

## The morning after

The poem opens in the aftermath. Its first image of action is שָׁמָּה שִׁבַּר, "there He broke." The emphatic "there" points back to Zion, and it also answers something. Isaiah's oracle against Sennacherib promised that the Assyrian "shall not shoot an arrow there (לֹא־יוֹרֶה שָׁם חֵץ) nor come before it with a shield (מָגֵן)." The psalm takes the same three terms, there, arrow and shield, and turns the prophecy into a report. The list of broken things starts concretely with רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת, "the flaming shafts of the bow." The phrase carries the name of Resheph, the Canaanite archer-god of plague, who is reduced here to kindling. The list continues through shield and sword and ends on an abstraction: וּמִלְחָמָה, "and war." You can snap a bow, but "war" is not a physical object. The line breaks the category itself and then stops. The Selah that follows makes the listener hear a rest immediately after that word, as though the noise of all combat had been switched off.

Then we meet the soldiers, and the poem shows them only as sleepers. אַבִּירֵי לֵב, "the stout of heart," are אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ, "stripped." The verb is a rare reflexive form of the root for plunder, so the plunderers have themselves become the spoil. נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם, "they slept their sleep": the possessive gives the sleep its finality, the only sleep that belongs to them now. Then comes a line found nowhere else in the Bible: וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ כָל־אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל יְדֵיהֶם, "and none of the men of valor found their hands." The line does not say they could not use their hands. It says they could not find them, which is like the dream in which you reach for something and your own arm is gone. The cause is given only afterward, in verse 7, and it is a single breath: מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ, "at Your rebuke," horse and chariot נִרְדָּם, "sank into deep sleep." Malbim notices that horses, of all creatures, stay alert at night. His lexical companion work adds a sharper point. When a person rebukes a sleeper, the sleeper wakes. When God rebukes the waking, they fall asleep.

At that moment the poem turns to face God: אַתָּה נוֹרָא אַתָּה, "You, awesome are You." The word "awesome" is enclosed between two "You"s, and the question that follows, "who can stand before You?", has no answer. Then the camera pulls back to heaven. The decisive event of the whole psalm is a sentence being read out, מִשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּ דִּין, "from heaven You made judgment heard." What began as a battle has turned into a courtroom. The earth's response is אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה, "the earth feared and grew still." The rabbis puzzled over the pairing ("if it feared, why was it still?") and answered that it feared first and was still afterward (b. Shabbat 88a). The whole sequence of the poem is compressed into those two verbs.

Then the poem states what the verdict was for, and this is its most surprising line: לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, "to save all the lowly of the earth." A judge rises in order to rescue. A second Selah follows.

## Who does what

Line up the verbs and look at who performs each one. Every verb of force belongs to God: He broke, He made heard, He rose, He saved, He girds, He cuts back. The enemy's verbs are reflexive ("were stripped"), static ("slept," "sank into sleep"), impossible ("who can stand?"), or negated ("did not find"). The one transitive verb humans get before verse 11 is a failure to find, and its object is their own hands. The earth's verbs are fearing and falling quiet. Human action returns only after the verdict, and only as worship: fury תּוֹדֶךָּ, "will praise You"; the congregation is told נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ, "vow and pay"; the surrounding peoples יֹבִילוּ שַׁי, "will bring tribute." In this poem a human being can do three things. He can fail, he can fall silent, or he can give thanks.

What is missing is equally telling. The psalm contains no "I," no "we," no "us," no "our." Its sister Zion song, Psalm 46, uses the same title for God and adds "us": אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב מִשְׂגָּב־לָנוּ, "the God of Jacob is our stronghold." Psalm 76 says אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "God of Jacob," and stops there. The commentators feel the gap and fill it. Rashi makes the saved "Hezekiah and his company," and Radak says the lowly of the earth are Israel. But the poem itself withholds the possessive. It celebrates a deliverance in Judah without calling the delivered "us," and the only people it names as saved are defined by their lowliness, not their nationality. The universalism of verse 10 is not a single generous line. It is built into the grammar of the whole psalm.

## Three tellings of one night

Set the psalm beside Sennacherib's prism and a more pointed reply emerges. Assyrian royal inscriptions repeatedly credit victory to the king's *melammu*, the dreadful radiance of majesty that overwhelms enemies and makes them send gifts. Psalm 76 takes every part of that claim and reassigns it. God is נָאוֹר, "radiant" (verse 5). He is נוֹרָא, "awesome," twice. His very title becomes מוֹרָא, "the Fear," and tribute is carried to Him (verse 12). The prism's final image is a small king sending gold to Nineveh. The psalm's final image is מַלְכֵי־אָרֶץ, "the kings of the earth," in awe of Zion's God.

A third account, from Egypt, preserves a detail that brings the psalm's fourth and sixth verses to life. Herodotus (2.141) heard from Egyptian priests that when "Sanacharib, king of the Arabians and Assyrians" camped against Egypt, field mice swarmed the camp by night and gnawed through quivers, bowstrings and the handles of shields. In the morning the army fled with nothing to fight with. A statue of the Egyptian king, the priests said, held a mouse in its hand and bore the inscription ἐς ἐμέ τις ὁρέων εὐσεβὴς ἔστω, "let whoever looks at me be reverent." Whatever happened in that season of 701, three peoples remembered it as a night on which an army's weapons failed without a fight. Two of them, the Egyptian and the Hebrew, ended the story with a call to reverence.

Lord Byron read the same night through its sleepers. In "The Destruction of Sennacherib," written for his *Hebrew Melodies* in 1815, the breath of heaven stills the camp:

> For the Angel of Death spread his wings on the blast,
> And breathed in the face of the foe as he pass'd;
> And the eyes of the sleepers wax'd deadly and chill,
> And their hearts but once heaved, and for ever grew still!
>
> And there lay the steed with his nostril all wide,
> But through it there roll'd not the breath of his pride.

Byron draws on Kings, but he arrives at the psalm's images: the blast, the sleepers, the stillness, the horse. His "breath of his pride" comes close to the psalm's last verse, where God יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "clips the breath (or spirit) of princes," using the verb for cutting grape clusters. The difference is instructive. Byron lingers over corpses, widows' wailing and foam on the turf. The psalm shows no corpse at all, only sleep, and it does not end in lament. Byron's poem is a spectacle of death. The psalm uses the sleeping army as a way into worship.

The psalm also reverses two texts it clearly knows. Hosea promised a covenant in which God would break "bow and sword and war" (וְקֶשֶׁת וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה אֶשְׁבּוֹר) and then וְהִשְׁכַּבְתִּים לָבֶטַח, "make them lie down in safety." Psalm 76 has the same verb and the same climb from bow to war, and it is followed by sleep too, but here the sleepers are the aggressors, who do not get up. Behind that stands the Song of the Sea, which gives us "horse and chariot," אַדִּיר, "majestic," and נוֹרָא, "awesome," and which calls God אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, "a man of war." Psalm 76 keeps the vocabulary and changes the title. Its God does not make war. He breaks it.

## Fury as praise

The coda moves into the future tense, and the move matters. Up to verse 10 the verbs describe what happened. From verse 11 on they describe what will keep happening, so that one night becomes a pattern. Verse 11 is famously obscure: כִּי־חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ שְׁאֵרִית חֵמֹת תַּחְגֹּר, "for human fury will praise You; with the remnant of furies You gird Yourself." Rashi offers two readings. In one, the tyrant's rage, once it has visibly failed, turns into an admission of God's power, as Nebuchadnezzar blessed God after the furnace. In the other, drawing on a Mishnaic sense of the root, תַּחְגֹּר means "You restrain," as a nick in a knife "catches" a fingernail. Either way, human anger no longer operates independently. It is either converted into praise or held back. The Greek translators read the verb as "will keep festival," so that the leftover fury ends up celebrating a feast. This may be part of why the psalm came to be recited on Sukkot, when Zechariah's leftover nations go up to Jerusalem to keep the festival.

For anyone who has lived under a threat much larger than themselves, the psalm offers something other than a fantasy of fighting back and winning. It offers the stranger relief of waking to find that the thing you feared is asleep, and that the air, which has been full of noise for so long, has gone quiet.

It would be easy to hear Psalm 76 as a song of national triumph. Read closely, it is closer to a song of disarmament. Its grammar keeps force for God alone, its silence about "us" leaves room for all the lowly of the earth, and its ending points human anger toward the only use the poem allows it: to stop, and to give thanks.
