### INTRODUCTION ESSAY

The strangest casualty in Psalm 76 is a soldier’s hand. The stout-hearted warriors have fallen asleep, and the men of valor לֹא־מָצְאוּ יְדֵיהֶם, “could not find their hands.” The usual English rendering, “could not lift a hand,” makes good sense. But the Hebrew is more disturbing. What should be nearest to them—the means by which they hold a weapon or defend themselves—has become unavailable. The psalm never shows an Israelite fighter taking those weapons away. It asks its hearers to imagine an army losing the ability to act.

That is the song’s governing reversal. It begins with God’s reputation in Judah and Israel, narrows to his dwelling in Salem and Zion, then points to the place where weapons have been broken. Its middle addresses God directly as it describes the disabled army. The view rises to heaven for a verdict, returns to an earth gone quiet, and only then reveals whom the verdict serves: כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, “all the lowly of the earth.” Finally the singer turns to the living with commands to make vows and bring gifts. Human action disappears from the battlefield and reappears in worship.

The first image of Zion prepares us for a less comfortable song than its place-name suggests. God’s dwelling is סוּכּוֹ, “his shelter” or “his covert,” and מְעוֹנָתוֹ, “his dwelling-place.” A shelter can protect its occupants; a covert can conceal a hunter. Neither word by itself makes God a lion, but together with the later הַרְרֵי־טָרֶף, “mountains of prey,” they give that reading force. Elsewhere a wicked man waits כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה, “like a lion in its covert,” to seize the poor (Psalm 10:9). Here the lowly are the ones God rises to save. The image may reverse the frightening question of who is hunting whom. Zion is not safe because it is harmless.

There is another way to picture a besieged Jerusalem. In his account of the campaign of 701 BCE, Sennacherib boasts that he shut Hezekiah up in his royal city “like a bird in a cage.” The Assyrian king did not claim to have captured Jerusalem; he did claim to have confined its king and received tribute from him. Psalm 76 does not name Sennacherib, though its ancient Greek heading identifies the Assyrian as its opponent. It should not be made into a report proving what happened that night. But set beside the imperial inscription, its view of the city becomes striking: what looks from outside like a cage may, from the singer’s position, be the dangerous resident’s covert. By the end, tribute travels *toward* the God of Zion. That reverses the direction in Sennacherib’s story.

The first action comes with שָׁמָּה, “there.” God שִׁבַּר, “shattered,” the fiery shafts of the bow, then shield, sword, and מִלְחָמָה, “war.” The last item is no longer something a soldier can carry. Hosea imagines God breaking “bow and sword and war” (קֶשֶׁת וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה) so that his people may lie down safely (Hosea 2:20). Psalm 76 takes that hope through a grim reversal: the people who lie down are the attackers. The poem is not pretending that disarmament costs no lives, nor promising that no war will ever be fought again. It gives us no battle to admire. After the catalogue of broken arms comes סֶלָה, *Selah*. Whatever direction that word gave the performers, here it leaves the listener with the weapons before anyone has described a blow struck by human hands.

God is then addressed as נָאוֹר, “resplendent,” and אַדִּיר, “majestic.” The radiant word has a near echo in נוֹרָא, “awesome,” when the singer turns to him again a few lines later: beauty becomes difficult to face. Meanwhile the heroes are despoiled and נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם, “slept their sleep.” Horse and chariot fall into a deeper stupor at God’s גַּעֲרָה, “rebuke.” The chariot cannot literally sleep; the whole apparatus of battle has been made as useless as the hands of its soldiers. The rebuke is the effective force in the scene.

“Horse and chariot” recalls the Song of the Sea, where Pharaoh’s force is overcome. That song moves from victory toward the sanctuary God will establish (Exodus 15:17); Psalm 76 begins at the sanctuary and tells of victory there. The contrast reaches the word מִלְחָמָה, “war.” At the sea God is called אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, “a man of war” (Exodus 15:3). At Zion he breaks war. The old deliverance has not merely been repeated in a new location. Its language has been turned so that the instruments of fighting, rather than a fighting hero, occupy the foreground.

Lord Byron’s *The Destruction of Sennacherib* gives this sleeping army a memorable later voice. In a poem published in 1815, he writes:

> For the Angel of Death spread his wings on the blast,  
> And breathed in the face of the foe as he passed;  
> And the eyes of the sleepers waxed deadly and chill,  
> And their hearts but once heaved, and for ever grew still!

Byron supplies an angel, wings, and a last breath. Psalm 76 supplies none of them. Its hearer learns what has happened to the soldiers, then hears the cause in a single divine rebuke. The difference matters. Byron allows us to watch death pass through the camp; the psalm leaves us facing a power whose word is enough.

The singer seems to turn fully toward that power in the doubled אַתָּה נוֹרָא אַתָּה, “You are awesome, you.” Then the poem changes the description of what we have just heard. God has made דִּין, “a verdict,” audible מִשָּׁמַיִם, “from heaven.” The battle is presented as a judgment. In ordinary life, a judge’s sentence does something as well as saying something—but only because the judge has authority to pronounce it. Here there is no interval between God’s pronouncement and its effect. The earth hears, fears, and וְשָׁקָטָה, “falls quiet.” The military noise has ended in the silence of a court hearing its decision.

Only at this point does the psalm say what the decision was for. God rose לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כׇּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, “to save all the lowly of the earth.” The phrase can be heard first as the afflicted people of the land around Zion; as the song expands toward the earth’s kings, it invites a wider hearing too. Notably, the singers never say “God saved *us*.” There is no “we,” “us,” or “our” anywhere in the psalm. Psalm 46, another Zion song, calls the same deity אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, “the God of Jacob,” and says that he is לָנוּ, “our” stronghold. Psalm 76 names the God of Jacob but withholds that possessive. Its second סֶלָה, *Selah*, falls after the lowly have been named, as the first fell after the weapons were broken. It asks us to hold those two moments together.

If you have watched people with power decide what will happen to people without it, the delayed naming of the lowly changes the whole scene. The stillness is frightening; it is also the first moment in which those people may be safe.

The ending refuses an easy account of how violence becomes peace. חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ, “human fury will praise you,” says the singer; then God תַּחְגֹּר, “will gird on,” the remainder of fury. Is the remaining rage something God wears, or something he binds and restrains? The Hebrew has allowed both readings. Rashi takes the second as a check on the rage still to come, while also allowing the more literal image of God girding himself with it. Either way, human anger does not get the final word. Yet the line’s difficulty keeps us from making that claim sound effortless.

The commands that follow are clearer: נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ, “make vows and pay them.” At last people are told to do something. The sound of שַׁלְּמוּ, “pay,” recalls שָׁלֵם, “Salem,” where the song began; the city associated with peace is now a place where an obligation must be met. Those around God bring שַׁי, “tribute,” to הַמּוֹרָא, “the Awesome One.” We cannot tell exactly how far that circle extends, though the final sight is of the kings of the earth. God יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, “curbs the spirit of princes”: even rulers’ resolve is not beyond his reach.

For those singing after deliverance, the psalm offers no human general to imitate and no victory to claim as their own. For those still facing force, it refuses to treat the powerful as people whose hands will always work. It ends by giving human beings something to do, but not another weapon to pick up. They can keep their word.
