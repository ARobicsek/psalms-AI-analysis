Psalm 76 is a war song in which no one fights. Read it looking for the battle and you will not find one. There is no muster, no charge, no clash of lines, no soldier of Judah, no king of Judah. The poem arrives the morning after. Arrows lie snapped, shields and swords are strewn about, and the men who carried them are asleep and will not wake. In the first half of the poem the only sound is a single divine exhalation, גְּעָרָה, a "rebuke" or "blast." In the second half the only sound is a verdict spoken from heaven, and after it the whole earth goes quiet.

The shape is simple, and it widens as it goes. Verses 2–4 fix a place: God is "known in Judah," His lair is in Salem and His den in Zion, and "there He broke" the weapons. A *Selah* follows, a pause at the sight of the wreckage. In verses 5–7 the poem stops talking about God and turns to address Him, אַתָּה, "You," while the enemy sleeps. Verses 8–10 lift the scene from Zion to heaven and from the battlefield to a courtroom, and end on "all the lowly of the earth," with a second *Selah*. Verses 11–13 shift from past tense to future, and the local rescue becomes a standing rule for the kings of the world. The poem moves from Judah to earth, from one morning to all time, from a camp of corpses to a procession of tribute-bearers.

### Who is allowed to act

The poem's central move is easiest to see if you ask of each verb who is doing it. Every active verb of force belongs to God. He broke, שִׁבַּר. He made judgment heard, הִשְׁמַעְתָּ. He rose, קוּם, to save, לְהוֹשִׁיעַ. He girds, תַּחְגֹּר. He clips, יִבְצֹר. Now look at what the enemies are given. The warriors אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ, "were despoiled," a rare reflexive form of the verb for plunder: men who came to strip a city are themselves stripped. They נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם, "slumbered their sleep," doing nothing but lying still. Chariot and horse נִרְדָּם, "lay stunned," and the verb is singular with a double subject, as if the whole cavalry were one sleeping body. Malbim adds a detail only a horseman would notice: horses normally stay alert at night, so even the animal that keeps watch has gone under. Then comes the rhetorical question מִי יַעֲמֹד, "who can stand?", whose answer is no one. Finally there is the one transitive verb any human gets before the coda, and it is negated: וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ כָל־אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל יְדֵיהֶם, "all the men of valor did not find their hands." The object of the only human action in the poem is the soldiers' own bodies, and they cannot locate them.

Students of animal behavior call this tonic immobility, the freeze. A hunted creature, cornered by a predator, goes rigid and limp, unable to fight or flee. The psalm's imagery is built to produce this reversal. God's dwelling is a סֻכּוֹ, a word that means a festival booth but also, in Jeremiah 25:38, the covert a young lion leaves when it goes out in wrath. Beside it stands מְעוֹנָתוֹ, the same word Nahum uses for the lions' den of Nineveh. God shines forth "from the mountains of prey," מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף, and the Song of Songs pairs the same two words, "from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards." Elsewhere in the Psalms the lion in its covert is the oppressor: in Psalm 10:9 the wicked man "lurks like a lion in his covert" (כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה) "to seize the poor." Psalm 76 takes that picture and gives the covert a new occupant. There is one lair in the world whose lion hunts the hunters. The men who marched against Zion as predators receive the prey's reflex, and they freeze.

The sleep that follows carries a sharp irony for anyone who knows the prophets. Hosea promised a covenant in which God would break "bow and sword and war" from the land, וְקֶשֶׁת וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה אֶשְׁבּוֹר, "and I will make them lie down in safety." Psalm 76:4 repeats that sequence almost word for word, with the same verb of breaking and the same climb from weapon to the abstract noun "war," and then it also turns to sleep. But in this version the sleepers are the aggressors. Peace, in Hosea, means Israel can close its eyes. Peace, here, means the attacker never opens his. Another psalm hangs over the scene: "the Guardian of Israel does not slumber (לֹא יָנוּם) and does not sleep." The enemy's נָמוּ answers it exactly. The one who never nods stands over those who have nodded off for good.

### The other side of the story

The Greek translators titled this psalm "against the Assyrian," and Rashi, Malbim and Meiri all read it as the night in 701 BCE when Sennacherib's army died outside Jerusalem. Assyria told the story differently. Sennacherib's own annals, on the clay prisms from Nineveh, boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage" and that Hezekiah afterward sent him gold, silver and his own daughters as tribute. The annals never explain why the king went home without taking the city. Two and a half centuries later Herodotus heard yet another version from Egyptian priests (*Histories* 2.141). An army of "Sanacharib, king of the Arabians and Assyrians," camped against Egypt, and in the night field mice swarmed the camp and gnawed through the quivers, the bowstrings and the handles of the shields, so that in the morning the soldiers fled unarmed. Herodotus says a statue in the temple of Hephaestus showed the Egyptian king holding a mouse, with the inscription, "Whoever looks on me, let him revere the gods."

Every version agrees on the outline: a great army, a single night, weapons that stopped working, reverence as the lesson. They differ on who gets the credit. The Assyrian scribe credits the king, and the Egyptian priest credits mice and a local god. Psalm 76 names no agent but one, and it gives no man even a supporting role. Remarkably, Herodotus's mice attack exactly what verse 4 lists: bows and shields, רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת מָגֵן. Isaiah's oracle before the siege had promised that Sennacherib "shall not shoot an arrow there, nor come before it with a shield," וְלֹא־יוֹרֶה שָׁם חֵץ וְלֹא־יְקַדְּמֶנָּה מָגֵן. The psalm's emphatic opening, שָׁמָּה, "there," answers that שָׁם. What was forbidden there was broken there.

Byron, who took the story from Kings, came very close to the psalm's picture in "The Destruction of Sennacherib" (1815):

> For the Angel of Death spread his wings on the blast,
> And breathed in the face of the foe as he pass'd;
> And the eyes of the sleepers wax'd deadly and chill,
> And their hearts but once heaved, and for ever grew still!
>
> And there lay the steed with his nostril all wide,
> But through it there roll'd not the breath of his pride.

Byron has the blast, the breath, the sleepers, and the horse, and even "the breath of his pride," which is almost a translation of the psalm's last line, רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "the spirit of princes," clipped like grapes from a vine. The difference is telling. Byron's camera stays among the corpses, lingering on the pallor, the rust on the mail, the silent tents. Once the psalm has shown the sleepers it moves on to address God and then the whole earth. It is interested in the wreckage only as evidence of who can act and who cannot.

### Two silences, and no "us"

The most remarkable absence in the psalm is grammatical. Psalm 76 contains no "I," no "we," no "us," no "our." Its sister Zion song, Psalm 46, uses the same title for God and adds the possessive: "The LORD of hosts is with us, the God of Jacob is our stronghold." Psalm 76 says אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "God of Jacob," and stops. It never says who was saved, and when it finally names the beneficiaries it names כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, "all the lowly of the earth." The commentators feel the gap and fill it. Rashi says God rose "to save Hezekiah and his company," and Radak says the lowly of the earth "are Israel." Both readings are natural, but both supply exactly the word the poet held back. The poem is sung in Jerusalem and never claims the victory as Jerusalem's own.

That restraint changes how we should hear verse 9: אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה, "the earth feared and was still." The Talmud (Shabbat 88a) notices that the two verbs pull against each other: if it feared, why was it still, and if it was still, why did it fear? It answers that first it feared and in the end it was still. Malbim reads the hush as relief. The earth had been afraid of Assyria, and when it heard that God had risen to judge, "it was quieted from its fear," because divine judgment "does not come to strengthen the tyrant who oppresses and robs, but to save the lowly crushed under his hand." Here is the center of the poem. The same silence falls on everyone, and it means opposite things depending on where you stood. To the man who came with a bow, it is the freeze of the hunted. To the one who has spent years hiding from men with bows, it is the first night in a long time without the sound of marching. Anyone who has lived under a threat that suddenly lifted knows that quiet: the body does not yet believe it, and then, slowly, it does.

### When human verbs come back

In the coda the verbs shift to the future, and humans are allowed to act again, but only in one way. חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ, "human fury will praise You." Rashi's example is Nebuchadnezzar, who threw three men into the furnace and ended up blessing their God. Then come the imperatives, נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ, "vow and pay," and the surrounding peoples יֹבִילוּ שַׁי לַמּוֹרָא, "bring tribute to the Awesome One." This reverses the Assyrian prism. There, a king of Judah carries gold to Nineveh. Here, the kings of the earth carry gifts to a God they fear. The Greek translation reads the difficult close of verse 11 as "the remnant of wrath will keep festival to You." That fits the haftarah read on the first day of Sukkot, when Psalm 76 is the psalm of the day in many communities: Zechariah's vision of "all who are left of all the nations that came against Jerusalem" going up each year "to keep the festival of booths." The booth of verse 3, God's סֻכּוֹ, becomes the destination of the people who once came to destroy it.

The Song of the Sea called God אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, "a man of war." Psalm 76 shares that song's horse and chariot and its words for "mighty" and "awesome," but its God does something Exodus never claims: "there He broke… shield and sword and war," וּמִלְחָמָה. The last item on the list is not a weapon but war itself. What the reader should carry away is the grammar that makes this true. In this poem human beings may do three things: fail, fall silent, or give thanks. Every other verb has been taken from them, and the poem presents that as the definition of peace.
