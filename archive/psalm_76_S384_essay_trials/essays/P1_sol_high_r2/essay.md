The strongest men in Psalm 76 cannot find their hands. The phrase is stranger than saying they cannot lift them: וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ … יְדֵיהֶם, “they did not find … their hands” (v. 6). The poem has removed a soldier’s ability to act before it has shown us a battle. No defender strikes a blow. Indeed, no defender appears. The hearer is made to watch an army lose its weapons, its courage, and finally its command of its own body.

That is the experience this song gives someone threatened by armed power: for a few minutes, the army ceases to be the most capable actor in the world. The poem begins close to home, with God known in Judah and Israel (v. 2) and dwelling in Salem and Zion (v. 3). There he breaks the weapons of war (v. 4). We see the attackers stunned (vv. 5–7); then the view lifts to heaven, where God’s judgment is heard and the earth falls quiet (vv. 8–10). Only after that silence do people speak and act again, making vows and bringing gifts (vv. 11–13). The movement is from a particular city to the kings of the earth, but also from human helplessness to a different kind of human agency.

Salem is an old name for Jerusalem, and its sound suggests peace. Yet God’s dwelling there is called סוּכּוֹ, “his shelter” or perhaps “his covert.” A *sukkah* can protect someone from danger; the same word can describe a lion’s hiding place. Jeremiah pictures a lion leaving סֻכּוֹ, “its lair” (Jer. 25:38). Psalm 76 never calls God a lion, but its next description of him is מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף, “from the mountains of prey” (v. 5). The suggestion changes how we enter the city. Salem is peaceful not because it contains nothing dangerous, but because the dangerous one who lives there confronts its attackers.

Another psalm puts a lion in a covert for the opposite purpose. In Psalm 10:9 the wicked man waits כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה, “like a lion in his covert,” לַחֲטֹף עָנִי, “to snatch the poor.” Psalm 76 may turn that frightening scene around: God inhabits the covert, and when he rises, it is לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כׇּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, “to save all the lowly of the earth” (v. 10). The lion image is an overtone, not an explicit portrait. It matters because it asks whose safety a fearsome presence serves.

At Zion God shatters רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת, “the fiery shafts of the bow,” followed by מָגֵן וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה, “shield and sword and war” (v. 4). The list moves beyond objects to the thing for which they are made. Hosea uses a strikingly close sequence in a promise of peace: וְקֶשֶׁת וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה אֶשְׁבּוֹר, “bow and sword and war I will break” (Hos. 2:20). Hosea continues וְהִשְׁכַּבְתִּים לָבֶטַח, “I will make them lie down in safety.” Psalm 76 continues toward sleep too, but it is the warriors who sleep: נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם, “they slept their sleep” (v. 6). In one poem, disarmament lets the vulnerable rest. In the other, it is imposed on those who threaten them. The first סֶלָה, “Selah,” comes immediately after war is broken. Whatever its precise musical direction, the word gives a performance somewhere to stop before showing the human consequences.

Those consequences receive more space than the destruction of the weapons. God is נָאוֹר, “radiant,” and אַדִּיר, “majestic” (v. 5); the men facing him are אַבִּירֵי לֵב, “mighty of heart” (v. 6). The near-echo of *’ad-DIYR* and *’ab-BIY-rēy* brings the two kinds of might briefly together in the ear. Then the men are despoiled, sleep, and cannot find their hands. Even וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס, “chariot and horse,” lie stunned at God’s גְּעָרָה, “rebuke” (v. 7). A rebuke is something one does with a voice. The poem gives the war machine several lines of equipment and gives God one utterance.

This is one reason the old association with the Assyrian assault on Jerusalem is compelling, though the psalm itself names no enemy. Its ancient Greek heading adds “against the Assyrian”; later readers heard Sennacherib’s campaign of 701 BCE in the sleeping soldiers. Nahum, addressing Assyria directly, says נָמוּ רֹעֶיךָ מֶלֶךְ אַשּׁוּר, “your shepherds slumber, king of Assyria” (Nah. 3:18). The word נָמוּ, “they slumber,” is rare, and Nahum also mocks Nineveh as a vanished lions’ den. There is another side to the story. In his own account of the campaign, Sennacherib says he shut Hezekiah inside Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage” and received tribute from him. He does not claim to have captured the city. Psalm 76 likewise puts Jerusalem at the center, but makes its resident God the one before whom others bring tribute. The correspondence does not date the poem securely. It lets us hear the competing claims that could surround a city which survived an imperial attack.

The poem does something more unsettling than give Jerusalem a better military report. It refuses to let its singers take credit. There is no “I,” “we,” “us,” or “our” anywhere in it. Psalm 46, another Zion song, confidently says יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת עִמָּנוּ, “the LORD of Hosts is with us,” and calls him אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, “the God of Jacob” (Ps. 46:8). Psalm 76 uses that same title, אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, “God of Jacob” (v. 7), but supplies no “us.” Its first direct address to human hearers comes near the end: נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ, “make vows and pay them” (v. 12). They have not been asked to imagine themselves as the victorious army. They are asked to answer a deliverance.

At the poem’s center, the fear shifts. First God is addressed face to face: אַתָּה נוֹרָא אַתָּה, “You are awesome, you” (v. 8). The repeated אַתָּה, “you,” holds him before the hearer. Then comes a verdict מִשָּׁמַיִם, “from heaven”; אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה, “the earth feared and fell still” (v. 9). The quiet is not simple comfort. For an instant, even the earth does not know what the judgment will mean for it. The next line says why God has risen: לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כׇּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, “to save all the lowly of the earth” (v. 10). That is where the second סֶלָה, “Selah,” falls.

From inside a frightened city, those lines might offer a moment in which you no longer have to match an enemy’s strength. You can be among the lowly and still matter to the judge. The poem begins by naming Judah and Israel, but here it names the beneficiaries by their condition rather than by their nation. “All the lowly of the earth” need not erase the original city or its people; it prevents them from being the only people for whom this judgment can be good.

The Song of the Sea helps show how far Psalm 76 has moved. Exodus 15 begins with victory over סוּס וְרֹכְבוֹ, “horse and rider” (Exod. 15:1). Nations then become יִדְּמוּ כָּאָבֶן, “silent as a stone” (15:16), before the song reaches מָכוֹן לְשִׁבְתְּךָ, “a place for your dwelling” (15:17). Psalm 76 starts with the dwelling, then shows the disabled chariot and the stilled earth. The order is reversed, as though the God who brought a people toward a sanctuary now acts from one. Exodus calls him יְהוָה אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, “the LORD, a man of war” (15:3). In Psalm 76 the last item he breaks is מִלְחָמָה, “war” (v. 4). The text celebrates a violent defeat; it does not promise that no war will ever recur. But it will not make warfare itself an object of praise.

The turn from rebuke to verdict to vow recalls the philosopher J. L. Austin’s observation that some words do things: a judge’s sentence changes someone’s standing; a promise places an obligation on its speaker. God’s rebuke disables the army, and his judgment establishes the purpose of its defeat. Only then do human words regain force. The call נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ, “make vows and pay them,” insists on deeds that follow speech (v. 12). It also answers the city’s name: שָׁלֵם, “Salem,” is answered by שַׁלְּמוּ, “pay.” Peace is not a mood produced by watching an enemy fall. It makes a claim on what the survivors will do next.

The obscure verse between judgment and vows says כִּי־חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ, “for human fury will acknowledge you,” and then שְׁאֵרִית חֵמֹת תַּחְגֹּר, perhaps “you will gird yourself with the remainder of fury” (v. 11). Readers have also understood the last verb as restraining fury; the image is difficult. In either case, the poem denies human rage the last word. Yet its confidence has a cost worth noticing. After serving as a medical orderly in the First World War, Georg Trakl wrote in *Grodek* of a battlefield’s sounds:

> Am Abend tönen die herbstlichen Wälder  
> Von tödlichen Waffen, die goldnen Ebenen  
> Und blauen Seen, darüber die Sonne  
> Düstrer hinrollt; umfängt die Nacht  
> Sterbende Krieger, die wilde Klage  
> Ihrer zerbrochenen Münder.
>
> At evening the autumn woods resound  
> With deadly weapons, the golden plains  
> And blue lakes; above them the sun  
> Rolls darkly; night enfolds  
> Dying soldiers, the wild lament  
> Of their broken mouths.

Trakl keeps listening where Psalm 76 moves toward silence. His broken mouths warn us not to mistake the psalm’s stillness for an account of everything a defeated person experiences. Psalm 76 is a song sung from danger, not a report from the dead. It chooses to ask what happens when the power that endangered the lowly can no longer act.

The ending does not return us to a grateful “we.” God יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, “curbs the spirit of princes,” and remains נוֹרָא לְמַלְכֵי־אָרֶץ, “awesome to the kings of the earth” (v. 13). The word for curbing can suggest cutting grapes from a vine: royal resolve, which seemed so formidable at the start, is something God can remove. The last word is אָרֶץ, “earth.” What began with a God known in Judah finishes with rulers everywhere subject to him, while the people named most tenderly along the way are the lowly. The song gives a threatened community courage without handing it the victor’s weapons—or allowing it to call those weapons peace.
