### INTRODUCTION ESSAY

Herodotus heard a story in Egypt about an Assyrian army that was beaten without a battle. The priests of Memphis told him that when "Sanacharib, king of the Arabians and Assyrians," marched on Egypt, their king Sethos had no warriors willing to fight. In the night, field mice swarmed the Assyrian camp and gnawed through the quivers, the bowstrings and the handles of the shields, and in the morning the invaders fled unarmed (*Histories* 2.141). The priests showed him a stone statue of their king holding a mouse, inscribed: "Whoever looks on me, let him revere the gods."

The Greek translators of the Psalms added a heading to Psalm 76, "an ode concerning the Assyrian." It is the same story told from inside Jerusalem, and it shares the Egyptian version's odd emphasis. Neither text describes a victory. Both describe a disarmament: the bows, the arrows, the shields. The psalm then goes further than the priests did. After listing the weapons, it names one more thing God breaks, and that thing is מִלְחָמָה, "war."

This is a victory song with no battle in it, and no "we." Hearing it properly means noticing what is missing.

## The shape of a silence

The poem moves like a camera that keeps changing its distance. It opens on a whole country: נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים, "God has made Himself known in Judah," and His name is great in Israel. Then it closes in on one hill, Salem and Zion. Then a finger points: שָׁמָּה, "There." What follows is not fighting but the sound of wood and bronze snapping: "the flames of the bow, shield and sword, and war." Then comes the first סֶלָה, *Selah*. Whatever the word meant in performance, it falls exactly where the noise stops.

After the pause the speaker turns and addresses God directly: "You are resplendent." This is the longest movement, and it is spoken to God's face. It passes over the enemy camp, where the soldiers sleep, and then over the whole earth, where it goes quiet: מִשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּ דִּין, אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה, "from heaven You made judgment heard; the earth feared and was still." Only now are we told what this was for: God rose "to save all the lowly of the earth." The second *Selah* falls here.

The two pauses are placed with care. The first comes after weapons are destroyed, the second after the helpless are rescued, so each pause explains the other. A short coda follows, and in it the congregation is addressed for the first time with plural commands: make vows, pay them, bring gifts. The last word goes to the kings of the earth, who are left in awe.

## Zion as a lair

Before any enemy appears, we are told where God lives, and the nouns are unsettling. וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ בְצִיּוֹן, "In Salem is His covert, His den in Zion." סֻכָּה can mean a festival booth, but סֹךְ is also the thicket where a lion waits. Jeremiah uses it of God "like a young lion leaving his covert" (Jer 25:38). מְעוֹנָה is the word for the lion's den in Amos and Nahum. A few verses later God blazes מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף, "from the mountains of prey." That rare construction appears once more, in the Song of Songs, again beside lions' dens: "from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards" (Song 4:8). Radak draws the obvious conclusion: God goes out against the enemy "like the lion that comes out of the mountains of prey."

What gives this image its moral force is what lions usually do in the Psalms. In Psalm 10 the wicked man "lies in wait like a lion in his covert, בְסֻכֹּה, to seize the poor." Everywhere else, the lion in its thicket is the predator of the helpless. Nahum mocks Assyria's capital as a lions' den that has disappeared: "Where is the den of the lions?" Psalm 76 answers that there is one lair left on earth, and its lion hunts predators. The listener comes to Zion expecting a sanctuary and is told it is also a hunting ground. It is safe, and it is not tame.

## The grammar of disarmament

The poem's argument is carried most exactly by its verbs. Every active verb that does something to something else belongs to God. He *broke* (שִׁבַּר), He *made heard* (הִשְׁמַעְתָּ), He *rose* (קוּם) to *save* (לְהוֹשִׁיעַ). Later He *girds* and He *clips*. The enemy never gets a verb of attack.

The stout-hearted אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ, "were plundered." The form is strange and Aramaic in flavor, and it bends back on its subjects: men who came to take spoil are spoiled. נָמוּ שְׁנָתָ**ם**, "they slumbered their sleep," and the possessive suggests a sleep that belongs to them for good. Then comes the most uncanny line in the psalm: וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ כָל־אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל יְדֵיהֶם, "and all the men of valor did not find their hands." It does not say they could not use their hands. It says they could not locate them. Malbim pictures exactly this: the hands seem to have gone missing, so that when the predator arrives there is nothing to fight with. Anyone who has read accounts of combat terror will recognize the freeze, the body going absent at the moment it is most needed. Then, at God's גַּעֲרָה, the "rebuke" or "roar" that elsewhere drives back the sea, נִרְדָּם וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס, "chariot and horse fell into a stupor." The verb is the root of the deep sleep that fell on Adam. Malbim adds a sharp observation: horses normally wake at night.

In the first half of the poem, humans can do only three things: fail, fall asleep, and fall silent. The one transitive verb they receive is "did not find," and its object is their own hands.

This is also where the poem shows its sources and turns them around. The phrase וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה, "and sword and war," with the same verb "break," comes from Hosea's covenant of peace: "Bow and sword and war I will break from the land, and I will make them lie down in safety" (Hos 2:20). In Hosea, disarmament lets Israel sleep. In the psalm, disarmament also ends in sleep, but it is the invader who sleeps and does not wake. The Song of the Sea supplies the "horse and chariot" and the word אַדִּיר, and it gives God a title: יְהוָה אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, "the LORD is a man of war" (Exod 15:3). Psalm 76 keeps the Warrior and removes the war. The God of the sea is still here, but His final act is to break the category He was named for. Even the enemy's poetic vocabulary is taken back. The only other place in the Bible where the verb נָמוּ appears is Nahum's taunt to Assyria: "Your shepherds slumber, O king of Assyria, your nobles (אַדִּירֶיךָ) lie still." Psalm 76 gives אַדִּיר to God and leaves the enemy only the slumber.

Byron's "Destruction of Sennacherib" (1815) is the most famous retelling of the scene, and he understood the central image, which is sleep rather than slaughter:

> For the Angel of Death spread his wings on the blast,
> And breathed in the face of the foe as he pass'd;
> And the eyes of the sleepers wax'd deadly and chill,
> And their hearts but once heaved, and for ever grew still!
> And there lay the steed with his nostril all wide,
> But through it there roll'd not the breath of his pride.

Byron's "blast" and "breath" match the psalm's גַּעֲרָה, and his "grew still" matches the earth's שָׁקָטָה. His horse lies stunned. The comparison is most useful for what Byron leaves out. His poem ends in spectacle: the wailing widows of Assyria, the idols broken in the temple of Baal. The psalm refuses to stay with the corpses. Its camera leaves the camp and rises to heaven, and when it comes down again it is looking at other people entirely.

## Saved, and no one says "us"

Psalm 76 contains no first-person form anywhere: no "I," no "we," no "our." That is remarkable for a Zion song celebrating a deliverance in Judah. Its sister psalm, Psalm 46, has the refrain "The LORD of hosts is with us, the God of Jacob is our refuge." Psalm 76 uses the same title, אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "God of Jacob," and attaches no "us" to it. When the poem finally names who was saved, it is כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, "all the lowly of the earth." The criterion is not membership in a people but lowliness, and the scope is the whole earth.

The commentators felt this gap and filled it. Rashi makes the saved "Hezekiah and his company." Radak says the lowly of the earth are Israel. Their instinct is natural, and the text holds back exactly what they add. Malbim is closer to the poem's logic when he explains why the terrified earth grew calm. Divine judgment, he says, "does not come to give strength to the tyrant who oppresses and robs, but to save the lowly crushed under his hand." When the earth heard that this was the kind of judge arriving, its fear turned to quiet. The Talmud (Shabbat 88a) asks the same question of the same verse: if the earth feared, how could it be still, and if it was still, why did it fear? It answers: "At first it feared, and in the end it was still." The poem's peace is reached through terror, not around it.

To be rescued like this is an odd experience from the inside. Something enormous was aimed at you, and it has simply stopped. You did nothing and cannot claim to have done anything. What you feel first is not triumph but a shaking that slowly turns into quiet, and then the question of what you owe.

## Paying at Salem

The coda starts on the poem's hardest line: כִּי־חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ שְׁאֵרִית חֵמֹת תַּחְגֹּר, "for human fury will acknowledge You; the remnant of furies You gird on." The two words for fury press against each other until it is unclear whose rage is whose. Human wrath seems to be taken up into God's and worn like a belt. Rashi offers a second, rabbinic sense of חגר, "to hold back," so that once the wicked see their fury come to nothing, the rest of it is restrained. Either way, the violence that drove the poem is not destroyed. It is absorbed and redirected.

Then humans get their first real verbs, and they are verbs of worship: נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ, "vow and pay." The root of **שָׁלֵם**, Salem, the name that opened the poem and that the Greek translators rendered "in peace," returns as וְ**שַׁלְּמוּ**, "pay." The city of peace is answered with payment. Then "all around Him" bring שַׁי, "tribute," to the Fearsome One. The one picture we have of an Israelite king from his own century is on Shalmaneser III's Black Obelisk: "Jehu son of Omri" bowing to the ground with tribute before the Assyrian throne. Every Judean who sang this psalm knew who normally received the gifts. Malbim reads לַמּוֹרָא as "to the source of fear." Assyria was feared, he says, only because God lent it some of His terror, so bring the tribute to where the fear comes from. The procession of vassals is turned toward Zion.

Jewish custom sings this psalm on the first day of Sukkot, attracted by the word סֻכּוֹ. The haftarah of that day, Zechariah 14, stages the coda: "everyone who is left of all the nations that came against Jerusalem" will go up year after year "to keep the festival of Sukkot." The ancient Greek translation of the psalm read the difficult verb in verse 11 not as "gird" but as "keep festival": the remnant of fury will celebrate before You. Whether or not the translator was thinking of Zechariah, the festival makes the reading visible. The leftover of the armies ends up as pilgrims.

That is the poem's movement, and the thing to take away from it. It starts where a war song would start and replaces the war cry with the sound of a bow snapping, then with sleep, then with the silence of a whole earth. When human hands finally reappear in this psalm, found again after all, they are not holding weapons. They are paying what they owe.
