**Governing idea**
Psalm 76 removes human agency from the battlefield: soldiers cannot even find their hands. It returns that agency to people only as worship, in vows kept and tribute brought.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**
The essay traces a movement from reputation to dwelling to broken weapons, then up to a heavenly verdict and back down to a silenced earth. Only after that does the psalm reveal the beneficiaries, "the lowly of the earth." It hears Zion as a predator's covert rather than a safe enclosure, and reads the psalm against Hosea, the Song of the Sea, Sennacherib's annals and Byron as a song where rebuke, not combat, decides the outcome.

**Strongest insights**
- "could not find their hands": the literal Hebrew is more unsettling than the idiom. [poetic craft]
- "The last item is no longer something a soldier can carry": on מִלְחָמָה ending the list of weapons. [poetic craft]
- "At the sea God is called אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה… At Zion he breaks war." [Bible]
- "what looks from outside like a cage may… be the dangerous resident's covert." [beyond the Bible]
- "There is no 'we,' 'us,' or 'our' anywhere in the psalm": contrasted with Psalm 46's לָנוּ. [Bible]

**Best writing**
"Zion is not safe because it is harmless."
"It ends by giving human beings something to do, but not another weapon to pick up. They can keep their word."

**Weak spots**
- "Reversal" does a lot of work: "governing reversal," "grim reversal," "reverse the frightening question," "reverses the direction." The repetition blurs distinct points.
- "The difference matters" and "Yet the line's difficulty keeps us from making that claim sound effortless" read as filler.
- The direct appeal "If you have watched people with power decide…" is vaguer than the textual observations around it.
- The judge analogy ("does something as well as saying something") takes a sentence of setup for a modest point.
- "The Hebrew has allowed both readings" is an odd tense.
- The lion reading of סוּכּוֹ/מְעוֹנָתוֹ could draw on מְעוֹן used of lions' dens elsewhere (e.g., Nahum 2:12). The essay leans only on Psalm 10:9.

**Claims worth checking**
- **Rashi on v. 11:** The essay says Rashi takes תַּחְגֹּר as a check on future rage while "also allowing" the literal girding image. This is plausible, but the exact wording and whether Rashi offers both readings should be verified.
- **Other references:** The rest appear accurate:
  - Psalm 10:9 (כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה)
  - Hosea 2:20 in Hebrew numbering (2:18 in English)
  - Exodus 15:3 and 15:17
  - Sennacherib's "like a bird in a cage"
  - The LXX heading πρὸς τὸν Ἀσσύριον
  - Byron's poem (1815, *Hebrew Melodies*) and the quoted stanza
  - Psalm 46's אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב and לָנוּ
- **"Horse and chariot" and the Song of the Sea:** The pairing in the Song is closest in Exodus 15:19 (and "horse and rider," 15:1), not an exact match.
