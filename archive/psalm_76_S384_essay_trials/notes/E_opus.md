**Governing idea** — Psalm 76 tells of a victory with no human fighters on the winning side, and so turns a military rescue into a divine verdict for "all the lowly of the earth," a group the poem refuses to narrow to the singing community.

**What it says the poem does and evokes** — The essay traces four movements: God's dwelling, the attackers' paralysis, a heavenly sentence, and the widening to vows and kings. It hears echoes of lion imagery (Jeremiah, Psalm 10), Hosea's disarmament, the Sea crossing, and Sennacherib's siege. It argues that the psalm reverses or redirects each of these echoes.

**Strongest insights**
- "Psalm 76 calls on that same 'God of Jacob'… but does not add 'with us.'" [Bible]
- "Here the dangerous inhabitant of the covert rises on the poor person's side." The essay sets סֻכֹּה in Psalm 10 against Psalm 76. [Bible]
- "In Hosea, disarmament lets the protected people sleep. In Psalm 76, it is the armed men who sleep." [Bible]
- "The Song of the Sea begins with the defeated horse and ends with a place for God to dwell; Psalm 76 begins at God's dwelling." [poetic craft]
- "Radak takes those lowly to be Israel… But the verse says 'all.'" [tradition]

**Best writing** — "Exodus calls YHWH אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה, 'a warrior' (Exod 15:3). The Zion song remembers the warrior by having him break מִלְחָמָה, 'war.'"

**Weak spots**
- "Psalm 46… could easily have supplied one" is awkward. It sounds as if Psalm 46 might lend Psalm 76 a line, when the point is contrast.
- "Less placid than its other name, Salem, might lead us to expect" relies on the *shalom* association without stating it.
- The lair reading is attached only to סוּכּוֹ. The essay glosses מְעוֹנָתוֹ neutrally as "dwelling," though the word can itself mean a lion's den, which would strengthen its own case.
- "The last item is no piece of equipment" is dropped and only picked up again two paragraphs later.
- "The identification has more behind it than a resemblance between sleeping soldiers and an army dead by morning" is vague. It is unclear what the "more" is beyond the Isaiah parallel just cited. The allusion to Isa 37:36 goes unstated.
- The short paragraph beginning "A person facing an army has no use for assurances…" reads as an aphorism loosely attached to the argument.

**Claims worth checking**
- The biblical quotations all appear accurate in Hebrew verse numbering: Ps 46:8, Jer 25:38, Ps 10:9, Hos 2:20, Ps 106:9, Exod 15:3, Isa 37:33. English Bibles number Ps 46:8 as 46:7 and Hos 2:20 as 2:18, so a reader using an English Bible may be momentarily thrown.
- The LXX heading πρὸς τὸν Ἀσσύριον is correct. The psalm is Ps 75 in LXX numbering.
- Rashi's reading of the psalm as Sennacherib's defeat matches his commentary.
- Radak's identification of the "lowly" as Israel is plausible. However, Radak's overall setting for the psalm may be Sennacherib, the future war of Gog, or both. It is worth confirming exactly what he says at v. 10.
