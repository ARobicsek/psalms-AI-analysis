**Governing idea**
The essay reads Psalm 76 as a song that disables armed power by God's word alone, so a threatened community can answer deliverance with vows instead of claiming a victor's weapons or glory.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**
The essay traces a movement from Salem/Zion outward to "the kings of the earth," and from soldiers who cannot act to worshippers who can. It hears a lion's covert behind סֻכּוֹ, notes that the psalm has no "we," and treats the heavenly verdict for "all the lowly of the earth" as widening who benefits.

**Strongest insights**
- "they did not find … their hands": the literal Hebrew as loss of bodily command. [poetic craft]
- Hosea 2:20's "bow and sword and war I will break" leads to the vulnerable lying safe, whereas in the psalm "it is the warriors who sleep." [Bible]
- The absent pronoun: Psalm 46 says "with us," but Psalm 76 "uses that same title… but supplies no 'us.'" [Bible]
- "שָׁלֵם, 'Salem,' is answered by שַׁלְּמוּ, 'pay.'" [poetic craft]
- Sennacherib's "bird in a cage" and tribute claim, set against the psalm's tribute to God. [beyond the Bible]

**Best writing**
"Salem is peaceful not because it contains nothing dangerous, but because the dangerous one who lives there confronts its attackers."
"Trakl keeps listening where Psalm 76 moves toward silence."

**Weak spots**
- "The poem gives the war machine several lines of equipment and gives God one utterance" is neat but overstated. God is also "radiant" and "majestic" (v. 5), and the equipment is sparse.
- The Assyrian paragraph opens "This is one reason…," but the link back to the single rebuke is loose.
- "For an instant, even the earth does not know what the judgment will mean for it" imports suspense the text does not state.
- The Austin paragraph restates what the essay has already shown about rebuke, verdict and vow; the philosopher's name adds little.
- The closing clause "or allowing it to call those weapons peace" strains to echo the Hosea contrast.
- "Yet its confidence has a cost worth noticing" is an abrupt pivot from v. 11 to Trakl.

**Claims worth checking**
- Trakl "after serving as a medical orderly": he served as a military pharmacist/medical officer and wrote *Grodek* during the war in 1914, shortly before his death, not afterward. "Orderly" and "after" are loose.
- "The word נָמוּ… is rare": the root נוּם is uncommon, but check the count if you rely on it.
- The Nahum "lions' den" is Nah. 2:12 (Hebrew numbering; 2:11 in English). The essay does not cite it; its word מְעוֹן also echoes the psalm's מְעוֹנָתוֹ (v. 3).
- The Hebrew verse numbers (Hos. 2:20, Ps. 46:8) run one ahead of most English Bibles (2:18, 46:7).
- The Jeremiah, Psalm 10, Exodus 15 and LXX-heading citations appear accurate.
