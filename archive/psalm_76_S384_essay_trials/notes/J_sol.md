**Governing idea**

The essay reads Psalm 76 as a song in which God disables military power to save the lowly, while denying the rescued a claim to victory by their own force.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**

It follows the poem from God’s dwelling in Zion through broken weapons, stunned fighters, a judgment heard across the earth, and a final summons to worship. The movement evokes safety without making battle or human retaliation its spectacle.

**Strongest insights**

- “Only afterward do the warriors appear, already overcome.” [poetic craft]
- “Psalm 76 seems to reverse the hiding place”: the covert associated with a predator in Psalm 10 becomes a place from which the lowly are defended. [Bible]
- “What the Assyrian calls a cage becomes God’s dwelling.” [beyond the Bible]
- “There is no ‘we,’ ‘us,’ or ‘our’ anywhere in the psalm”: the essay uses that absence to keep “all the lowly of the earth” open beyond Jerusalem. [poetic craft]
- “The Greek translation instead imagines a remnant keeping festival”: this gives the difficult final turn about fury a different possible sound. [tradition]

**Best writing**

“By the time Psalm 76 lets us look at the army, its soldiers cannot find their hands.” “The one before whom no warrior can stand rises on behalf of those least able to defend themselves.”

**Weak spots**

“Psalm 76 tells the other side” briefly sounds as though the psalm’s connection to Sennacherib has been settled, although the essay soon qualifies it. The standalone “You do not have to become as dangerous…” shifts from describing the poem to addressing the reader; it also smooths over some of the unease the essay later finds in the line about fury.

**Claims worth checking**

The citations “Hosea 2:20” and “Psalm 46:8” use verse numbering that differs in many English Bibles, where the passages appear at Hosea 2:18 and Psalm 46:7. The claim about what the psalm’s “first hearers knew” is an inference; the Assyrian annals cannot identify those hearers or date the psalm.
