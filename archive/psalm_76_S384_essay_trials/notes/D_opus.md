**Governing idea**
The essay argues that Psalm 76 gives every verb of force to God, leaves the attackers frozen and sleeping, and names no human victor, so that peace comes to mean human powerlessness before a divine lion.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**
It traces a widening arc from Zion to earth and from one morning to all time, with lion-den vocabulary turning God into the predator of predators. It sets the psalm beside Sennacherib's annals, Herodotus, Hosea and Byron. It hears the silence of v.9 as terror for aggressors and relief for the lowly.

**Strongest insights**
- "the verb is singular with a double subject, as if the whole cavalry were one sleeping body" [poetic craft]
- "There is one lair in the world whose lion hunts the hunters" (סֻכּוֹ via Ps 10:9, Jer 25:38) [Bible]
- "Peace, in Hosea, means Israel can close its eyes. Peace, here, means the attacker never opens his." [Bible]
- "Herodotus's mice attack exactly what verse 4 lists" [beyond the Bible]
- "both supply exactly the word the poet held back" (Rashi and Radak filling the missing "us") [tradition]

**Best writing**
"Psalm 76 is a war song in which no one fights." "The object of the only human action in the poem is the soldiers' own bodies, and they cannot locate them."

**Weak spots**
- "goes rigid and limp" contradicts itself.
- The claim of "the one transitive verb any human gets" is shaky, since נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם also takes an object.
- Linking Byron's "breath of his pride" (a horse's) to רוּחַ נְגִידִים is stretched.
- "Remarkably" and "most remarkable" repeat.
- "Anyone who has lived under a threat…" leans toward the sentimental.
- The closing triad ("fail, fall silent, or give thanks") leaves out the vowing and tribute-bearing the essay has just described.
- "the Song of Songs pairs the same two words" is ambiguous. The shared words are מְעֹנוֹת and הַרְרֵי, not טָרֶף.

**Claims worth checking**
- Rashi's Nebuchadnezzar example on v.11 and his "Hezekiah and his company" gloss: plausible, but I cannot confirm the exact wording.
- Malbim's horse-alertness remark and the long quotation from him: unverified.
- Shabbat 88a is correctly quoted, but the Talmud is discussing Sinai, not Assyria. The essay transplants it without saying so.
- Psalm 76 as the Sukkot "psalm of the day": this is a custom in some rites, not universal. The wording "in many communities" may overstate it.
- Isaiah's oracle is "before the siege": Isa 37:33 comes during the crisis, not strictly before it.
- The Byron lines, Herodotus 2.141, the LXX title and LXX v.11, Hosea 2:20, and the prism's "bird in a cage" and daughters all appear accurate.
