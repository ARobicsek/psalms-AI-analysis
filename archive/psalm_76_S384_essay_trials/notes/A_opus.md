**Governing idea**
Psalm 76 praises a deliverance with no human victor and no "we," so its protection reaches outward to "all the lowly of the earth" while its terror reaches outward to the earth's kings.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**
The essay traces the psalm's movement from one city to the whole world, with enemy strength simply switched off before anyone fights. It finds both relief and fear in this. Zion is a shelter and a predator's covert, the silence is sharp-edged, and v. 10 is the hinge that makes the power answerable to the vulnerable.

**Strongest insights**
- "This song about a deliverance in Judah contains no 'I,' 'we,' or 'us.'" It is set against Ps. 46's "with us… our stronghold." [Bible]
- Hosea's "bow and sword and war… lie down in safety," except "Here, though, it is the threatening warriors who sleep." [Bible]
- "The one successful action attributed to the enemy is a failure whose object is part of their own bodies." [poetic craft]
- "The call to 'pay,' שַׁלְּמוּ, sounds back to שָׁלֵם, 'Salem.'" [poetic craft]
- Sennacherib's annal "makes the king the acting subject and Jerusalem the trapped creature. Psalm 76 gives the action to God." [beyond the Bible]

**Best writing**
"They have not lost a contest of strength. Their strength has ceased to be available to them."

**Weak spots**
- The opening image is immediately qualified: "That is not quite what Psalm 76 says." The hedge slightly undercuts the hook.
- "Its own claim can be heard most clearly beside the empire's claim to have decided what happened" is somewhat abstract and muddy.
- The Paz excursus is attractive but long relative to its payoff ("Paz's second silence is not empty: it has an edge").
- The one-sentence interlude "For people who know what armed men can do…" restates the relief-and-fear theme already present.
- "Exodus calls God… 'a man of war'" is contrasted with this psalm breaking war. The point is suggestive, but the contrast is asserted more than developed.

**Claims worth checking**
- **Paz's nationality:** Octavio Paz is called "the Spanish poet." He was Mexican; "Spanish-language" would be accurate.
- **Rashi and Radak:** The attributions of the "lowly" to "Hezekiah and his companions" (Rashi) and to "Israel oppressed by the nations" (Radak) are plausible, but they should be confirmed against the commentaries.
- **Song of Songs 4:8:** The Hebrew there reads מִמְּעֹנוֹת, with a prefixed *mem*. The essay drops the prefix, a minor inaccuracy.
- **Hosea citation:** "Hos. 2:20" uses Hebrew verse numbering, which is 2:18 in English Bibles. This is correct, but readers may be confused.
- **Other citations:** The Jeremiah, Ps. 106:9, Exodus, and Ps. 46 references, the Sennacherib details, and the Greek heading and readings (the differently understood mountains; "festival" in v. 11) all appear accurate.
