**Governing idea**  
The essay reads Psalm 76 as a movement from God’s disabling of armed power to the fearful, active response demanded of those who remain.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**  
It begins with the Sukkot association, then uses the psalm’s dwelling words and Ted Hughes’s jaguar to picture Zion as a lair rather than a place that confines God. It traces a change from completed acts of defeat to commands for worship, arguing that the heavenly judgment serves “the lowly of the earth,” not national military glory.

**Strongest insights**  
- “a festival named for booths will take its psalm wherever it can find the noun” [tradition] — identifies the liturgical connection while questioning what the word means here.
- “There is no Israelite army in this poem” [Bible] — draws attention to who acts, and who does not, in the battle scene.
- “the hinge is audible in the liturgical pause” [poetic craft] — connects the second *Selah* with the shift in verbal forms and purpose.
- “a court that finally sits” [Bible] — makes the rescue of the lowly central to the judgment scene.
- “none of the men of valor could find their hands” [poetic craft] — sets up the closing contrast between paralysis and bringing tribute.

**Best writing**  
“The psalm's answer to imperial power is not a stronger empire. It is a court that finally sits.”

**Weak spots**  
“The floor plan” announces “two panels of roughly equal weight, each closed by סֶלָה,” but its own outline gives material after the second *Selah* a separate role; the proposed divisions are hard to follow. “God roars” extends the lion comparison beyond what the psalm explicitly says. Calling the defeated action “unrepeatable” asks more of the perfect verbs than their form alone establishes.

**Claims worth checking**  
“מְעוֹנָתוֹ is a den — specifically … the den of a big cat” narrows a dwelling word on the strength of a lion example. The claim that דִּין // מִשְׁפָּט is a “fixed courtroom idiom” whose object is *everywhere else* the same class of person needs wider evidence than the three passages given. “Four times the same sound closes the line” does not match the supplied psalm: the fear-root words occupy different positions. In verse 12, the covenant name precedes לַמּוֹרָא, rather than arriving “at that word.”
