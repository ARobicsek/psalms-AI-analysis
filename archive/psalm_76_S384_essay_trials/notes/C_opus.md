**Governing idea**
The essay reads Psalm 76 as a victory song with no battle and no "we": God disarms the enemy through sleep and breakage, and human hands reappear at the end only to pay tribute.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**
It traces a "camera" moving from country to hill to enemy camp to heaven. It hears Zion as a lion's lair whose lion "hunts predators," and argues that the grammar gives God every transitive verb. It closes on rescue felt as trembling that turns into stillness and obligation, with vows and tribute redirected from Assyria to Zion.

**Strongest insights**
- "it names one more thing God breaks, and that thing is מִלְחָמָה" [poetic craft]
- "all the men of valor did not find their hands… It says they could not locate them" [poetic craft]
- "In Hosea, disarmament lets Israel sleep… it is the invader who sleeps" [Bible]
- "The root of שָׁלֵם, Salem… returns as וְשַׁלְּמוּ, 'pay'" [poetic craft]
- "The ancient Greek translation… read the difficult verb… as 'keep festival'", tied to Zechariah 14 and Sukkot [tradition]

**Best writing**
"Psalm 76 keeps the Warrior and removes the war."
"It is safe, and it is not tame."

**Weak spots**
- "It is the same story told from inside Jerusalem" presents the LXX heading's interpretation as fact.
- "Every active verb that does something to something else belongs to God" sits awkwardly beside the later admission that humans receive "did not find." Verse 11's "human fury will acknowledge You" also gives humans a verb with God as its object.
- "each pause explains the other" is asserted rather than shown.
- "Anyone who has read accounts of combat terror will recognize the freeze" leans on the reader's assumed experience.
- "break the category He was named for" edges toward overstatement.
- The camera metaphor recurs in several sections.

**Claims worth checking**
- The essay says מֵהַרְרֵי appears "once more" only in Song 4:8. Num 23:7 has מֵהַרְרֵי־קֶדֶם, so the claim needs qualifying.
- "The only other place… the verb נָמוּ appears is Nahum" holds only for that exact form. The root נום occurs elsewhere, for example Isa 5:27 and Ps 121:3–4.
- The Song of the Sea "supplies the 'horse and chariot' and the word אַדִּיר." Exodus 15 has "horse and rider" and forms of אדר, with אַדִּירִים used of the waters. The link is looser than stated.
- The Black Obelisk as "the one picture… of an Israelite king": the kneeling figure is usually read as Jehu, though some think it is his envoy.
- The attributions to Radak (lion from the mountains), Malbim (horses wake at night; "source of fear") and Rashi (חגר as "hold back") are plausible but should be verified against the commentaries.
- Herodotus 2.141, Jer 25:38, Ps 10:9, Hos 2:20, Nah 3:18, Shabbat 88a, the LXX renderings, and the Byron quotation (1815) all appear accurate.
