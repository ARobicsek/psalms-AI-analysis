**Governing idea**  
The essay reads Psalm 76 as a victory song in which God alone wields force, leaving defeated attackers silent and the vulnerable able to rest.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**  
It traces a widening scene: broken weapons at Zion, a judgment heard from heaven, and an appeal to surrounding peoples and kings. It hears two kinds of quiet in the poem—the attackers’ helplessness and the relief of those they threatened—and treats the closing vows and tribute as the return of human action.

**Strongest insights**  
- “Ask of each verb who is doing it” [poetic craft]: this directs attention to the contrast between divine action and the warriors’ inability to act.
- “Peace, here, means the attacker never opens his” [Bible]: the comparison with Hosea makes the psalm’s sleep imagery newly unsettling.
- “It never says who was saved” [poetic craft]: the absence of “us” gives weight to “all the lowly of the earth.”
- “This reverses the Assyrian prism” [beyond the Bible]: the direction of tribute becomes part of the essay’s account of power.
- The reading of the earth’s hush as relief [tradition] draws out a possibility alongside fear.

**Best writing**  
“The same silence falls on everyone, and it means opposite things depending on where you stood.” “The last item on the list is not a weapon but war itself.”

**Weak spots**  
“A camp of corpses” and “the morning after” make the sleep imagery more definite than the psalm does; it names neither deaths nor a time of day. “Tonic immobility” vividly extends the predator imagery, but risks making a physiological explanation out of a poetic comparison. The closing claim that people may only “fail, fall silent, or give thanks” leaves out the vows and gifts the essay has just discussed.

**Claims worth checking**  
“Every version agrees” on “a single night” and “weapons that stopped working” overstates the agreement: Sennacherib’s annals do not report either. The quoted Greek rendering “the remnant of wrath will keep festival” also needs checking; the standard Septuagint text uses *enthymion*, usually rendered “thought,” rather than “wrath.”
