**Governing idea**

The essay reads Psalm 76 as a victory song in which God disables war, while the people who benefit are named broadly as “the lowly of the earth,” not as an army or nation.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**

It follows a movement from Zion’s broken weapons through divine judgment to vows and tribute, paying particular attention to who acts and who falls still. Its closing paragraphs imagine how that judgment might sound to people who live with wars they cannot control.

**Strongest insights**

- “No one in the poem ever says ‘we.’” [poetic craft] This makes the absence of a collective speaker something to notice throughout.
- “Three things you can hold, then one you cannot.” [poetic craft] The observation makes the list of weapons ending in “war” vivid.
- “In Hosea it is Israel who lies down, safe at last. In Psalm 76 the sleepers are the invaders.” [Bible] The contrast gives the recurring sleep imagery a different force.
- “The Song at the Sea ends where this psalm starts.” [Bible] This neatly states the essay’s proposed reversal of victory and sanctuary.
- Rashi and Radak “fill” the gap left by “all the lowly of the earth.” [tradition] Their readings clarify what the essay thinks the psalm leaves open.

**Best writing**

“Most people who have ever lived had no army and no say in the wars that crossed their fields. They heard of armies the way one hears of floods.”

**Weak spots**

“God’s verbs are all acts of force” compresses actions such as making God known and saving the lowly. “Human beings get their agency back only as worship” likewise makes a stark rule of a coda that also contains fury and tribute. “The only people ever addressed directly” needs “human people”: the poem directly addresses God several times.

**Claims worth checking**

“The poet … borrowed it from Hosea” treats a verbal parallel as proof of direction of borrowing. Verse 7 uses a verb related to sleep, not the noun *tardemah* named in the essay. The account of Sennacherib’s army being found dead “outside Jerusalem” is more geographically specific than the biblical account itself.
