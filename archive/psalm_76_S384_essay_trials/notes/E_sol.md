**Governing idea** — The essay reads Psalm 76 as a victory song that removes human military prowess from view so that God’s judgment, and the lowly it protects, come into focus.

**What it says the poem does and evokes** — It traces a movement from Zion and a disabled army to a heavenly verdict, the deliverance of the lowly, and a summons to worship. Its comparisons with other biblical passages evoke both a dangerous divine presence and an older pattern of rescue by God’s rebuke.

**Strongest insights**
- “No one from Judah fires an arrow, raises a shield, or even says ‘we.’” [poetic craft] The essay notices whose actions and voices are absent.
- “Here the dangerous inhabitant of the covert rises on the poor person’s side.” [Bible] The lion-lair passages give the Zion imagery an unsettling edge.
- “In Psalm 76, it is the armed men who sleep.” [Bible] The comparison with Hosea makes the reversal vivid.
- “A remembered rescue has become a way to speak about the next threat as well.” [tradition] This connects the Assyrian reading to the psalm’s unnamed enemy and forward-looking ending.

**Best writing** — “The psalm shows no countercharge. Its decisive weapon is God’s ‘rebuke’ (גַּעֲרָתְךָ): one utterance against an entire arsenal.”

**Weak spots** — “The opening confines God’s reputation” sounds stronger than the observation that Judah and Israel are named first. The claim that “all” the lowly cannot be narrowed to Israel because there is no “our” leaves little room for the historical reading the essay has just acknowledged. “A person facing an army has no use for assurances that the defenders will fight bravely” makes the contrast with divine action more absolute than it needs to be.

**Claims worth checking** — “The Song of the Sea begins with the defeated horse and ends with a place for God to dwell” describes its broad movement, but the dwelling appears in Exodus 15:17; verse 18 ends with God’s reign. The attributions to Rashi and Radak are worth checking against their commentaries, since the essay does not quote the passages behind them.
