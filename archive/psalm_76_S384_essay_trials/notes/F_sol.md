**Governing idea** — The essay reads Psalm 76 as a victory song that gives God, rather than an army or king, the power to end violence and save the lowly.

**What it says the poem does and evokes** — It follows the poem from broken weapons and sleeping soldiers through a judgment heard from heaven to vows and tribute. Its historical and literary parallels bring out the contrast between royal displays of power and the psalm’s frightening quiet.

**Strongest insights**
- “a victory song in which nobody fights” [poetic craft] — a way of noticing what the account leaves out.
- “The line breaks the category itself” [Bible] — the list moves from breakable weapons to “war.”
- “The one transitive verb humans get before verse 11 is a failure to find” [poetic craft] — its account of the soldiers’ helplessness.
- “A judge rises in order to rescue” [Bible] — it keeps the purpose of judgment in view.
- “the tribute travels the other way” [beyond the Bible] — it frames the contrast with Sennacherib’s account.

**Best writing** — “What remains is a field of broken weapons, a camp of men who will not wake, a sentence spoken from heaven, and a silence.” “A judge rises in order to rescue.”

**Weak spots** — “From verse 11 on they describe what will keep happening” smooths over the commands to vow and pay in verse 12. “The universalism of verse 10 … is built into the grammar of the whole psalm” presses the absence of “us” quite far: the poem also locates God in Judah and Zion and addresses worshipers as those whose God he is.

**Claims worth checking** — The assertion that the psalm takes Isaiah’s “same three terms, there, arrow and shield” needs qualification: the psalm’s Hebrew has “bow,” not Isaiah’s word for “arrow.” “Three peoples remembered it as a night on which an army’s weapons failed without a fight” does not fit the Assyrian account as the essay presents it; that account claims conquest and tribute. Herodotus’s mice story may be relevant, but its identification with the events behind the psalm is uncertain. The stated connection between the Greek rendering of verse 11 and the psalm’s recitation on Sukkot also needs a source.
