**Governing idea:** The essay argues that Psalm 76 keeps human military prowess off the stage entirely, so that God's overthrow of force becomes a judgment aimed at saving "all the lowly of the earth," a safety no single community may claim as its own.

**What it says the poem does and evokes:** It traces the poem moving from Zion's shelter, through broken weapons and paralysed warriors, to a heavenly verdict and a summons to worship. It hears a predator's lair behind "covert" and "mountains of prey," an inversion of Assyrian siege-and-tribute, and a spreading vocabulary of fear. It also notes the absence of any "we."

**Strongest insights:**
- "Only then does the poem say whom the judgment is for." [poetic craft]
- "a wicked man waits 'like a lion in his covert'… Psalm 76 seems to reverse the hiding place" [Bible]
- "What the Assyrian calls a cage becomes God's dwelling; tribute goes not to the imperial king" [beyond the Bible]
- "There is no 'we,' 'us,' or 'our' anywhere in the psalm." [poetic craft]
- "Hosea's next promise is that his people will lie down safely. Psalm 76 puts the aggressors to sleep instead." [Bible]

**Best writing:** "By the time Psalm 76 lets us look at the army, its soldiers cannot find their hands." / "The very part of the body on which a fighter relies has become unavailable to him."

**Weak spots:**
- The one-line paragraph "You do not have to become as dangerous as the people who threaten you to be worth saving" shifts abruptly into a homiletic second person.
- The Su Shi comparison does little beyond "grants the fighters no such interval of glory," and the long quotation outweighs the payoff.
- The Aeschylus parallel is heavily qualified by the essay itself, so its contribution is thin.
- The phrase "a place from which their pursuer must beware" is grammatically awkward.
- In "The earth that heard the verdict and the lowly whom it sheltered now includes its rulers," the referent of "it" is unclear.
- The claim that "the city's name returns as something worshippers must do" leans hard on a sound-echo.
- "Easy to follow and strange to experience" is generic.

**Claims worth checking:**
- The attribution of "Hezekiah and his company" to Rashi on v. 10 is plausible (וסיעתו), but it should be verified against the text.
- "Single 'rebuke'": the Hebrew does not say "single."
- The citations check out as far as I can tell: the LXX's ἐν εἰρήνῃ, its title "πρὸς τὸν Ἀσσύριον" and its festival reading of v. 11; Song 4:8; Ps 10:9; Exod 15:3 and 15:19; Hos 2:20 (Hebrew numbering, 2:18 in English); Ps 46:8 (Hebrew numbering); the Taylor Prism "bird in a cage" and the date 701; Su Shi in 1082; *Eumenides* in 458 BCE; and YHWH occurring only in v. 12.
