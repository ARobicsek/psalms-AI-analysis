**Governing idea**

The essay reads Psalm 76 as a victory song that removes human combat from the scene and gives the aftermath to the lowly and to worship.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**

It follows the poem outward from Zion to the whole earth, noting the absence of a narrated fight or a named national beneficiary. It evokes an army stopped by a rebuke, then sets that stillness beside the Song of the Sea, Hosea, and the frozen hand in *Don Giovanni*.

**Strongest insights**

- “none of the men of valor could find their hands” [poetic craft] draws attention to how the warriors’ only attempted action is negated.
- “Where Exodus calls God a warrior, here God breaks מִלְחָמָה” [Bible] makes the last item in the weapons list matter.
- “the one lair whose lion hunts the predators” [Bible] connects the dwelling in Zion with the rescue of the lowly.
- “Psalm 76:7 says ‘God of Jacob’ and stops” [Bible] brings the missing “us” into focus through Psalm 46.
- “the hand that held his sword freezes” [beyond the Bible] gives the psalm’s immobilized fighters a theatrical counterpart.

**Best writing**

“That word closes a list that runs from arrows to shield to sword, and it is the one item on the list that cannot be held in a hand.” “God does not even extend a hand.”

**Weak spots**

“[I]t never says who was delivered” needs qualification: verse 10 does say “all the lowly of the earth,” though it names no nation or person. “Every verb in the poem that reaches out and acts upon something belongs to Him” is broader than the essay’s later account of people praising, making vows, and bringing tribute. “They do not wake” makes the warriors’ sleep more final than the cited verses explicitly do.

**Claims worth checking**

The opening’s “line of kings bearing gifts” joins two groups the psalm does not explicitly equate: those bringing tribute in verse 12 and the kings in verse 13. The account of Radak changing his interpretation from Assyria to Gog, and the note about seventh-day Passover recitation, are specific claims worth checking against his commentary and the relevant liturgical custom.
