**Governing idea**

Psalm 76 gives a threatened community courage by showing God disabling armed power, saving the lowly, and asking people to respond without claiming the victory as their own.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**

The essay follows the poem from Zion’s broken weapons through stunned soldiers and a silent earth to vows, tribute, and rulers held in awe. It imagines what that sequence offers someone facing an army: a moment when strength need not be met with strength. It also notes that the psalm’s silence does not tell us everything defeated people experience.

**Strongest insights**

- [Bible] “God inhabits the covert”: the comparison with Psalm 10 turns a lion’s hiding place from a threat to the poor into a possible image of their protection.
- [poetic craft] “The near-echo of *’ad-DIYR* and *’ab-BIY-rēy*” makes divine majesty and the warriors’ might meet in sound before the warriors fall.
- [tradition] “competing claims that could surround a city”: the Greek heading, Nahum, and Sennacherib’s account frame the psalm without fixing its date.
- [Bible] “supplies no ‘us’”: the contrast with Psalm 46 brings out how little credit Psalm 76 gives its singers.
- [poetic craft] “שָׁלֵם, ‘Salem,’ is answered by שַׁלְּמוּ, ‘pay’”: the essay hears a link between the opening city and the closing obligation.

**Best writing**

“The poem gives the war machine several lines of equipment and gives God one utterance.” “Psalm 76 is a song sung from danger, not a report from the dead.”

**Weak spots**

“For an instant, even the earth does not know what the judgment will mean for it” gives the earth an uncertainty the text does not state. “Only after that silence do people speak and act again” also presses the sequence: verse 12 commands vows rather than depicting people making them. The long *Grodek* quotation makes its caution about silence at some distance from the psalm.

**Claims worth checking**

“Salem is an old name for Jerusalem” presents a traditional biblical identification as a straightforward historical naming fact. The closing suggestion that “curbing” can evoke cutting grapes merits a lexical check; no vine appears in the psalm.
