**Governing idea**  
The essay reads Psalm 76 as a song in which God stops armed aggression for the sake of the lowly, while leaving the safety and fear of that intervention in tension.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**  
It traces a widening view from God’s dwelling in Zion to judgment heard by the earth and feared by its kings. The helplessness of the warriors evokes relief for those they threaten, but the earth’s frightened quiet prevents that relief from becoming simple ease.

**Strongest insights**  
- “none of the men of valor found their hands” [Bible] — Defeat is imagined as strength becoming unavailable, not merely being overmatched.
- “from things soldiers carry to the activity those things enable” [poetic craft] — The list of broken weapons ends with war itself.
- “does not add ‘our’” [tradition] — Set beside Psalm 46 and the named traditional readings, the missing communal pronoun helps explain the essay’s attention to “all the lowly.”
- “Jerusalem the trapped creature” [beyond the Bible] — Sennacherib’s boast gives the essay a contrasting account of who acts and who receives tribute.
- “The quiet may hold the end of fighting” [poetic craft] — The essay keeps the earth’s silence tied to its fear rather than treating it as uncomplicated peace.

**Best writing**  
“They have not lost a contest of strength. Their strength has ceased to be available to them.”

**Weak spots**  
“There is no ‘we’” reprises an absence already developed at length. “The one successful action attributed to the enemy is a failure” makes the point about helplessness in a slightly strained paradox. The long Paz quotation gives considerable space to the distinction between frightening silence and ease, which the surrounding prose also states.

**Claims worth checking**  
“Hos. 2:20” follows Hebrew verse numbering; readers using many English Bibles will find the cited promise at Hosea 2:18. The precise summaries of Rashi’s and Radak’s readings of “the lowly” would be worth checking against their comments on verse 10.
