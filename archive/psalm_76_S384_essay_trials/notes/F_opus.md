**Governing idea**
The essay argues that Psalm 76 is a victory song without combat: its grammar gives all force to God and withholds any "we," so it reads less as national triumph than as disarmament that ends in praise.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**
It says the poem opens in the aftermath, moves from broken weapons to sleeping soldiers to a courtroom verdict and silence, then turns to a future in which human fury becomes thanks and tribute. It hears the psalm as a reversal of Assyrian royal boasting, with the tribute travelling toward Zion, and as the relief of "waking to find that the thing you feared is asleep."

**Strongest insights**
- "turns the prophecy into a report": the psalm reuses Isaiah's "there," arrow and shield. [Bible]
- "ends on an abstraction: וּמִלְחָמָה… The line breaks the category itself." [poetic craft]
- "The one transitive verb humans get before verse 11 is a failure to find, and its object is their own hands." [poetic craft]
- The reassignment of Assyrian *melammu* to God through נאור, נורא and מורא. [beyond the Bible]
- Hosea's "bow and sword and war… lie down in safety" reversed, so that the sleepers are the aggressors. [Bible]

**Best writing**
"It is a victory song in which nobody fights."
"Its God does not make war. He breaks it."

**Weak spots**
- Some images strain: Resheph "reduced here to kindling," "as though the noise of all combat had been switched off," "Then the camera pulls back."
- The "your own arm is gone" dream simile pushes the idiom further than it needs to go.
- The pastoral paragraph ("For anyone who has lived under a threat…") shifts register abruptly.
- "Its God does not make war" sits uneasily beside the psalm's anger and rebuke (vv. 8, 11), which the essay does not address.
- The claim that universalism is "built into the grammar" puts heavy weight on an absence. Verse 12 does address a congregation ("your God").

**Claims worth checking**
- **"Two… prisms from Nineveh":** The Taylor Prism came from Nineveh, but the Chicago (Oriental Institute) prism was purchased on the antiquities market, and its findspot is not securely known.
- **Radak's change of mind:** The claim that he "first agreed and later changed his mind" toward Gog needs a source, since it describes a sequence rather than a single stated view.
- **Malbim's "lexical companion work":** The sleeper/rebuke inversion should be located in the actual text.
- **b. Shabbat 88a:** The passage does exist, but it reads the verse as referring to Sinai, not to a war. The essay does not say this.
- **Rashi's knife-nick reading:** Both of Rashi's readings of תחגר, including the Mishnaic knife-nick sense, are worth confirming.
- **Sukkot:** The liturgical link to the festival and the causal suggestion that the Septuagint's "keep festival" explains it are speculative, and the essay itself says only "may be."
- **"A line found nowhere else in the Bible":** This is plausible for the whole phrase, but it is worth verifying.
