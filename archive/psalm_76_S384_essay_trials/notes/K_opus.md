**Governing idea**
The essay argues that Psalm 76 celebrates war undone rather than a battle won, and shows this through its grammar: God performs every act of force, and humans can only fail, fall still, or give thanks.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**
It reads the psalm as a deliberate reversal of Hosea 2:20 and the Song at the Sea: the "man of war" becomes the breaker of war, and the sleep of safety becomes the invader's stupor. It stresses the missing "we" and the unnamed beneficiaries ("all the lowly of the earth"). From that it hears the poem addressed to everyone who has suffered wars without waging them.

**Strongest insights**
- "no one in the poem ever says 'we'" (the contrast with Psalm 46's עִמָּנוּ) [Bible]
- "In Hosea, peace means Israel can sleep. Here, peace means the aggressor does not wake." [Bible]
- "The man of war has become the breaker of war." [Bible]
- "The poem's verbs give each reading exactly half its footing" (past verbs support Sennacherib, future verbs support Gog) [tradition / poetic craft]
- "Most people who have ever lived had no army… They heard of armies the way one hears of floods." [beyond the Bible]

**Best writing**
"Rage, the thing that drives history in every other war poem, is given one last job in this one: to say thank you."

**Weak spots**
- "The geography widens at every stage: Judah, Zion…" Zion is narrower than Judah, so the sequence contradicts the claim.
- "the argument writes itself" and "Both absences are the point" read as announcements rather than demonstration.
- "the only war poem whose principal casualty is a noun" is witty but sweeping.
- "In both passages, sleep follows." Hosea's וְהִשְׁכַּבְתִּים means "lie down," not sleep, so the parallel is looser than stated.
- "The commentators know a gap in a sentence when they see one" is slightly arch.
- Listing תַּחְגֹּר, an imperfect, among God's acts of force sits awkwardly beside the later claim of a clean past-to-future switch.

**Claims worth checking**
- **Passover custom.** The statement that communities recite Psalm 76 on the seventh day of Passover is doubtful. Lists following the Vilna Gaon usually assign Psalm 76 to Sukkot (plausibly because of סֻכּוֹ, v. 3) and Psalm 114 to Passover.
- **Radak on Gog.** Radak is presented as reading the psalm as the war of Gog. He may instead read it primarily as Sennacherib and mention Gog as another view. The unpointed Radak quotation should also be verified.
- **Rashi quotation.** The wording לְהוֹשִׁיעַ חִזְקִיָּהוּ וְסִיעָתוֹ is plausible but should be confirmed.
- **Malbim.** Attaching Malbim to the Sennacherib reading also needs confirming.
- **Battle-eve singing.** "Sung on the eve of real battles" is unsourced.
- **Verified references.** Psalm 46:8/12, Hosea 2:20, Jeremiah 25:38, and Exodus 15:3, 6, 11, 16–17 are cited accurately (Hebrew numbering).
