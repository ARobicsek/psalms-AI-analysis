**Governing idea**

The essay reads Psalm 76 as a victory won by stopping violence, with the poem’s attention moving from a defeated army to the lowly who are saved and the worship that follows.

**What it says the poem does and evokes**

It traces a movement from Judah to Zion, from broken weapons and stunned soldiers to a quiet earth and a procession bearing tribute. Its comparisons with Herodotus, other biblical texts, Byron, and Jewish readings evoke a rescue that feels less like triumph than fear gradually giving way to stillness.

**Strongest insights**

- “no battle in it, and no ‘we’” [poetic craft]: the missing battle and first-person voice shape how the essay hears the victory.
- “its lion hunts predators” [Bible]: the Zion-as-lair image reverses Psalm 10’s lion stalking the poor.
- “The one transitive verb they receive is ‘did not find’” [poetic craft]: the soldiers’ missing hands make their helplessness stranger than ordinary defeat.
- “The city of peace is answered with payment.” [poetic craft]: the essay connects Salem with the command to pay vows.
- “The leftover of the armies ends up as pilgrims.” [tradition]: the Greek reading and Sukkot setting give the ending a festival scene.

**Best writing**

“The listener comes to Zion expecting a sanctuary and is told it is also a hunting ground.” “What you feel first is not triumph but a shaking that slowly turns into quiet, and then the question of what you owe.”

**Weak spots**

The suggestion that the soldiers’ sleep “belongs to them for good” goes beyond what the psalm explicitly says. “When human hands finally reappear” completes the essay’s imagery, but hands are not mentioned again in the final verses. “Anyone who has read accounts of combat terror” invokes accounts it does not name.

**Claims worth checking**

“It is the same story told from inside Jerusalem”: the Greek heading names “the Assyrian,” but that alone does not establish identity with Herodotus’s mouse story. “The one picture we have of an Israelite king from his own century” needs qualification: the Black Obelisk names Jehu, but whether its kneeling figure is Jehu himself or a representative is debated.
