## OBSERVATION 1

**Type:** PATTERN

**Primary verse(s):** whole psalm (esp. vv. 3–9)

**Claim:** The psalm leaves out almost every material noun of the biblical and Ugaritic storm repertoire. There is no rain (גשם/מטר), no cloud (ענן/עב), no wind (רוח), no hail (ברד), and no lightning-word (ברק). Of the storm's physical stuff it keeps only water (vv. 3, 10) and fire (v. 7), and the fire is something the voice *hews*. Two absences the dossier records separately, the missing rain (Amzallag) and the avoided ברק (micro note on v. 7), turn out to be parts of one consistent reduction: the storm becomes almost entirely acoustic, a qol.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the absences are a matter of counting; reading them as a deliberate program is interpretive but economical.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- None of גשם, מטר, ענן, רוח, ברד, ברק occurs anywhere in Ps 29 (the psalm text).
- Ps 18:14, in the concordance: וַיַּרְעֵם בַּשָּׁמַיִם יְהֹוָה... יִתֵּן קֹלוֹ בָּרָד וְגַחֲלֵי־אֵשׁ. The same thunder verb comes there with hail and coals.
- KTU 1.4 V 8–9 (deep research): Baal gives "his voice in the clouds… lightning". KTU 1.101: Baal's seven lightnings.
- Amzallag 2021 (deep research): rain is never mentioned. Micro note on v. 7: ברק is avoided.

**Payoff (what clicks):** Amzallag's "missing rain" and the avoided ברק stop being two oddities and become one choice. The Ugaritic voice-plus-cloud-plus-lightning is cut back to voice alone. That is why the sevenfold qol can stand in for Baal's seven *lightnings*: the visual storm has been turned into the audible one.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The dossier treats each absence on its own (rain in one source, ברק in another). It never observes that the whole meteorological lexicon is absent.

## OBSERVATION 2

**Type:** PATTERN

**Primary verse(s):** whole psalm (vv. 1–2 vs. 3–11)

**Claim:** All four instances of the divine name in vv. 1–2 are ליהוה. There YHWH is the recipient of giving and bowing. After v. 2 the name never takes a preposition again: in all fourteen remaining instances it is bare יהוה, either the subject or the genitive in קול יהוה. The lamed of the recipient comes back only once, attached to the people (לְעַמּוֹ, v. 11).

**Confidence:** ESTABLISHED — exact counts (computed table: ליהוה 4x in vv. 1–2; יהוה 14x in vv. 3–11).

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Computed distributional facts: ליהוה 4x, all in vv. 1–2. Bare יהוה 14x, in vv. 3–5 and 7–11.
- v. 11: יְהֹוָה עֹז לְעַמּוֹ יִתֵּן.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The familiar 'oz inclusio (up in v. 1, down in v. 11) shows up in the grammar of the name itself. Once the gods have "given," the name is never again on the receiving end. The only receiver who remains is the people.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The inclusio is standard. Tracing it through the prepositional behaviour of all 18 names is not. This is real but modest texture.

## OBSERVATION 3

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 1–2, 11

**Claim:** The micro notes treat the switch from יהב (v. 1) to נתן (v. 11) as a deliberate change of root that marks the reversal of direction. BDB shows that יהב survives in Hebrew *only in the imperative*. A finite "YHWH will give" therefore had to use נתן, so the asymmetry is imposed by the lexicon. The real choice lies at the top: הָבוּ was preferred to the available imperative תְּנוּ, the one Ps 68:35 uses (תְּנוּ עֹז לֵאלֹהִים). Perhaps it was chosen for its tribute register ("hand over," Gen 47:16; the leech's הַב הַב, Prov 30:15), and also for its sound, since the v/w consonant with a back vowel runs hā-VŪ … kā-VŌDH wā-ʿŌZ.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the morphological restriction is lexicon fact; the sound motive is conjecture.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- BDB entry on יהב (lexicon section): "only Imv."
- Ps 68:35 תנו עז לאלהים (micro note on v. 11; concordance).
- Phonetic transcription of v. 1: hā-VŪ lay-hō-WĀH … kā-VŌDH wā-ʿŌZ.

**Payoff (what clicks):** It corrects an overread in the dossier. It also moves the poet's marked choice to the place where a choice actually existed, and that choice fits both the vassal-tribute reading of v. 1 and the sound of the line.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The dossier presents the root switch as meaningful without checking that the downward verb had no alternative.

## OBSERVATION 4

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** whole psalm (vv. 1–2, 3–9, 11) and the Amidah

**Claim:** The rabbinic derivations, scattered across several sugyot, together lay the Amidah over the psalm frame by frame. Vv. 1–2 yield the first three blessings (Megillah 17b). V. 11 yields the closing peace-blessing (Megillah 18a: שים שלום after the priestly blessing because "the Holy One's blessing is peace," citing 29:11; y. Ber. 2:4: "all benedictions end with peace," citing 29:11). The 18 divine names yield 18 blessings, and some middle blessings are keyed to the theophany itself (y. Ber. 2:4: the ninth to v. 5, the fifteenth to v. 10). Read together, the psalm's envelope (summons, theophany, blessing of shalom) becomes the Amidah's envelope (praise, petitions, peace).

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — every derivation is attested; reading them as a single architecture is a synthesis.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Megillah 17b / RH 32a (reception; Torah Temimah v. 1).
- Megillah 18a and y. Ber. 2:4:14 (Torah Temimah v. 11; reception).
- b. Ber. 28b; y. Ber. 2:4:10, 2:4:13 (reception).
- y. Ber. 4:3:7: the seven Shabbat blessings correspond to the seven qolot.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The "18 names = 18 blessings" tradition stops being an isolated number game. The rabbis heard the whole prayer's shape, opening praise and closing shalom, in Ps 29's inclusio.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** Each derivation is usually cited alone (Rashi gives only the 18). No source in the dossier puts the opening and closing derivations together into a frame-to-frame map.

## OBSERVATION 5

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** whole psalm, in its Kabbalat Shabbat position

**Claim:** In Kabbalat Shabbat, Ps 29 follows Pss 95–99, and those psalms between them hold Ps 29's components in dispersed form. Ps 95:3 has the king over all gods. Ps 96 has the הבו summons, now addressed to peoples. Ps 97:7 has the gods bowing (השתחוו לו כל אלהים), and 97:4 has lightning and the earth writhing (ותחל הארץ). Ps 98:7 has the roaring sea (ירעם הים). Ps 99:1 has the king and the trembling peoples. The service thus arrives at Ps 29 as a compressed source after its expansions, and this reverses the historical direction of borrowing. Perhaps it is less a break in the sequence (Sacks) than its summation.

**Confidence:** CONJECTURE — the dispersion is verifiable; no documented intention by the Safed compilers.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Liturgical section: Ps 29 is said in Kabbalat Shabbat in all rites, after Pss 95–99. Sacks: "breaks the sequence of the previous psalms (95–99)."
- Computed table: השתחוו also in Ps 97:7.
- Concordance: Ps 98:7 יִרְעַם הַיָּם.
- General scholarship (text of Pss 95:3; 97:4; 99:1).

**Payoff (what clicks):** It explains why a psalm that "breaks" the YHWH-malakh run still feels like its climax. Each preceding psalm has rehearsed one piece of it.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The usual explanations (six weekdays; seven qolot) say nothing about Ps 29's lexical relation to the five psalms it follows.

## OBSERVATION 6

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 1–2, 4, 8–9 (with Ps 96)

**Claim:** Ps 96 does more than swap בני אלים for משפחות עמים. It takes Ps 29's theophany verbs from YHWH and the landscape and gives them to worshippers. The writhing of desert and hinds (יחיל/יחולל) becomes "tremble before Him, all the earth" (96:9, חילו). The divine thunder over the sea becomes the sea's own joyful thunder (96:11, ירעם הים, beside "let the heavens rejoice"). The hadar and 'oz of the voice and frame are installed in the sanctuary (96:6, עז ותפארת במקדשו). And קול is gone entirely. Zevachim 116a arrives at the same humanizing move from the other end: it makes the nations' kings, "in their palaces," the speakers of v. 9c's "Glory!"

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the redistributions are textual facts; reading them as a systematic adaptation is interpretive.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Related-psalms section: shared roots רעם (96:11), הדר (96:6), חיל (96:9), עז (96:6). Contiguous shared phrases (96:7–9).
- Zevachim 116a:22 (reception): kings "in their palaces… said song, as it is stated: ובהיכלו כולו אומר כבוד."
- Micro note: the 3ms suffix of היכלו is ambiguous.

**Payoff (what clicks):** Ps 96's single famous substitution turns out to be one of several coordinated moves. And the midrash's distributive reading of היכלו carries out, at v. 9c, the same domestication of the "gods" into nations that Ps 96 carried out at v. 1.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** Commentators note the בני אלים substitution, not the reassignment of חיל, רעם and עז, and not its convergence with Zevachim's reading of the answering choir.

## OBSERVATION 7

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 1–2 (LXX)

**Claim:** The two famous LXX deviations in vv. 1–2 both correspond to the one line Ps 96 *adds* to this summons, שְׂאוּ מִנְחָה וּבֹאוּ לְחַצְרוֹתָיו (96:8): bring an offering, come into his courts. These are the doublet "bring to the Lord young rams" (υἱοὺς κριῶν), which makes the addressees into the offering, and "in his holy court" (αὐλῇ). This suggests that the Greek of Ps 29 has been pulled toward the sacrificial-procession form of the summons attested in Ps 96 and 1 Chr 16.

**Confidence:** CONJECTURE — the correspondence is clear; the direction of influence cannot be proven.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- LXX v. 1 doublet and v. 2 ἐν αὐλῇ ἁγίᾳ αὐτοῦ (psalm text; micro notes).
- Ps 96:8, in the related-psalms section and the concordance under הבו: שְׂאֽוּ־מִנְחָה וּבֹאוּ לְחַצְרוֹתָיו.
- Deep research attributes the "court" reading to Ps 96:9, which in MT itself reads בהדרת קדש.

**Payoff (what clicks):** Two unrelated-looking Greek oddities get one explanation, and the dossier's attribution of "court" to 96:9 is refined, since the courts actually sit in 96:8.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The rams doublet and the court reading are always explained separately (אילים/אלים confusion; ה/ח and ד/צ interchange).

## OBSERVATION 8

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 1, 6

**Claim:** בְּנֵי אֵלִים (v. 1, bə-NĒY 'ē-LIYM) and בֶן־רְאֵמִים (v. 6, ven-rə-'ē-MIYM) are sound-echoes: "son(s) of" followed by a plural in 'ē-…-IYM. Consonantally, אלים is also the plural spelling of "rams." Ps 114:4, 6 shows that "rams" plus "sons of [the flock]" was the standard stock for skipping mountains (הֶהָרִים רָקְדוּ כְאֵילִים גְּבָעוֹת כִּבְנֵי־צֹאן), with the same verb רקד as v. 6's וירקידם. Perhaps, then, the gods summoned in v. 1 return in v. 6 as an undertone: frisking young bulls. That would be a demotion carried out by sound (El, after all, bore the title "Bull").

**Confidence:** CONJECTURE — sound and topos are real; intent is unprovable.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Phonetic transcriptions of vv. 1 and 6.
- BDB "ram" entry: pl. "אֵילִים (אֵילִם, אֵלִים)" (lexicon).
- Concordance under בני אלים returns Ps 114:4, 6.
- LXX υἱοὺς κριῶν.
- General scholarship: Ugaritic El's epithet ṯr il, "Bull El."
- Micro note v. 6: calf ↔ Baal bull iconography.

**Payoff (what clicks):** It explains why the LXX translator could hear "rams" in v. 1 at all, since the poem itself invites the animal reading by v. 6. It also links the opening council to the central comic verse, which Diehl–Diesel–Wagner identify as the concentric axis.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The rams doublet is treated as a scribal error. No one in the dossier connects it to v. 6's ben re'emim or to Ps 114's skipping-ram stock.

## OBSERVATION 9

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 2, 8

**Claim:** R. Joshua b. Levi's al tikrei, "do not read בְּהַדְרַת but בְּחֶרְדַת" (Ber. 30b; y. Ber. 5:1), looks arbitrary, but it has inner-biblical support he does not cite. Ps 96:9 places בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ immediately before חִילוּ מִפָּנָיו, "tremble before Him." Ps 29 itself echoes v. 2's קדש in v. 8's קדש, where the verb is יָחִיל. In both texts, the holiness phrase sits next to trembling.

**Confidence:** CONJECTURE — the adjacency facts are solid; that they underlie the midrash is suggestion.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Ber. 30b:18 and y. Ber. 5:1:7 (reception; Torah Temimah v. 2).
- Ps 96:9 (concordance, הדרת קדש search): הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ לַיהֹוָה בְּהַדְרַת־קֹדֶשׁ חִילוּ מִפָּנָיו.
- Computed: קדש 2x here (vv. 2, 8). יחיל 2x in v. 8.

**Payoff (what clicks):** It turns a homiletical swap into a reading the psalm and its parallel already invite: approaching holiness means trembling, as the landscape does.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The al tikrei is usually presented as free derash. The Ps 96:9 adjacency and the qodesh/Qadesh/yahil chain are not cited in its support.

## OBSERVATION 10

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 3–4, 11

**Claim:** Ps 29 belongs to a small cluster of texts that join a thundering voice to a transfer of 'oz. 1 Sam 2:10 has עָלָו בַּשָּׁמַיִם יַרְעֵם … וְיִתֶּן־עֹז לְמַלְכּוֹ. Ps 68:34–36 has יִתֵּן בְּקוֹלוֹ קוֹל עֹז, then תְּנוּ עֹז לֵאלֹהִים, then נֹתֵן עֹז… לָעָם. Against these, Ps 29 does two pointed things. It withholds 'oz from the voice: the voice is בַּכֹּחַ and בֶּהָדָר, not קוֹל עֹז. And it routes the 'oz to the *people*, not to a king, keeping מֶלֶךְ for YHWH alone (v. 10), even under a Davidic superscription.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the parallels are contiguous and certain; the reading of the differences as pointed is interpretive.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- 1 Sam 2:10 (concordance, רעם search): …יַרְעֵם… וְיִתֶּן־עֹז לְמַלְכּוֹ.
- Ps 68:35–36 (micro note v. 11; concordance).
- Ps 68:34 הֵן יִתֵּן בְּקוֹלוֹ קוֹל עֹז (general scholarship, text of the Psalter).
- Micro note v. 4: "voice qualified by koah, not 'oz."

**Payoff (what clicks):** The dossier's observation that 'oz is "reserved for the frame" gains a comparative basis: the formula's usual "voice of 'oz" is avoided. The psalm's lack of any human king despite לדוד also looks deliberate when set beside Hannah's "gives 'oz to his king."

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** Ps 68:36 is noted in the dossier, but not Ps 68:34's qol 'oz, and 1 Sam 2:10's thunder-plus-'oz-to-king is not connected to v. 11 at all.

## OBSERVATION 11

**Type:** PATTERN

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 5, 11

**Claim:** The psalm marks a definite direct object with אֵת only twice: וַיְשַׁבֵּר יְהֹוָה אֶת־אַרְזֵי הַלְּבָנוֹן (v. 5) and יְבָרֵךְ אֶת־עַמּוֹ (v. 11). Both are Piel, both have YHWH as subject, and the two verbs share the consonantal sequence ב-ר (שבר / ברך). What YHWH shatters and what YHWH blesses are set in matching syntactic frames, and this lends grammatical support to Rashi's reading of the cedars as the kings of the nations, set against "his people."

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the grammatical facts are exact; the consonantal play as intended is conjecture.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Micro note v. 5: "direct-object marker 'et' (v. 5; also v. 11 et-ammo) is rare in archaic verse."
- Phonetic: way-shab-BĒR / yə-vā-RĒKH.
- Rashi v. 5: שובר ארזים = the nations' kings. Meiri, Radak: cedars = kings.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The two את-objects, which the dossier treats only as a dating problem, turn out to be the poles of the psalm's arc from shattering to blessing.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The את-marker is discussed only as evidence against antiquity, never as a structural pairing.

## OBSERVATION 12

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 5–7, 9c–10

**Claim:** The storm's verbs and objects belong to the vocabulary of temple-building. Cedars of Lebanon are the timber of Solomon's house (1 Kgs 5:20). Of the exact form חצב, the other four occurrences in the Tanakh all denote construction hewing: three are Solomon's quarrymen, חֹצֵב בָּהָר (1 Kgs 5:29; 2 Chr 2:1, 17), and one is the vat in Isaiah's vineyard (Isa 5:2). Ezra 3:7 joins חֹצְבִים with cedars from Lebanon for the Second Temple. Ps 29 runs through the same sequence as Baal's palace episode: cedars of Lebanon and Sirion (KTU 1.4 VI), then thunder from the finished palace (1.4 VII), then the god seated in his house. The difference is that YHWH *breaks* the cedars and hews *fire*, not stone. Perhaps the point is that this king's hekhal needs no building campaign.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE for the lexical and sequential facts — CONJECTURE for the intended inversion.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Computed rare forms: חצב 5x (1 Kgs 5:29; 2 Chr 2:1; 2 Chr 2:17; Isa 5:2; Ps 29:7).
- Shared-vocabulary parallels: Ezra 3:7 (חצב, לבנון, ארז).
- Concordance: 1 Kgs 5:20 cedars from Lebanon.
- Deep research / macro: KTU 1.4 VI 18–21 (Lebanon//Sirion timber for the palace); KTU 1.4 VII 25–39 (palace window, qlh qdš, Baal seated in his house).

**Payoff (what clicks):** It explains why hotsev, a quarryman's verb, was chosen for lightning. It also gives order and logic to the sequence cedars → hekhal → throne, which the dossier lists only as separate parallels.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** "The destroyer of cedars is enthroned in a cedar house" appears in the dossier, but the construction-only distribution of חצב, and the alignment of the order with KTU 1.4 VI–VII, are not made.

## OBSERVATION 13

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 5, 8–9

**Claim:** Jer 22:23 fuses the image complex of Ps 29:5–9 into one line: you who dwell in Lebanon, nested in cedars, will writhe "like a woman in labour" (חִיל כַּיֹּלֵדָה). Jer 22:20 adds Lebanon, "give your voice," and נִשְׁבְּרוּ. This supports keeping MT's labouring hinds in v. 9 against Lowth's "oaks." Birth-pangs are native to the Lebanon-cedar cluster and do not intrude on it, and the parallelism-driven emendation loses its main argument.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the Jeremiah texts are contiguous; this is one supporting datum, not proof.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Shared-vocabulary parallels: Jer 22:23 יֹשַׁבְתְּ בַּלְּבָנוֹן מְקֻנַּנְתְּ בָּאֲרָזִים… חִיל כַּיֹּלֵדָה. Jer 22:20 עֲלִי הַלְּבָנוֹן… תְּנִי קוֹלֵךְ… נִשְׁבְּרוּ.
- Deep research: Lowth's "oaks" emendation, adopted by RSV/NIV/NET.
- Job 39:1 חֹלֵל אַיָּלוֹת.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The text-critical debate gains an inner-biblical datum the dossier never uses. Lebanon's cedars and labour pains travel together, so the line from v. 5 to v. 9 coheres as MT has it.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The debate over "hinds vs. oaks" usually rests on Job 39:1 and on parallelism. Jer 22 appears in the computed parallels but no one draws the conclusion from it.

## OBSERVATION 14

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 2, 5–6, 8–9 (with Isa 35)

**Claim:** Isa 35 shares five distinctive words with Ps 29 (כבוד, הדר, לבנון, מדבר, איל) and runs every one of them backward. In Ps 29 the voice breaks Lebanon's cedars, convulses the wilderness, and sends hinds into labour. In Isa 35 the wilderness *receives* "the glory of Lebanon… the majesty (הדר) of Carmel," sees "the glory of YHWH," the lame "leap like a hart," and water breaks out in the wilderness. Perhaps Isa 35 reads as a redemptive inversion of the storm theophany.

**Confidence:** CONJECTURE — the vocabulary overlap is computed; dependence is unproven.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Shared-vocabulary parallels: Isa 35 (הדר, לבנון, איל, כבוד, מדבר). Isa 35:2 כְּבוֹד הַלְּבָנוֹן נִתַּן־לָהּ הֲדַר הַכַּרְמֶל. Isa 35:6 יְדַלֵּג כָּאַיָּל… נִבְקְעוּ בַמִּדְבָּר מַיִם.
- Figurative curator: the Isa 35:6 hind is contrasted with Ps 29.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The individual contrasts the figurative curator notes (the hind) belong to a whole-chapter reversal. This is real texture, though its weight depends on the conjecture.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The dossier contrasts only the hind image, not the full five-word overlap or its inversion.

## OBSERVATION 15

**Type:** PATTERN

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 7–8

**Claim:** The isolated monocolon v. 7 ends in אֵשׁ ('ĒSH), and v. 8 ends in קָדֵשׁ (qā-DHĒSH). The end-rhyme ties the lone lightning-line to the following bicolon. This fits any analysis that reads v. 7 as the first colon of a tricolon with v. 8 (qol / qol / YHWH, the same shape as v. 3), and it weakens the hypothesis of a lost line.

**Confidence:** CONJECTURE — the rhyme is certain; that it is structural is a suggestion. Minor texture.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Phonetic transcriptions: v. 7 'ĒSH; v. 8 qā-DHĒSH.
- Macro and deep research: v. 7 monocolon; possible lacuna; Pardee's five-tricola parsing of vv. 3–9.

**Payoff (what clicks):** Sound gives evidence on a structural crux the dossier argues only by colon count.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The monocolon is debated on metrical grounds without reference to its end-sound.

## OBSERVATION 16

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** vv. 3–9, 9c, 11

**Claim:** In the psalm, the divine voice never *says* anything. Its predicates are effects: thundering, breaking, hewing, convulsing, birthing, stripping. The poem's only verb of speech (אֹמֵר) and its only quoted word (כָּבוֹד) belong to the choir in v. 9c. The Sinai midrash can be read as filling exactly this gap. Rashi on v. 7 has the Decalogue issue in flames and engrave itself on the tablets. Rashi and Exod. Rab. on v. 4 have the voice measured to each listener's strength or carried in Moses' voice. Zevachim 116a makes v. 11's 'oz the Torah. Deuteronomy's "you heard a voice of *words*" (קוֹל דְּבָרִים) is the connector: the midrash gives the psalm's wordless qol the words of Sinai.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the grammatical fact is exact; the midrash-as-supplement reading is interpretive.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Psalm text: אמר appears only in v. 9c.
- Rashi on vv. 4 and 7 (commentaries); Zevachim 116a:25 (reception); Hirshman / Exod. Rab. on בכח (deep research).
- General scholarship: Deut 4:12 קוֹל דְּבָרִים אַתֶּם שֹׁמְעִים.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The Sinai reading stops looking imposed. It answers the psalm's most conspicuous silence, a voice with no content, and it explains why the 'oz of v. 11 had to become a text (Torah).

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The Sinai midrashim are catalogued verse by verse, never as a response to the psalm's wordless voice.

## OBSERVATION 17

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** v. 9c (Torah-return rite)

**Claim:** The standard reason for singing Ps 29 as the Torah returns is that 'oz means Torah (Zevachim 116a). The rubrics supply a second, independent support that the dossier does not connect. The Ark is called the הֵיכָל ("when the Torah scroll is returned *להיכל*"; Edot HaMizrach: "when the scroll is brought *להיכל*"). So the procession reaches its goal as the congregation sings וּבְהֵיכָלוֹ כֻּלּוֹ אֹמֵר כָּבוֹד. The custom is overdetermined: v. 9c gives the destination, v. 11 gives what is deposited.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — both rubrics are quoted in the dossier; it is not documented that the hekhal link was the motive.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Liturgical section: Ashkenaz rubric "כשמחזירין הספר תורה להיכל אומרים"; Edot HaMizrach "כשמכניסים את ספר התורה להיכל."
- Zevachim 116a (reception).
- Micro v. 9: היכלו is unique in this form.

**Payoff (what clicks):** It explains why the psalm fits the *moment of entry* in particular, and not Torah-honouring in general. The congregation becomes the כֻּלּוֹ of the hekhal.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The liturgical note gives only the 'oz=Torah explanation. The naming of the Ark goes unremarked.

## OBSERVATION 18

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** v. 10

**Claim:** The exact consonantal form למבול occurs elsewhere only in Gen 9:15, the covenant oath: וְלֹא־יִהְיֶה עוֹד הַמַּיִם לְמַבּוּל. BDB notes that mabbul lacks the article *only* in Gen 9:11, 15, the two oath verses. The vocalization (לַמַּבּוּל with the article vs. לְמַבּוּל) and the syntax (the ל of becoming) differ. Even so, the consonantal identity anchors in the text the reading that Zevachim 116a builds into its Balaam story ("He already swore never to bring a flood"), and it supports reading v. 11's shalom as covenant peace after the Flood.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — form counts are exact; the allusion is plausible, not proven.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Computed rare forms: למבול 2x (Gen 9:15; Ps 29:10).
- BDB מבול: "always c. art. except Gen 9:11, 15."
- Concordance: Gen 9:15.
- Zevachim 116a:23 (reception).
- Analyst's note: shalom "may echo post-Flood covenant."

**Payoff (what clicks):** The ambiguity of the ל-crux is partly resolved by its one consonantal twin, and the midrash's leap from v. 10 to the Noahide oath turns out to be textually triggered.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The dossier notes that mabbul appears only in Gen 6–11 and Ps 29, but not the exact-form match with the oath verse.

## OBSERVATION 19

**Type:** CONNECTION

**Primary verse(s):** whole psalm, with vv. 3, 8, 10 (festival setting)

**Claim:** The autumn-festival setting the dossier argues from the LXX title (ἐξοδίου σκηνῆς) and from form has independent support in the Temple-liturgy traditions. RH 30b names the Rosh Hashanah afternoon song by the incipit קול ה' יחיל מדבר. Sukkah 55a assigns Ps 29 to the first intermediate day of Sukkot. Ps 29 is thus attached at three points across Tishrei: Rosh Hashanah (a day of kingship, cf. מֶלֶךְ, v. 10), Sukkot, and its close. It is the season of the first rains, for a psalm that never names rain, and Pesachim 112a's remedy recites "the seven voices David said *over the water*," lifting v. 3's על המים.

**Confidence:** PROBABLE — the attestations are in the dossier; their bearing on the enthronement hypothesis is interpretive.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Torah Temimah v. 8 (RH 30b): "במנחה מהו אומר קול ה' יחיל מדבר."
- Torah Temimah v. 1 / deep research (Sukkah 55a).
- LXX superscription.
- Pesachim 112a:3 (reception).
- Macro: Mowinckel's autumn enthronement setting.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The reception keeps setting the psalm against water and the rain season, as if to supply the rain the text withholds (Observation 1). The kingship setting also gains a Temple-liturgy datum beyond the LXX.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** RH 30b sits buried in Torah Temimah. The dossier's case for the festival setting cites only the LXX and Mowinckel.

## OBSERVATION 20

**Type:** PATTERN

**Primary verse(s):** v. 11 (reception)

**Claim:** Rabbinic tradition repeatedly uses v. 11 as a *terminal* line, reproducing in larger works its own position as the psalm's last word. It closes the Mishnah (Uktzin 3:12), tractate Berakhot (64a) and with it the incense section, and Grace after Meals. It also serves as the proof that blessings end in peace (y. Ber. 2:4; Megillah 18a). It is not used only at endings (it appears at the opening of the Ark and inside Veyiten Lekha), but endings dominate.

**Confidence:** ESTABLISHED for the placements — the pattern reading is modest; real but minor texture.

**Anchors (every fact this rests on, each with its footing):**
- Reception: Mishnah Oktzin 3:12; y. Ber. 2:4:14.
- Liturgical section: end of Berakhot 64a passage; Birkat HaMazon closing verses; Ark-opening and Veyiten Lekha uses.
- Torah Temimah v. 11: Megillah 18a.

**Payoff (what clicks):** The macro note on end-stress (ba-shalom as the climactic final word) has an afterlife: the tradition treats the verse as a ready-made ending.

**Novelty of the linkage (why a learned reader of Psalms has not already made this connection):** The uses are listed separately in the liturgical catalogue. Their shared terminal function, mirroring the verse's place in the psalm, is not drawn out.
