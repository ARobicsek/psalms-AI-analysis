### Verse 1  
לְדָוִד ׀ יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי מִמִּי אִירָא; יְהוָה מָעוֹז־חַיַּי, מִמִּי אֶפְחָד׃  

The psalm opens like a fanfare. Three bold titles for God; two sharp questions.

- **“YHWH is my light”** – יְהוָה אוֹרִי (*YHWH ’ori*). Calling God *light* (אוֹר, *’or*) is rare in the Bible. Light in the ancient world isn’t just “nice ambiance”; it’s what exposes danger and makes life possible. In Mesopotamia, the sun‑god Shamash was the god of justice because his light “revealed” hidden crimes. By saying “YHWH is *my* light,” the psalmist claims: the one who truly unmasks darkness and guides life is Israel’s God.

- **“and my salvation”** – וְיִשְׁעִי (*veyish‘i*). The noun *yesha‘* (יֶשַׁע) means rescue, deliverance. Putting it with “my” turns theology into biography: God isn’t just *a* savior; He’s *my* savior, with history behind that claim.

- **“YHWH is the stronghold of my life”** – יְהוָה מָעוֹז־חַיַּי (*YHWH ma‘oz ḥayyay*). *Ma‘oz* (מָעוֹז) is a fortress—a place you flee when enemies attack. Think of a hilltop citadel with thick walls. “Of my life” (חַיַּי, *ḥayyay*) makes it comprehensive: every area of existence has this fortress around it.

That sets up the double question:

> “Whom shall I fear? … Whom shall I dread?” – מִמִּי אִירָא… מִמִּי אֶפְחָד

Both verbs—*’ira* (אִירָא, fear) and *’epḥad* (אֶפְחָד, dread)—cover the emotional spectrum from “nervous” to “terrified.” The question isn’t expecting an answer; it’s a rhetorical way of saying: *no one*.

Notice how compact this is. Hebrew poetry loves to pair near‑synonyms:

- “light / salvation / stronghold” pile up images of security.
- “fear / dread” intensify the emotional impact.

The verse also quietly introduces the psalm’s tension: if God really is all this, why *does* the psalmist fear later? The poem will spend the rest of its time working that out.

Traditional Jewish commentators hear different shades here. Rashi (11th‑c. France) hears “light” as rescue from trouble, “salvation” as ultimate deliverance, and “stronghold” as ongoing protection. The Malbim (19th‑c. Eastern Europe) reads the whole verse as a thesis: *God’s protection follows from closeness to Him. If I cleave to Him (“dveikut”), I’m under constant providence—so fear makes no sense.*

The verse sets the key in which the rest of the psalm plays: trust in a God who both **exposes** and **protects**.

### Verse 2  
בִּקְרֹב עָלַי מְרֵעִים לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי; צָרַי וְאֹיְבַי לִי, הֵמָּה כָשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ׃  

Here the danger gets vividly concrete—and disturbingly gory.

- **“When evildoers drew near against me”** – בִּקְרֹב עָלַי מְרֵעִים (*biqrov ‘alay mərē‘im*). *Mərē‘im* (מְרֵעִים) are “evil‑doers,” people actively doing harm. *Biqrov* (בִּקְרֹב) gives you the moment the attack closes in: “as they came near.”

- **“to devour my flesh”** – לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי (*le’ekhol ’et‑bəsari*). This is not polite. Eating someone’s flesh is the imagery of wild animals or cannibalistic siege horror. In prophetic books and war poetry, “devour” often means “destroy violently.” Here, enemies are imagined as predators.

The second half names them more formally:

- **צָרַי וְאֹיְבַי לִי** – “my adversaries and my enemies, to me” (*tzarai ve’oyvai li*). *Tzar* (צַר) is an oppressor or enemy in conflict; *oyev* (אוֹיֵב) is a more general “enemy.” The little **לי (li, “against me/for me”)** stresses that this is personal.

Then comes the twist:

> הֵמָּה כָשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ  
> “They, they stumbled and fell.”

The independent pronoun *hema* (הֵמָּה, “they themselves”) is emphatic: they, not I, lose their footing. The verbs *kashlu* (כָשְׁלוּ, stumbled) and *nafalû* (נָפָלוּ, fell) form a little downward slide—first you trip, then you go down.

Grammatically, these are perfect verbs (past tense): “they stumbled and fell.” But in a psalm of confidence, Hebrew often uses past forms to describe future trust—what scholars sometimes call a “prophetic perfect.” In effect: “Whenever this kind of thing happens, they *end up* stumbling and falling.”

The combination of animal imagery (“devour my flesh”) with legal/military titles (“my adversaries and enemies”) already starts blending the psalm’s worlds: physical danger, social threat, and spiritual meaning overlap.

For a listener, it’s also emotionally smart. The verse lets you picture the worst (“they’re going to eat me alive”)—and then flips it: “Actually, they’re the ones who crash.” That remembered reversal is what fuels the confidence of the next verse.

### Verse 3  
אִם־תַּחֲנֶה עָלַי מַחֲנֶה לֹא־יִירָא לִבִּי; אִם־תָּקוּם עָלַי מִלְחָמָה, בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ׃  

Now the poet moves from a remembered attack (v. 2) to **hypothetical worst‑case scenarios**.

- **“If an army encamps against me”** – אִם־תַּחֲנֶה עָלַי מַחֲנֶה (*’im‑taḥaneh ‘alay maḥaneh*). Nice wordplay: *taḥaneh* (תַּחֲנֶה, “encamps”) and *maḥaneh* (מַחֲנֶה, “camp/army”) echo each other. You can almost hear the camp settling in. In Iron Age warfare, a besieging camp outside your walls meant: you’re in deep trouble.

- **“my heart will not fear”** – לֹא־יִירָא לִבִּי (*lo‑yira libbi*). In Hebrew thought, the *lev* (לֵב, heart) isn’t just emotions; it’s thinking + will. So “my heart will not fear” means “my inner decision‑center won’t panic.”

Then:

- **“If war rises up against me”** – אִם־תָּקוּם עָלַי מִלְחָמָה (*’im‑taqum ‘alay milḥamah*). War is personified as something that “stands up” (from *qum*, קוּם) and comes at you. We’ve moved from siege (*camp*) to full conflict (*war*).

The line ends:

> בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ  
> “In this I am confident.”

The demonstrative *zot* (זֹאת, “this”) is deliciously vague. In what, exactly? Medieval commentators split:

- Rashi: in what I just said, “YHWH is the stronghold of my life” (v. 1).
- Ibn Ezra and others: in what I’m about to say—the “one thing” in v. 4.

Both make sense, and the ambiguity is probably intentional. His confidence is rooted both in:

1. Who God is (light, salvation, fortress), and  
2. What he is aiming his life at (dwelling in God’s house).

Structurally, this verse is the **high point of pure confidence** before the psalm turns to temple longing and, later, to lament. It’s like the crest of a wave before the water starts dropping.

Notice also the rhythm: *im… lo yira libbi; im… b’zot ani voteaḥ.* The repetition of *im* (אִם, “if”) and the balanced cola create a steady, marching feel—the cadence of someone rehearsing courage.

### Verse 4  
אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהוָה אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ; שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהוָה כָּל־יְמֵי חַיַּי; לַחֲזוֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְהוָה, וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ׃  

This is the **pivot** of the whole psalm. After “In this I trust” (v. 3), we finally hear what “this” is.

> אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי… אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ  
> “One thing I have asked from YHWH; that I will seek.”

Two verbs of desire:

- *sha’alti* (שָׁאַלְתִּי, “I have asked”) – a decisive past choice.
- *’avaqesh* (אֲבַקֵּשׁ, “I will seek”) – ongoing pursuit.

He’s saying: I boiled my prayers down to one request, and I’m still chasing it.

The request has three parts:

1. **שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהוָה כָּל־יְמֵי חַיַּי**  
   “to dwell in the house of YHWH all the days of my life.”

   *Shivti* (שִׁבְתִּי) here is an infinitive construct: “my dwelling.” *Beit YHWH* (בֵּית־יְהוָה) is the temple. Obviously, David didn’t move his bed into the sanctuary. This is metaphor plus legal language: he wants **ongoing access**, almost like someone with the right to be there whenever needed—think permanent “temple resident” status.

2. **לַחֲזוֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְהוָה**  
   “to gaze upon the pleasantness/beauty of YHWH.”

   *Laḥazot* (לַחֲזוֹת) is from *ḥazah* (חָזָה), often used for prophetic “seeing” (visions). *No‘am* (נֹעַם) means pleasantness, charm, sweetness. So this is not “tourist photo of the temple”; it’s a deep, ongoing encounter with God’s gracious presence.

3. **וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ**  
   “and to inquire/visit in His temple.”

   The root *b‑q‑r* can go two ways:

   - Rashi: from *boker* (בֹּקֶר, morning) – “visit again and again,” like a daily worship habit.
   - Others: like 2 Kings 16:15, where the altar is “for me to inquire by” – seeking God’s verdict.

   Either way, this is about **ongoing relationship**: bringing questions, receiving guidance, coming back regularly.

What’s striking is the **reframing**: after all the war imagery, the one thing he really wants is not “crush my enemies” but “let me live close to You.” Safety is now defined as *proximity to God*, not the absence of conflict.

Psychologically, this is huge. Many of us treat God as a crisis hotline: call when the army encamps. Here the psalmist inverts that: he wants God Himself as the constant, and crisis‑help as a byproduct.

This verse also anticipates how later Jewish tradition will read the whole psalm during Elul and the High Holidays: as a season of asking, *What is my “one thing”? Where do I actually want my life to dwell?*

### Verse 5  
כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְסֻכּוֹ בְּיוֹם רָעָה; יַסְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵתֶר אָהֳלוֹ; בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי׃  

Verse 5 begins with **כִּי (ki, “for/because”)**: it explains the confidence and the “one thing” request.

Three metaphors of protection:

1. **“He will hide me in His sukkah in the day of trouble”**  
   כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְסֻכּוֹ בְּיוֹם רָעָה (*ki yitzpeneni b’sukko b’yom ra‘ah*).

   - *Yitzpeneni* (יִצְפְּנֵנִי) from *tz‑p‑n* (צ־פ־ן) means “to hide, to treasure.” It’s used for hiding both valuables and people.
   - *Sukkah* (סֻכָּה) here is a booth/pavilion. In other texts it’s the fragile hut for Sukkot, but in war language it can mean a royal or military tent.

   “Day of trouble” (*yom ra‘ah*, יוֹם רָעָה) is a stock phrase for crisis. The picture: when everything goes bad, God tucks me into His war‑tent.

2. **“He will conceal me in the shelter of His tent”**  
   יַסְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵתֶר אָהֳלוֹ (*yastireni b’seter ’ohalo*).

   - *Yastireni* (יַסְתִּרֵנִי) from *s‑t‑r* (ס־ת־ר) also means “to hide, conceal.”
   - *Seter* (סֵתֶר) is a “secret place, hiding place.”
   - *’Ohel* (אֹהֶל) is a tent—evoking the Tabernacle and God’s presence in the wilderness.

   If the sukkah was an outer pavilion, the *seter ohalo* is the inner, most secure spot.

3. **“On a rock He will lift me up”**  
   בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי (*b’tzur yeromemeni*).

   - *Tzur* (צוּר, rock) is both literal cliff and divine title (“The Rock,” Deut 32).
   - *Yeromemeni* (יְרוֹמְמֵנִי) from *r‑w‑m* (ר־ו־ם), “to lift high.”

   Now the imagery goes vertical: you’re placed on high ground, out of reach.

There’s a nice progression: **tent → tent’s inner hiding place → rock**. From cloth (temporary) to stone (permanent); from horizontal shelter to vertical elevation.

Theologically, this locks in what v. 4 implied: the *temple space*—God’s house, tent, rock—is where true security happens. Notice also how the “day of trouble” (*yom ra‘ah*) answers the “day of battle” in v. 3. Whether the trouble is military, legal, or personal, the answer is the same: be hidden *with* God.

Later, Jewish liturgy will hear Sukkot in this verse: “He will hide me in His sukkah.” That’s why Psalm 27 is recited through Sukkot as well—linking the physical booths we build to the divine pavilion this verse imagines.

### Verse 6  
וְעַתָּה יָרוּם רֹאשִׁי עַל־אֹיְבַי סְבִיבוֹתַי; וְאֶזְבְּחָה בְּאָהֳלוֹ זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה; אָשִׁירָה וַאֲזַמְּרָה לַיהוָה׃  

A new time word shows up: **וְעַתָּה (ve‘attah, “and now”)**. We’ve moved from “day of trouble” to the celebration that follows.

> יָרוּם רֹאשִׁי עַל־אֹיְבַי סְבִיבוֹתַי  
> “My head will be lifted up above my enemies around me.”

“Head lifted” (*yarum roshi*, יָרוּם רֹאשִׁי) is restoration and honor. In ancient courts, to “lift someone’s head” was to restore them; to bow the head was shame. The enemies are still “around” (*sevivotai*, סְבִיבוֹתַי), but he’s now above them in dignity and safety.

The response is liturgical:

- **“I will sacrifice in His tent sacrifices of teru‘ah”** – וְאֶזְבְּחָה בְּאָהֳלוֹ זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה (*ve’ezbeḥah b’ohalo zivḥei teru‘ah*).

   - *’Ezbeḥah* (אֶזְבְּחָה) is a cohortative: “let me / I will offer sacrifices.”
   - *Teru‘ah* (תְּרוּעָה) is a shout or trumpet‑blast—used both for battle cries and joyful worship.

   So “sacrifices of teru‘ah” are offerings accompanied by loud praise: songs, shouts, perhaps trumpets.

- **“I will sing and make music to YHWH”** – אָשִׁירָה וַאֲזַמְּרָה לַיהוָה (*’ashirah va’azamrah laYHWH*).

   *Shir* (שׁיר) is to sing; *zamar* (זמר) often implies music with instruments. He imagined himself in the very tent he begged for access to, now performing grateful worship.

Musically, you can almost hear the psalm modulate: from minor key danger to major key praise.

One subtle point: in vv. 4–5 he wanted to “gaze” and “inquire.” Here, having been protected, he doesn’t just watch. He **acts**—sacrifices, sings, makes music. Presence leads to participation.

Some Jewish traditions associate “sacrifices of teru‘ah” with the shofar blasts of Rosh Hashanah (one of the shofar patterns is called *teru‘ah*). That fits the double edge of the word: the same sound that once framed battle now celebrates victory and repentance.

### Verse 7  
שְׁמַע יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא; וְחׇנֵּנִי וַעֲנֵנִי׃  

Here’s the famous **mood shift**. The grammar changes (imperatives), the direction changes (talking *to* God instead of about Him), and the tone becomes much more vulnerable.

> שְׁמַע יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא  
> “Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call.”

*Shema‘* (שְׁמַע, “hear!”) is an imperative. It’s the same verb in the *Shema* prayer (“Hear, O Israel”). *Qoli* (קוֹלִי, my voice) emphasizes not just the content of the prayer but the raw cry itself.

Then two more requests:

- **וְחׇנֵּנִי** – “be gracious to me / show me favor.”
- **וַעֲנֵנִי** – “and answer me.”

*Chanan* (חנן) is “to show grace/favor”; *‘anah* (ענה) is “to respond, answer.” Together they ask both for merciful attitude and concrete response.

Students often ask: is this a second, different psalm glued on? Many scholars used to think so. But notice:

- The one praying still assumes God hears.
- The verbs “hear” and “answer” (שְׁמַע / עֲנֵנִי) pick up the earlier trust. The psalm is deliberately moving from **creed** (“YHWH is my light”) to **prayer** (“So, please hear me”).

What’s changed is not the theology; it’s the honesty. After declaring that enemies stumble and God protects, the psalmist lets you into the ongoing struggle: he still has to pray, and he still fears being ignored.

In personal terms, this is where many of us actually live: between doctrinal confidence and emotional pleading.

### Verse 8  
לְךָ אָמַר לִבִּי, בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי; אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ יְהוָה אֲבַקֵּשׁ׃  

This verse is syntactically tricky—and theologically central.

Literally: **“To You said my heart, ‘Seek my face’; Your face, YHWH, I will seek.”**

How to untangle that?

- **לְךָ אָמַר לִבִּי** – “To You my heart has said…” or “On Your behalf my heart said…”

   The preposition *lekha* (לְךָ) can mean “to you” or “for you / on your behalf.” Medieval commentators use this ambiguity.

- **בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי** – “Seek My face.”

   This is a plural imperative (“you [all] seek”), and *panai* (פָנַי) is “my face.” That sounds like **God** speaking: “All of you, seek My face.” Rashi reads it like this and glosses: “On Your behalf, as Your agent, my heart says to me, ‘All of you, Israel, seek My face.’”

So the inner dialogue goes like this:

1. God’s command, heard inwardly: **“Seek My face.”**
2. The heart acts as a go‑between, relaying that divine call.
3. The psalmist responds:  

   > אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ יְהוָה אֲבַקֵּשׁ  
   > “Your face, YHWH, I will seek.”

The idiom “seek (God’s) face” (*baqesh panim*, בִּקֵּשׁ פָּנִים) is technical. In royal and temple contexts, to “see the face” of a king or god is to be admitted into the throne room. In an idol‑using culture, that meant literally approaching the statue. In Israel’s aniconic practice (no image), it came to mean **standing in the place where God’s presence is manifested**—the temple, or later, the place of prayer—and asking for favor.

So this verse dramatizes the **call‑and‑response of prayer**:

- God: “Seek My face.”
- Human: “Your face, YHWH, I *will* seek.”

It also shows how the psalm understands faith: not just abstract believing, but *obeying an inner summons* to turn toward God, again and again. The following verse (v. 9) will show why that’s hard.

### Verse 9  
אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי; אַל־תַּט בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ; עֶזְרָתִי הָיִיתָ; אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי וְאַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי, אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי׃  

This is the emotional low point. Count the “do not”s:

1. **אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי** – “Do not hide Your face from me.”
2. **אַל־תַּט בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ** – “Do not turn Your servant away in anger.”
3. **אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי** – “Do not forsake me.”
4. **וְאַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי** – “and do not abandon me.”

The fear is not “let my enemies win,” but **“let God not leave.”**

- *Hide Your face* – *taster panekha* (תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ). As we saw, “face” means relational presence. Hiding it = withdrawing favor.
- *Turn away… in anger* – *al‑tat b’af ‘avdekha* (אַל־תַּט בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ). The verb *natah* (נָטָה) here is “thrust aside.” *Af* (אַף, literally “nose”) is a standard idiom for anger—think flared nostrils.
- *Forsake / abandon* – *titsheni* (תִּטְּשֵׁנִי) and *ta‘azveni* (תַּעַזְבֵנִי). Two near‑synonyms, one after the other: you can feel the panic stacking.

In the middle we get a reminder:

> עֶזְרָתִי הָיִיתָ  
> “You have been my help.”

Perfect tense: in the past, You *have* helped. That memory is the only reason asking makes any sense.

The ending title is key:

> אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי  
> “God of my salvation.”

That echoes v. 1 (“my salvation,” *yish‘i*), creating a little **inclusio**: the God confidently praised at the start is the same one now begged not to leave. The psalm isn’t abandoning its theology; it’s pressing that theology into a crisis.

For students, this verse is a crucial honesty checkpoint. A life of faith can include, and even expect, seasons when your main prayer is simply “Don’t go.” Psalm 27 legitimizes that prayer as part of the journey between trust and waiting.

### Verse 10  
כִּי־אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי; וַיהוָה יַאַסְפֵנִי׃  

Short, sharp, and emotionally intense.

> כִּי־אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי  
> “For my father and my mother have forsaken me.”

The pairing “father / mother” (אָבִי / אִמִּי) is a **merism**: naming the two most fundamental human caregivers to express “even if *the closest possible humans* walk away.” Whether this literally happened to the psalmist or is poetic hyperbole, the point is: imagine the deepest abandonment you can think of.

> וַיהוָה יַאַסְפֵנִי  
> “But YHWH will gather me in.”

*Ya’asfeni* (יַאַסְפֵנִי) from *’‑s‑f* (אָסַף) means “collect, gather, take in.” Elsewhere it’s used for gathering exiles, or even “being gathered to one’s people” at death. Here it’s like God becomes the foster parent or adoptive family: taking in the one left outside.

Some traditional readings:

- Rashi says: human parents are only involved briefly at conception; God is the One who really “guards the drop and forms the fetus.” So in a sense, parents “leave” you early; God stays the whole time.
- Ibn Ezra takes it as “when my parents died, You sustained me.”

Either way, this verse answers v. 9’s fear of abandonment with a defiant counter‑memory: **even if every human bond fails, God’s bond holds.**

Placed here, between desperate petitions (vv. 9, 11–12), v. 10 is like a breath: a flash of core identity—“I am the one God took in”—that gives the courage to keep praying and to ask for guidance.

For readers, it also widens the psalm’s scope. The poem isn’t only for battlefield kings or temple priests. It’s for anyone who’s felt relationally unmoored—family, community, mentors—and needs to hear that the story doesn’t end there.

### Verse 11  
הוֹרֵנִי יְהוָה דַּרְכֶּךָ; וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, לְמַעַן שֹׁרְרָי׃  

We move from “don’t leave me” to “lead me.”

> הוֹרֵנִי יְהוָה דַּרְכֶּךָ  
> “Teach me, YHWH, Your way.”

*Horeni* (הוֹרֵנִי) from *y‑r‑h* (י־ר־ה) is the verb behind *Torah* (תּוֹרָה, instruction). It literally means “point out, instruct.” *Derkekha* (דַּרְכֶּךָ) is “Your way” – a metaphor for God’s patterns, values, and will.

> וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר  
> “and lead me on a level path.”

*Neḥeni* (נְחֵנִי) from *n‑ḥ‑h* (נחה) is used for God leading His people (cf. Psalm 23). *’Oraḥ* (אֹרַח) is another word for “path,” and *mîšôr* (מִישׁוֹר) means “level, even, upright.” So he’s asking not just “tell me the rules” but “guide my actual steps in a way that’s straight and safe.”

Why?

> לְמַעַן שֹׁרְרָי  
> “because of my shorerai.”

*Shorerai* (שֹׁרְרָי) is rare. It probably means “those watching me with hostile intent”—spies, critics, enemies who are “waiting to pounce” on any misstep. Radak glosses: “those who watch me to accuse me if I stumble.”

So the request is: *Make my life‑path so straight that my enemies have nothing real to use against me.*

This pulls temple‑trust and legal conflict together. The psalmist isn’t asking God to magically erase the danger. He’s asking for **ethical clarity and steadiness** so that, under scrutiny, he’ll stand.

For students, this verse offers a mature twist on “God, help”: not “remove the hard people,” but “shape me so their malice can’t stick.” It connects worship (“Your way”) with character (“level path”) with survival (“because of my watchers”).

### Verse 12  
אַל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי; כִּי קָמוּ בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, וִיפֵחַ חָמָס׃  

Back to explicit petition, now with courtroom flavor.

> אַל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי  
> “Do not give me over to the nefesh of my adversaries.”

*Nefesh* (נֶפֶשׁ) is a famously multivalent word: life, throat, appetite, desire. “To give someone into the nefesh of…” can mean “into the desire of” or “into the gaping throat of” one’s enemies. Either way, it’s “Don’t hand me over to what they want to do to me.”

> כִּי קָמוּ בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר  
> “For false witnesses have risen against me.”

*‘Edei sheqer* (עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר) are lying witnesses—technical legal language from the commandment “You shall not bear false witness” (Exod 20:16). *Qamu bi* (קָמוּ בִי, “have risen against me”) is how you’d describe bringing a case in court.

> וִיפֵחַ חָמָס  
> “and a yifeaḥ of violence.”

The phrase *yifeaḥ ḥamas* (יִפֵּחַ חָמָס) is tricky. The verb *p‑w‑aḥ / y‑p‑aḥ* can mean “breathe out, puff, blow,” or—by related Semitic evidence—“testify, speak.” So:

- Masoretic reading: “one who breathes out violence” – someone spewing aggressive lies.
- Alternative: “a witness of violence.”

Either way, the picture is of **verbal aggression**: people weaponizing words—“testimony”—to destroy him. That fits the asylum idea: he’s in the sanctuary partly because outside, the courts are rigged.

This verse brings together images from the whole psalm:

- Enemies as devourers (v. 2) → “nefesh/throat” here.
- Enemies rising up (v. 3 war) → witnesses rising up in court.
- God’s house as refuge (vv. 4–5) → now also legal shelter.

For modern readers, this can easily map onto reputational attacks, slander, or social media pile‑ons: situations where the main danger is **what people say about you**. The psalm gives voice to the fear of having your story told falsely—and hands that fear to God as Judge.

### Verse 13  
לוּלֵא הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת בְּטוּב־יְהוָה בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים׃  

This verse is famously *unfinished*.

> לוּלֵא הֶאֱמַנְתִּי…  
> “If I had not believed…”

In normal Hebrew, *lulei* (לוּלֵא) introduces a condition: “If not X, then Y.” Here we get the “if” clause:

- **הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת בְּטוּב־יְהוָה** – “I had believed to see the goodness of YHWH…”
- **בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים** – “in the land of the living.”

But the “then…” never comes. Ancient scribes even put tiny dots over each letter of לוּלֵא, a scribal way of flagging something odd.

How do we read it?

Most Jewish tradition hears it as **aposiopesis**—a deliberate breaking off. “If I hadn’t trusted that I would one day see God’s goodness here in the land of the living… [I don’t even want to say what I would have become].” The unsaid consequence could be despair, cynicism, loss of faith, or even suicide. The horror is left unspoken.

“The land of the living” (אֶרֶץ חַיִּים) elsewhere contrasts with Sheol, the realm of the dead (e.g., Isaiah 38:11). It’s this life, this history. So his faith is about **this‑worldly vindication**: “I believe I will yet see some tangible good from God before I die”—not just “go to heaven when it’s all over.”

Grammatically, *he’emanti* (הֶאֱמַנְתִּי, “I believed”) is past tense. He’s looking back on a choice he made: at some point, he chose trust over collapse.

For a modern reader, this verse is both raw and comforting. It admits that without some stubborn, almost irrational hope in God’s goodness, a person can go under. The psalmist doesn’t romanticize that struggle; he just refuses to describe the alternative.

### Verse 14  
קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה; חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ; וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה׃  

The psalm closes by **turning outward**—or perhaps by the psalmist talking to his own soul.

> קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה… וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה  
> “Wait for YHWH… and wait for YHWH.”

The verb *qaveh* (קַוֵּה) means “wait / hope,” but with a sense of **tension** (its root is used for twisting cords). It appears twice, bracketing the line.

In the middle:

> חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ  
> “Be strong, and let your heart take courage.”

- *Ḥazaq* (חֲזַק) – “be strong.” Command.
- *Ya’ametz libbekha* (יַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ) – “let your heart be brave/firm.” The verb *’‑m‑tz* (אַמֵּץ) is used in God’s encouragement to Joshua: “Be strong and courageous” (חֲזַק וֶאֱמָץ).

Who is being addressed? Several options:

- The psalmist talking to himself (“Come on, heart—stay with it”).
- A priest or teacher talking to the worshipper.
- The psalmist talking to the community, turning his private experience into shared instruction.

Any way you read it, the point is: the right response to the mix of trust (vv. 1–3), longing (v. 4–6), fear (vv. 7–12), and fragile hope (v. 13) is **not giving up**, but **active waiting**.

This is not denial. The psalm never pretends the enemies or the false witnesses have vanished. It simply insists that the decisive relationship in the story is between the worshipper and YHWH—and that relationship is sustained by “seeking the face” and “waiting for YHWH.”

So the psalm ends where it began, with **YHWH** as the focus:

- Opening: “YHWH is my light and my salvation.”
- Closing: “Wait for YHWH… wait for YHWH.”

Between those two lines lies the whole drama of a human heart learning to live at the threshold of God’s presence: sometimes confident, sometimes terrified, always called to turn its face toward the Face, and to wait.
