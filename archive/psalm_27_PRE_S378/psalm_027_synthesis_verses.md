# Verse-by-Verse Commentary on Psalm 27

**Verse 1**

לְדָוִ֨ד ׀ יְהֹוָ֤ה ׀ אוֹרִ֣י וְ֭יִשְׁעִי, מִמִּ֣י אִירָ֑א; יְהֹוָ֥ה מָעוֹז־חַ֝יַּ֗י, מִמִּ֥י אֶפְחָֽד׃

The opening verse establishes the psalm's theological architecture through a double metaphor bracketed by double rhetorical questions. The divine epithets *ori* (אוֹרִי, "my light") and *yish'i* (יִשְׁעִי, "my salvation") are not merely poetic abstractions but deliberate appropriations from the ancient Near Eastern religious imagination. "Light" (*or*) as a divine title is exceptionally rare in the Hebrew Bible—found elsewhere only in Micah 7:8 and Isaiah 10:17—and functions as a polemical claim against solar deities such as Mesopotamian Shamash, the god of justice whose rays were believed to expose hidden evil. By declaring YHWH as *ori*, the psalmist collapses the functions of multiple deities into Israel's God, who is simultaneously the illuminator of darkness and the warrior (*ma'oz*, מָעוֹז, "stronghold/fortress") who provides physical security.

The term *ma'oz* is not abstract; it references the limestone fortresses of Judean geography—sites like En-Gedi and the strongholds of David's fugitive years (1 Sam 22:4-5: וַיַּנְחֵם֙ אֶת־מְצוּדַ֣ת דָּוִ֔ד, "He brought them to the stronghold of David"). The BDB lexicon confirms *ma'oz* as a "place or means of safety," derived from the root *ʿ-z-z* (to be strong), frequently used for fortified cities (Isa 17:9; Ezek 24:25). This dual imagery—light and stronghold—creates a merism encompassing both spiritual illumination and military protection, the cosmic and the historical.

The double rhetorical questions—*mimmi ira'* (מִמִּ֣י אִירָ֑א, "whom shall I fear?") and *mimmi efchad* (מִמִּ֥י אֶפְחָֽד, "whom shall I dread?")—employ the interrogative *mi* (מִי, "who?") to assert the impossibility of fear. The Septuagint's rendering underscores this: *tina fobēthēsomai* ("whom shall I be made to fear?"), with the passive form suggesting that fear itself cannot be imposed upon one who possesses divine light. The phonetic structure reinforces this confidence: the opening syllables `lə-dhā-**WIDH** yə-hō-**WĀH**` place stress on the proper name, making YHWH himself the sonic anchor of the entire psalm. The repetition of *mimmi* creates anaphoric emphasis, while the shift from *ira'* (fear, general dread) to *efchad* (dread, more intense terror) employs synonymous parallelism that intensifies rather than repeats.

The verse's liturgical afterlife reveals its enduring power: it opens the month-long Elul recitation in Jewish tradition (Rosh Chodesh Elul through Hoshana Rabbah), where the midrashic reading maps *ori* onto Rosh Hashanah (the day of judgment's illumination) and *yish'i* onto Yom Kippur (salvation through atonement). This interpretive move transforms the verse from military confidence into penitential theology—the "enemies" become not human adversaries but the inner accusers of conscience, and the "light" becomes the divine scrutiny that paradoxically saves.

**Verse 2**

בִּקְרֹ֤ב עָלַ֨י ׀ מְרֵעִים֮, לֶאֱכֹ֢ל אֶת־בְּשָׂ֫רִ֥י, צָרַ֣י וְאֹיְבַ֣י לִ֑י הֵ֖מָּה כָשְׁל֣וּ וְנָפָֽלוּ׃

This verse pivots from abstract theology to visceral threat through the shocking image of cannibalistic consumption. The phrase *le'ekhol et-besari* (לֶאֱכֹ֢ל אֶת־בְּשָׂ֫רִ֥י, "to devour my flesh") is more than hyperbole; it zoomorphizes the enemies, transforming them from human adversaries into predatory beasts. The figurative language database reveals that this "devouring" imagery appears across Scripture with militaristic overtones. In Jeremiah 12:9, the prophet speaks of beasts brought *le'oklah* (לְאׇכְלָֽה, "to devour"): הַעַ֨יִט צָב֤וּעַ נַֽחֲלָתִי֙ לִ֔י הַעַ֖יִט סָבִ֣יב עָלֶ֑יהָ לְכ֗וּ אִסְפ֛וּ כׇּל־חַיַּ֥ת הַשָּׂדֶ֖ה הֵתָ֥יוּ לְאׇכְלָֽה ("Is my heritage a speckled bird of prey? Are birds of prey all around her? Go, assemble all the beasts of the field, bring them to devour!"). The parallel confirms that "eating" functions as a metaphor for military destruction—armies that consume cities like predators devour prey.

The temporal construction *biqrov* (בִּקְרֹ֤ב, "at the approaching/drawing near") uses the Qal infinitive construct of *q-r-b* (to approach), creating narrative immediacy: the threat is not past or future but *approaching* in the present moment. The triple designation of enemies—*mereʿim* (מְרֵעִים, "evil ones/doers of evil"), *tzarai* (צָרַי, "my adversaries"), and *oyvai* (אֹיְבַי, "my enemies")—builds through ascending intensity. The first term, from the root *r-ʿ-ʿ* (to be evil), describes moral character; the second, from *tz-r-r* (to bind/press/oppress), emphasizes hostile action; the third, from *'-y-b* (to be hostile/hate), denotes a permanent state of enmity.

The verse's reversal is emphatic: *hemma kashl-u ve-nafalu* (הֵ֖מָּה כָשְׁל֣וּ וְנָפָֽלוּ, "they—they stumbled and fell"). The independent personal pronoun *hemma* (הֵ֖מָּה, "they themselves") provides contrastive focus: not *I* but *they* are the ones who fall. The verbs *kashal* (כָשַׁל, "to stumble") and *nafal* (נָפַל, "to fall") form a hendiadys—a paired expression for a single concept—but with progression: stumbling leads to falling, the loss of footing results in total collapse. The perfect tense indicates completed action: the anticipated attack has already failed. This prophetic perfect is a feature of psalmic confidence poetry, where faith projects future deliverance as past accomplishment.

The Septuagint's rendering *prosengysan* ("they drew near") for *biqrov* and *prosptaiousin* ("they stumble") for *kashl-u* preserves the military imagery but shifts the tense to aorist, converting the Hebrew perfect into a simple past, thus framing the event as historical witness rather than confident projection. Rashi, in his medieval commentary, reads this verse as autobiographical: "This refers to the time when the enemy approached David to devour him, but they stumbled" (בִּקְרֹב עָלַי מְרֵעִים לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי—זֶה בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁהִקְרִיבוּ הָאוֹיְבִים לְדָוִד לְאָכְלוֹ וְהֵם כָּשְׁלוּ), linking it to Saul's pursuit. Ibn Ezra, however, suggests the verse is hypothetical: "Even if they came to devour me, they *would* stumble"—reading the perfect as modal, a grammatical possibility in Hebrew.

**Verse 3**

אִם־תַּחֲנֶ֬ה עָלַ֨י ׀ מַחֲנֶה֮, לֹא־יִירָ֢א לִ֫בִּ֥י; אִם־תָּק֣וּם עָ֭לַי מִלְחָמָ֑ה, בְּ֝זֹ֗את אֲנִ֣י בוֹטֵֽחַ׃

The escalation continues through two conditional clauses (*im... im*, אִם־... אִם־) that move from siege to open warfare. The first scenario—*tachaneh ʿalai machaneh* (תַּחֲנֶ֬ה עָלַ֨י ׀ מַחֲנֶה֮, "should an army encamp against me")—uses military vocabulary rooted in the verb *ch-n-h* (to encamp), which BDB defines as "to decline, bend down, encamp." The noun *machaneh* (מַחֲנֶה) refers to a fortified military camp, as in the narratives of Israel's wilderness encampment (Exod 14:2; Num 1:52). The paronomasia—*tachaneh... machaneh*—creates a phonetic echo that binds the threat through sound: the verb "to encamp" plays on the noun "encampment," making the threat linguistically imminent even as it remains hypothetical.

The second scenario escalates: *im-taqum ʿalai milchamah* (אִם־תָּק֣וּם עָ֭לַי מִלְחָמָ֑ה, "should war rise against me"). The verb *qum* (קוּם, "to arise/stand up") is often used for hostile actions taking initiative (cf. Psalm 3:7: קָ֤מוּ עָלַי֙ רִבְּבוֹת֙ עָ֔ם, "Myriads of people have risen against me"). War is personified as an active subject that "stands up" against the speaker, a common biblical idiom that treats conflict as an animate adversary. The progression from "encampment" to "war" mirrors the typical ancient siege narrative: first, the enemy surrounds; then, the assault begins.

The psalmist's response to these threats is *lo-yira libbi* (לֹא־יִירָ֢א לִ֫בִּ֥י, "my heart will not fear"). The verb *yira* (יִירָא) is imperfect, indicating future or habitual action—"my heart *will not* fear" or "my heart *does not* fear." The heart (*lev*, לֵב) in Hebrew thought is the seat not of emotion but of will and cognition; thus, "my heart will not fear" means "I will not falter in resolve." The same verb appeared in verse 1 (*ira*, "shall I fear?"), creating lexical bookending.

The verse culminates in the demonstrative *bezot ani voteach* (בְּ֝זֹ֗את אֲנִ֣י בוֹטֵֽחַ, "in this I am confident"). The pronoun *zot* (זֹאת, "this") is grammatically feminine, pointing backward to something previously stated. But to what? Rashi identifies it as referring to the divine epithets of verse 1: "In *this* that I said, 'The LORD is the stronghold of my life,' in this I trust" (בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ—בְּמַה שֶׁאָמַרְתִּי לְמַעְלָה ה׳ מָעוֹז חַיַּי). However, Ibn Ezra suggests it points *forward* to the "One Thing" of verse 4: "This refers to the request mentioned afterwards" (כִּי בְזֹאת—הִיא הַשְּׁאֵלָה הַנִּזְכֶּרֶת אַחַר כֵּן). The grammatical ambiguity creates interpretive richness: confidence is grounded both in what God *is* (v. 1) and in what the psalmist *seeks* (v. 4).

The Septuagint resolves the ambiguity by translating *bezot* with the simple dative *en toutōi* ("in this"), but adds *egō elpizō* ("I hope"), introducing the lexeme of hope (ἐλπίζω) that the Hebrew *batach* (בָּטַח, "to trust/be confident") does not explicitly contain. This reflects the Greek translator's theological move from confidence (Hebrew *batach*) toward expectation (Greek *elpis*). The verb *batach* appears 120 times in the Hebrew Bible, frequently in Psalms (e.g., Ps 4:6: בִּטְח֣וּ בַֽיהֹוָ֑ה, "Trust in the LORD"; Ps 37:3: בְּטַ֣ח בַּֽיהֹוָ֣ה, "Trust in the LORD"), establishing a semantic field of unshakeable reliance rather than passive waiting.

**Verse 4**

אַחַ֤ת ׀ שָׁאַ֣לְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהֹוָה֮, אוֹתָ֢הּ אֲבַ֫קֵּ֥שׁ: שִׁבְתִּ֣י בְּבֵית־יְ֭הֹוָה כׇּל־יְמֵ֣י חַיַּ֑י, לַחֲז֥וֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְ֝הֹוָ֗ה, וּלְבַקֵּ֥ר בְּהֵֽיכָלֽוֹ׃

This verse is the theological and rhetorical pivot of the entire psalm. The "one thing" (*achat*, אַחַ֤ת) formula creates radical prioritization: amid the multiplicity of threats (vv. 2-3), the psalmist collapses all petitions into a singular focus. The structure is emphatic: *achat | sha'alti me'et YHWH* (אַחַ֤ת ׀ שָׁאַ֣לְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהֹוָה)—"One thing I have asked from the LORD." The verb *sha'al* (שָׁאַל, "to ask/request") is in the perfect tense, indicating a completed, definitive action. This is followed by *otah avaqesh* (אוֹתָ֢הּ אֲבַ֫קֵּ֥שׁ, "that very thing I seek"), where the imperfect *avaqesh* (I seek) indicates ongoing pursuit. The pairing of perfect and imperfect verbs—asked (once) and seeking (continually)—creates temporal depth: the request was made definitively in the past, but the pursuit remains present and active.

The content of the "one thing" is *shivti beveit-YHWH kol yemei chayyai* (שִׁבְתִּ֣י בְּבֵית־יְ֭הֹוָה כׇּל־יְמֵ֣י חַיַּ֑י, "my dwelling in the house of the LORD all the days of my life"). The verb form *shivti* (שִׁבְתִּי) is grammatically ambiguous—it can be parsed as either a perfect (Qal, 1cs: "I have dwelt") or an infinitive construct with pronominal suffix ("my dwelling"). The Masoretic vocalization (*shivti* with shewa under shin) indicates the infinitive construct, which BDB confirms as expressing "permanent residence" rather than "occasional visitation." The Septuagint's *tou katoikein me* ("that I should dwell") interprets it as infinitive, framing it as the *telos* (goal/purpose) of the request. Radak emphasizes this permanence: "The psalm's author requests that he may dwell *perpetually* in the house of the Lord, with leisure to engage with spiritual matters, freed from the demands of warfare and political intrigue" (שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהוָה—שֶׁאֶשֵּׁב בְּבֵית ה׳ תָּמִיד, בְּהַשְׁקֵט מִן הַמִּלְחָמוֹת).

The two infinitival clauses that follow specify what "dwelling" entails. First: *lacḥazot benoam-YHWH* (לַחֲז֥וֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְ֝הֹוָ֗ה, "to gaze upon the beauty/pleasantness of the LORD"). The verb *chazah* (חָזָה) is striking—it typically designates prophetic vision (Isa 1:1: חֲזוֹן יְשַֽׁעְיָ֙הוּ֙, "The vision of Isaiah"; Amos 1:1: דִּבְרֵי עָמוֹס֙... אֲשֶׁר־חָזָ֖ה, "The words of Amos... which he saw"). BDB defines *chazah* as "to see, behold, perceive" with the specialized sense of "see in a vision." The noun *noam* (נֹעַם) appears only eight times in the Hebrew Bible and signifies "pleasantness, delightfulness, beauty." In Proverbs 3:17, wisdom's ways are *darkhei noam* (דַּרְכֵי־נֹעַם, "ways of pleasantness"). Psalm 90:17 petitions for *noam Adonai* (נֹעַם אֲדֹנָי, "the favor/beauty of the Lord") to rest upon the community. The term is thus aesthetic and experiential rather than strictly visual; it denotes the *favor* of YHWH as tangibly encountered in the sanctuary. The phrase *benoam-YHWH* (בְּנֹעַם־יְהֹוָה, "in the beauty/pleasantness of the LORD") collocates vision with presence—to "gaze upon the noam" is to experience God's gracious countenance in the worship space.

The second infinitive, *ulevaqer behekhalo* (וּלְבַקֵּ֥ר בְּהֵֽיכָלֽוֹ, "and to inquire in His temple"), has generated significant lexical debate. The root *b-q-r* is ambiguous. Rashi connects it to *boqer* (בֹּקֶר, "morning"), thus: "to visit [the temple] every morning" (לֵירָאוֹת שָׁם בְּכָל בֹּקֶר וָבֹקֶר). This interpretation emphasizes regular, habitual worship. However, modern scholars, following the usage in 2 Kings 16:15 (הַמִּזְבֵּחַ הַנְּחֹשֶׁת יִֽהְיֶה־לִּי לְבַקֵּר, "the bronze altar shall be for me to inquire by"), argue that *baqar* here means "to seek an oracle" or "to inquire" in a technical, divinatory sense. Ibn Ezra supports this: "And to inquire—like *lo yevaqer bein tov lara* (Lev 27:33, 'he shall not inquire between good and bad')—meaning that the priests who dwell continually in the house will teach him and show him" (וּלְבַקֵּר—כְּמוֹ לֹא יְבַקֵּר בֵּין טוֹב לָרָע וְהַטַּעַם שֶׁהַכֹּהֲנִים הַיּוֹשְׁבִים תָּמִיד בַּבַּיִת יְלַמְּדוּהוּ וְיוֹרוּהוּ). This reading transforms *baqar* from aesthetic contemplation to forensic inquiry—seeking divine guidance or verdict. The temple becomes not just a place of worship but a court of appeal, a legal sanctuary where oracular rulings are obtained.

The figurative language database reveals the "house" imagery across Scripture. The most direct parallel is Psalm 23:6: *veshavti beveit-YHWH le'orekh yamim* (וְשַׁבְתִּ֥י בְּבֵית־יְ֝הֹוָ֗ה לְאֹ֣רֶךְ יָמִֽים, "and I shall dwell in the house of the LORD for length of days"), where the verb *shavti* (I shall return/dwell) echoes Psalm 27:4's *shivti*. Both psalms frame temple access as the ultimate goal, not as a means to an end. This contrasts sharply with the "house of bondage" (*beit avadim*, בֵּ֣֥ית עֲבָדִ֑͏ֽים) of Exodus 20:2, where "house" signifies oppression. Psalm 27 reclaims the "house" as the *locus* of freedom and safety—the anti-Egypt, the place where servitude to YHWH liberates rather than enslaves.

The verse's liturgical reception underscores its centrality. In Edot HaMizrach tradition, the phrase *lacḥazot benoam-YHWH* appears in the *Hashkavah* memorial prayer: יַסְתִּירֵהוּ בְּצֵל כְּנָפָיו וּבְסֵתֶר אָהֳלוֹ לַחֲזוֹת בְּנוֹעַם יְהֹוָה וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ ("May He hide him in the shadow of His wings and in the shelter of His tent, to gaze upon the pleasantness of the LORD and to visit His temple"). The transition from life to death is thus framed as a movement into the ultimate "dwelling in the house of the LORD"—the eschatological fulfillment of the psalmist's petition.

**Verse 5**

כִּ֤י יִצְפְּנֵ֨נִי ׀ בְּסֻכֹּה֮ בְּי֢וֹם רָ֫עָ֥ה; יַ֭סְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵ֣תֶר אׇהֳל֑וֹ; בְּ֝צ֗וּר יְרוֹמְמֵֽנִי׃

The verse opens with the causal conjunction *ki* (כִּ֤י, "for/because"), linking the confidence of verses 1-3 and the "one thing" petition of verse 4 to the specific divine actions now enumerated. The psalmist's assurance rests on God's concrete protective interventions, described through three distinct metaphors of shelter.

The first metaphor—*yitzpeneni besukkoh* (יִצְפְּנֵ֨נִי ׀ בְּסֻכֹּה֮, "He will hide me in His sukkah")—is theologically and linguistically dense. The verb *tzafan* (צָפַן, Hiphil imperfect of *tz-p-n*) means "to hide, treasure, store up." BDB notes its usage for hiding persons (Exod 2:2: וַתִּצְפְּנֵהוּ שְׁלֹשָׁה יְרָחִים, "she hid him three months"—Moses in the basket) and for treasuring valuables (Prov 7:1: צְפֹן אֲמָרָי, "treasure my commandments"). To be "hidden" by God is thus to be both protected from enemies and valued as treasure.

The noun *sukkah* (סֻכָּה) poses a profound interpretive challenge. In Leviticus 23:42-43, *sukkot* are the flimsy harvest booths that Israel inhabits during the Feast of Tabernacles: בַּסֻּכֹּת תֵּשְׁבוּ שִׁבְעַת יָמִים... לְמַעַן יֵדְעוּ דֹרֹתֵיכֶם כִּי בַסֻּכּוֹת הוֹשַׁבְתִּי אֶת־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּהוֹצִיאִי אוֹתָם מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם ("You shall dwell in booths seven days... so that your generations may know that I made the people of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt"). These *sukkot* are deliberately fragile, constructed of branches, symbolizing vulnerability and dependence on divine provision. But in Psalm 27, the *sukkah* is God's own dwelling—*besukkoh* (בְּסֻכֹּה֮, "in His booth")—not a human shelter but the divine war-tent. The Dead Sea Scrolls' orthography (11QPsa) confirms the form with full plene spelling, clarifying the vocalization.

The Ancient Near Eastern parallel is illuminating. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.3-1.4), the god Baal constructs his palace, described with the term *sk* (cognate to Hebrew *sukkah*), a celestial pavilion on Mount Sapan. The *sk* functions as the war-camp of the deity, a place of strategic concealment from which he launches attacks against his enemies (particularly Mot, the god of death). Psalm 27:5 appropriates this imagery: YHWH's *sukkah* is not a flimsy human booth but the cosmic war-tent, the heavenly command center from which he protects his clients. The Jerusalem temple, then, is the earthly *sacramentum* of this divine *sukkah*, the place where YHWH "encamps" (recall *machaneh* from v. 3) among his people.

The temporal phrase *beyom ra'ah* (בְּי֢וֹם רָ֫עָ֥ה, "on the day of trouble/evil") specifies when this hiding occurs. The construct *yom ra'ah* (day of evil) appears frequently in the Psalms (Ps 41:2: בְּי֣וֹם רָעָ֑ה יְמַלְּטֵ֥הוּ יְהוָֽה, "In the day of trouble the LORD delivers him"; Ps 49:6: לָ֤מָּה אִירָ֓א בִּימֵ֥י רָ֑ע, "Why should I fear in days of trouble?"). The "day of evil" is not a specific historical event but a *topos* for crisis—the time when enemies attack, false witnesses testify, and death threatens. It is precisely *on that day* that YHWH intervenes.

The second metaphor shifts from the *sukkah* to the *ohel* (tent): *yastireni beseter ohalo* (יַ֭סְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵ֣תֶר אׇהֳל֑וֹ, "He will conceal me in the secret place of His tent"). The verb *satir* (סָתַר, Hiphil of *s-t-r*) means "to hide, conceal," intensifying the verb *tzafan* from the previous clause. The phrase *seter ohalo* (סֵ֣תֶר אׇהֳל֑וֹ, "the secret/shelter of His tent") employs the noun *seter* (סֵתֶר, "covering, shelter, hiding place"), often used for God's protective concealment (Ps 31:21: תַּסְתִּירֵם בְּסֵתֶר פָּנֶיךָ, "You hide them in the shelter of Your presence"; Ps 91:1: יֹ֭שֵׁב בְּסֵ֣תֶר עֶלְי֑וֹן, "He who dwells in the shelter of the Most High"). The *ohel* (אֹהֶל, "tent") parallels the *sukkah*—both are portable, impermanent structures associated with warfare and nomadic life. Yet both become metaphors for divine permanence: the God who "dwells" (*shokhen*, שֹׁכֵן) in a tent is the God who journeys with his people (Exod 25:8: וְעָשׂוּ לִי מִקְדָּשׁ וְשָׁכַנְתִּי בְּתוֹכָם, "Let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them").

The third metaphor shifts dramatically from textile shelters to geology: *betzur yeromemeni* (בְּ֝צ֗וּר יְרוֹמְמֵֽנִי, "upon a rock He will lift me high"). The term *tzur* (צוּר, "rock") appears 76 times in the Hebrew Bible, frequently as a divine epithet (Deut 32:4: הַצּוּר תָּמִים פָּעֳלוֹ, "The Rock—His work is perfect"; Ps 18:3: יְהֹוָה סַלְעִי וּמְצוּדָתִי, "The LORD is my rock and my fortress"). Here it functions both literally and metaphorically: the limestone cliffs of Judean geography provided physical refuge (David hid in rock fortresses; cf. 1 Sam 23:25: וַיֵּרֶד דָּוִד וַיֵּשֶׁב בַּמִּדְבָּר מָעוֹן, "David went down and stayed in the wilderness of Maon"), while theologically, YHWH himself is the ultimate *tzur*, the immovable foundation.

The verb *yeromemeni* (יְרוֹמְמֵֽנִי, Polel imperfect of *r-w-m*, "to lift up, raise high") emphasizes vertical movement—being elevated *above* the reach of enemies. This anticipates verse 6, where the psalmist's head is lifted high. The progression from *sukkah* (temporary shelter) to *ohel* (tent) to *tzur* (rock) moves from the ephemeral to the eternal, from fabric to stone, from human construction to divine nature. This triple metaphor encapsulates the theology of sanctuary: God's protection is at once mobile (tent), concealing (secret place), and immovable (rock).

Radak synthesizes these images: "God will hide me in His shelter on the day of trouble—in the temple precincts, as in a secure fortress" (כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְּסֻכֹּה בְּיוֹם רָעָה—בְּבֵית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ כְּבְמִבְצָר בָּצוּר). The temple is thus the architectural *actualization* of the divine *sukkah*, *ohel*, and *tzur*—a sacred geography where the vulnerable find invulnerability.

**Verse 6**

וְעַתָּ֨ה יָר֪וּם רֹאשִׁ֡י, עַ֤ל אֹיְבַ֬י סְֽבִיבוֹתַ֗י; וְאֶזְבְּחָ֣ה בְ֭אׇהֳלוֹ זִבְחֵ֣י תְרוּעָ֑ה; אָשִׁ֥ירָה וַ֝אֲזַמְּרָ֗ה לַֽיהֹוָֽה׃

The verse opens with the temporal adverb *ve'atah* (וְעַתָּ֨ה, "and now"), marking a dramatic shift from future confidence to present-tense reality. The Masoretic accentuation places a *pashta* on *ve'atah*, isolating it as a pivotal marker—"*now*," after the assurance of divine hiding (v. 5), the psalmist experiences the vindication. The verb *yarum roshi* (יָר֪וּם רֹאשִׁ֡י, "my head is lifted up") uses the Qal imperfect of *r-w-m*, the same root as *yeromemeni* (יְרוֹמְמֵֽנִי, "He will lift me high") from verse 5. The repetition creates lexical cohesion: the rock that lifts the person (v. 5) results in the lifted head (v. 6). But there's a tense shift: verse 5 uses the imperfect (*yeromemeni*, future/habitual), while verse 6 uses the imperfect *yarum* in a quasi-perfect sense, indicating imminent or realized action—"my head *is now* lifted" or "my head *will surely be* lifted."

The Septuagint resolves this ambiguity by translating *yarum* with the aorist *hypsōsen* (ὕψωσεν, "He lifted up"), converting the Hebrew imperfect into a past completed action: "And now He has lifted my head above my enemies." This interpretive move frames the psalm as a thanksgiving after deliverance, whereas the Hebrew preserves a more ambiguous temporality—confidence projected as present reality.

The phrase *yarum roshi* (יָר֪וּם רֹאשִׁ֡י, "my head is lifted") carries juridical and royal overtones. In ancient Near Eastern court protocol, to "lift the head" signified restoration to honor. In Genesis 40:13, Joseph tells Pharaoh's cupbearer: בְּעוֹד שְׁלֹשֶׁת יָמִים יִשָּׂא פַרְעֹה אֶת־רֹאשֶׁךָ וַהֲשִׁיבְךָ עַל־כַּנֶּךָ ("In three days Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore you to your office"). Conversely, to "lift the head" of the baker meant execution (Gen 40:19). The idiom thus spans a semantic range from restoration to judgment, but in Psalm 27, the lifted head is *above enemies* (*al oyvai sevivotai*, עַ֤ל אֹיְבַ֬י סְֽבִיבוֹתַ֗י, "above my enemies all around me"), indicating triumph.

The term *sevivotai* (סְֽבִיבוֹתַ֗י, "all around me") is a plural noun with a first-person singular suffix, derived from *saviv* (סָבִיב, "surrounding, round about"). It intensifies the threat: enemies do not attack from a single direction but encircle. Yet the psalmist's head is raised *al* (עַל, "above")—a preposition denoting elevation and superiority. The figurative language database reveals a similar usage in Psalm 3:4: וְאַתָּה יְהֹוָה מָגֵן בַּעֲדִי כְּבוֹדִי וּמֵרִים רֹאשִׁי ("But You, O LORD, are a shield about me, my glory, and the lifter of my head"), where God is explicitly the agent who lifts the head. Psalm 27:6, while passive or intransitive (*yarum*, without explicit agent), implies divine action through the broader context of verse 5.

The response to this vindication is cultic: *ve'ezbechah ve'oholo zivchei teruah* (וְאֶזְבְּחָ֣ה בְ֭אׇהֳלוֹ זִבְחֵ֣י תְרוּעָ֑ה, "I will sacrifice in His tent sacrifices of shouting/acclamation"). The cohortative *ezbechah* (אֶזְבְּחָה, "I will/let me sacrifice") expresses intention and volition. The phrase *zivchei teruah* (זִבְחֵ֣י תְרוּעָ֑ה, "sacrifices of shouting") is unique to this verse in the Hebrew Bible. The noun *teruah* (תְּרוּעָה) has a dual semantic range: it can denote the war cry or battle shout (Josh 6:5: וְהָרִיעוּ כָל־הָעָם תְּרוּעָה גְדוֹלָה, "All the people shall shout with a great shout"), or the cultic acclamation accompanying sacrifices (Ps 33:3: שִׁירוּ־לוֹ שִׁיר חָדָשׁ הֵיטִיבוּ נַגֵּן בִּתְרוּעָה, "Sing to Him a new song; play skillfully with a shout of joy"). BDB defines *teruah* as "shout or blast of war, alarm, or joy," capturing its martial and liturgical ambiguity. In Psalm 27:6, the two senses collapse: the war cry of victory becomes the worship shout of thanksgiving. This is a "sacrifice of shouting"—not a silent, somber ritual but a loud, joyful offering that declares triumph.

Rashi interprets *zivchei teruah* as "sacrifices over which a song is recited" (זְבָחִים שֶׁאוֹמֵר עֲלֵיהֶם שִׁיר), connecting the *teruah* to the Levitical singing that accompanied temple offerings (cf. 2 Chr 29:27-28: וּבְהֵחֵל הָעוֹלָה הֵחֵל שִׁיר יְהֹוָה וְהַחֲצֹצְרוֹת... וְכָל־הַקָּהָל מִשְׁתַּחֲוִים וְהַשִּׁיר מְשׁוֹרֵר וְהַחֲצֹצְרוֹת מַחְצְרִים, "And when the burnt offering began, the song to the LORD began also... and all the assembly worshiped, and the singers sang, and the trumpeters sounded"). This reading situates the psalm within active temple liturgy—not a hypothetical or future scenario but a present cultic reality.

The verse concludes with a double declaration of praise: *ashirah va'azamerah laYHWH* (אָשִׁ֥ירָה וַ֝אֲזַמְּרָ֗ה לַֽיהֹוָֽה, "I will sing and make music to the LORD"). The two verbs—*shir* (שִׁיר, "to sing") and *zamar* (זָמַר, "to make music, sing praise")—are near-synonyms that create a hendiadys of musical worship. The verb *zamar* often implies instrumental accompaniment (Ps 33:2: זַמְּרוּ־לוֹ בְנֵבֶל עָשׂוֹר, "Sing praises to Him with the ten-stringed lyre"), suggesting that the psalmist's worship involves both voice and instrument. This culminates the theological arc of verses 4-6: the desire to "dwell in the house" (v. 4) and to be "hidden in the sukkah" (v. 5) results in active worship—sacrificing, shouting, singing, and playing music (v. 6). The temple is not a passive refuge but a stage for exuberant praise.

The verse's liturgical afterlife is significant. In the Jewish High Holiday tradition, the phrase *zivchei teruah* (זִבְחֵ֣י תְרוּעָ֑ה, "sacrifices with shouting") is interpreted as alluding to the *shofar* blasts of Rosh Hashanah, where *teruah* designates a specific pattern of rapid, staccato notes (as opposed to *tekiah*, the sustained blast). The psalm's recitation during Elul thus frames the approaching Days of Awe as a time of spiritual triumph—the enemy is not an external army but the internal accuser (the *satan* of the High Holy Days), and the "lifted head" represents vindication in the divine court.

**Verse 7**

שְׁמַע־יְהֹוָ֖ה קוֹלִ֥י אֶקְרָ֗א; וְחׇנֵּ֥נִי וַעֲנֵֽנִי׃

With this verse, the psalm undergoes a dramatic tonal shift. After six verses of confidence, triumph, and cultic joy, the psalmist suddenly addresses God directly in the imperative mood: *shema-YHWH qoli* (שְׁמַע־יְהֹוָ֖ה קוֹלִ֥י, "Hear, O LORD, my voice"). The verb *shema* (שְׁמַע, imperative of *sh-m-ʿ*) is a command, though in Hebrew as in English, imperatives directed to the deity are softened by the relational context—they are petitions clothed in command forms. The structure *shema + qoli* ("Hear my voice") is a standard opening for laments and petitions (cf. Ps 5:3: הַקְשִׁיבָה לְקוֹל שַׁוְעִי מַלְכִּי וֵאלֹהָי, "Give ear to the sound of my cry, my King and my God"; Ps 64:2: שְׁמַע־אֱלֹהִים קוֹלִי בְשִׂיחִי, "Hear my voice, O God, in my complaint").

The phrase *qoli eqra* (קוֹלִ֥י אֶקְרָ֗א, "my voice I cry out") adds a verbal clause to the noun, creating emphasis: "Hear my voice *as I cry out*" or "my voice—*I am crying out*." The verb *qara* (קָרָא, Qal imperfect 1cs) means "to call, cry out, proclaim," often with the sense of summoning or invoking divine attention. The imperfect tense indicates either ongoing action ("I am crying") or habitual action ("I cry"). The phonetic stress pattern (`shə-maʿ-yə-hō-**WĀH** qō-**LIY** 'eq-**RĀ'**`) places emphasis on the divine name (YHWH), the speaker's voice (*qoli*), and the act of crying (*eqra*), aurally underscoring the desperation of the petition.

This tonal shift has generated the most sustained scholarly debate about Psalm 27's unity. Hermann Gunkel and form critics who followed him argued that verses 1-6 constitute a "Song of Trust" (*Vertrauenslied*), while verses 7-14 represent an "Individual Lament" (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*), and that a later redactor clumsily joined two originally separate compositions. The evidence for this view includes:

1. **Genre shift**: Verses 1-6 address God in the third person ("The LORD is my light," "He will hide me"), while verses 7-14 address God in the second person ("Hear my voice," "Do not hide Your face").
2. **Emotional shift**: The serene confidence of "My heart shall not fear" (v. 3) seems psychologically incompatible with the anxiety of "Do not hide Your face from me" (v. 9).
3. **Lack of narrative causality**: Nothing in verses 1-6 explains the sudden pivot to petition in verse 7.

However, Sigmund Mowinckel and subsequent scholars have defended the psalm's unity by emphasizing its cultic *Sitz im Leben* (setting in life). Mowinckel argued that liturgy naturally encompasses a range of emotions and that the psalm reflects a temple ritual where a worshiper (1) recites a credo of trust as they approach the sanctuary (vv. 1-6), (2) presents their petition before the altar (vv. 7-12), and (3) receives a priestly or prophetic oracle of assurance (vv. 13-14). In this reading, the "shift" is not an editorial seam but a liturgical progression.

Modern literary criticism has reinforced the psalm's unity by identifying rhetorical and lexical links that bind the two halves. Key terms recur: *yeshu'ah* (salvation, v. 1, v. 9), *oyvai* (my enemies, v. 2, v. 6, v. 12), the "house/tent" motif (vv. 4-6, echoed in vv. 9-10), and the "seeking" vocabulary (*baqash*, vv. 4, 8). The psalmist's initial confidence in verses 1-3 is *grounded* in the desire to dwell in the house (v. 4); that desire is *actualized* in worship (v. 6); but the *maintenance* of that relationship requires ongoing petition (vv. 7-12). The psalm thus models the full arc of faith: from objective truth (God's nature, vv. 1-3), to subjective desire (dwelling, vv. 4-6), to relational interaction (petition, vv. 7-12), to eschatological hope (v. 13-14).

The second half of verse 7—*vechoneni va'aneni* (וְחׇנֵּ֥נִי וַעֲנֵֽנִי, "and be gracious to me and answer me")—stacks two more imperative petitions. The verb *chanan* (חָנַן) means "to be gracious, show favor," often used in petitions for divine mercy (e.g., Ps 4:2: חׇנֵּנִי וּשְׁמַע תְּפִלָּתִי, "Be gracious to me and hear my prayer"; Ps 9:14: חׇנֵּנִי יְהֹוָה רְאֵה עׇנְיִי, "Be gracious to me, O LORD; see my affliction"). The root *ch-n-n* appears 77 times in the Hebrew Bible, predominantly in Psalms, establishing it as a core petitionary term. The second verb, *aneni* (עֲנֵֽנִי, "answer me"), from the root *ʿ-n-h* (to answer, respond), appears frequently in laments (Ps 4:2: בְּקׇרְאִי עֲנֵנִי, "Answer me when I call"; Ps 13:4: הַבִּיטָֽה עֲנֵנִי יְהֹוָה, "Consider and answer me, O LORD"). The pairing of *chanan* and *anah* creates a progression: "Be gracious" (grant favor) leads to "Answer" (provide a concrete response). The psalmist seeks not just God's benevolence but God's active intervention.

The Septuagint translates this verse as *eisakouson, Kyrie, tēs phōnēs mou hēs ekekraxa; eleēson me kai eisakouson mou* ("Hear, O Lord, my voice which I cried; have mercy on me and hear me"), using *eleēson* (have mercy) for *vechoneni* and *eisakouson* (hear) for *va'aneni*. The Greek *eleēson* (ἐλέησον, "have mercy") introduces the semantic field of mercy (*eleos*) that will become central to Christian liturgical usage (e.g., *Kyrie eleison*, "Lord, have mercy"). However, the Hebrew *chanan* is more specific—it denotes *favor* or *graciousness* rather than *mercy* in the sense of withholding punishment. The distinction is subtle but significant: *chanan* asks God to turn His face toward the petitioner in benevolence, while *eleos* asks God to withhold wrath. The psalm's movement from confidence (vv. 1-6) to petition (v. 7) is thus not a movement from innocence to guilt but from proclamation to personal appeal.

**Verse 8**

לְךָ֤ ׀ אָמַ֣ר לִ֭בִּי, בַּקְּשׁ֣וּ פָנָ֑י; אֶת־פָּנֶ֖יךָ יְהֹוָ֣ה אֲבַקֵּֽשׁ׃

This verse is the hermeneutical and theological center of the psalm, the axis around which the entire composition rotates. The syntax is notoriously difficult, generating centuries of debate among grammarians and exegetes. The Hebrew *lekha amar libbi* (לְךָ֤ ׀ אָמַ֣ר לִ֭בִּי, "To You/On Your behalf my heart has said") uses the preposition *lamed* with a second-person singular suffix (*lekha*, לְךָ֤, "to/for you"), creating grammatical ambiguity. Does *lekha* mean "to You" (indicating that the heart speaks *to* God), "for You" (the heart speaks *on behalf of* God), or "concerning You" (the heart speaks *about* God)? The three readings yield dramatically different theologies.

The Masoretic punctuation places a *reviaʿ* (a major disjunctive accent) after *lekha*, isolating it as a syntactic unit: "*To You* | my heart has said." This supports the interpretation that *lekha* is the indirect object of *amar* (אָמַר, "said")—the heart speaks *to* God. However, most medieval and modern commentators reject this parsing because of what follows: *baqshu fanai* (בַּקְּשׁ֣וּ פָנָ֑י, "Seek My face"). The verb *baqshu* (בַּקְּשׁוּ, plural imperative of *b-q-sh*) is in the second-person plural, and the noun *fanai* (פָנָ֑י, "My face") has a first-person singular suffix. The clause thus reads as a divine command: "Seek *My* face" (spoken by God). But if God is the speaker of *baqshu fanai*, then *lekha amar libbi* cannot mean "my heart said *to You*" (which would make the heart the speaker); instead, *lekha* must mean "on Your behalf" or "concerning You." Rashi articulates this interpretation clearly: "On Your behalf, as Your agent, my heart says to me, 'All of you Israelites, seek My face'" (בִּשְׁבִילְךָ, בִּשְׁלִיחוּתֶיךָ אוֹמֵר לִבִּי: בַּקְּשׁוּ כֻּלְּכֶם, יִשְׂרָאֵל, אֶת פָּנַי). The heart, then, is the mediator of a divine command, an internal prophet through which God speaks.

The phrase *baqshu fanai* (בַּקְּשׁ֣וּ פָנָ֑י, "Seek My face") employs technical theological vocabulary. The idiom "to seek the face" (*baqash panim*) appears throughout the Hebrew Bible with cultic and legal connotations. In ancient Near Eastern royal protocol, "to see the face of the king" meant to be granted an audience (cf. 2 Sam 14:24: וְאֶל־פָּנַי לֹא יִרְאֶה, "But he must not see my face"). In Akkadian, the phrase *amāru pān šarri* ("to see the face of the king") was a technical term for court access. When transposed to the divine sphere, "seeking the face of God" meant visiting the sanctuary to stand in the divine presence and receive favor or judgment. The Psalms employ this idiom frequently: Psalm 24:6 describes those who "seek Your face" (מְבַקְשֵׁי פָנֶיךָ) as those who enter the temple; Psalm 105:4 commands, "Seek His face continually" (בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָיו תָּמִיד). In Israelite worship, where God was not represented by a cult statue (aniconism), "seeking the face" underwent a semantic shift: it no longer meant gazing upon an image but standing in the temple precincts where God's presence (*kavod*) was manifested.

The psalmist's response to this internalized divine command is immediate and emphatic: *et-panekha YHWH avaqesh* (אֶת־פָּנֶ֖יךָ יְהֹוָ֣ה אֲבַקֵּֽשׁ, "Your face, O LORD, I will seek"). The syntax places *et-panekha* (אֶת־פָּנֶ֖יךָ, "Your face") in the fronted position for emphasis—"*Your face*—this is what I seek." The verb *avaqesh* (אֲבַקֵּֽשׁ, Piel imperfect 1cs of *b-q-sh*) is in the cohortative mood, expressing determination: "I will seek" or "let me seek." The use of the divine name *YHWH* (יְהֹוָ֣ה) makes explicit what was implicit in the command—the "face" to be sought is specifically the face of Israel's covenant God.

The verse's structure creates a chiastic exchange:
- A: *lekha* (To You / On Your behalf)
- B: *amar libbi* (my heart has said)
- C: *baqshu fanai* (**Seek My face**)
- B': *et-panekha* (Your face)
- A': *YHWH avaqesh* (O LORD, I will seek)

The heart mediates between the divine command ("Seek *My* face") and the human response ("*Your* face I will seek"). The first-person/second-person shift (*fanai* → *panekha*, "My face" → "Your face") mirrors the movement from divine speech to human obedience.

Radak offers a psychological reading that attempts to resolve the grammatical tension: "The psalmist speaks as if conducting an internal dialogue. His heart addresses him, conveying what God has commanded: 'Seek My face.' The psalmist then responds to his own heart, affirming, 'Your face, O LORD, I will indeed seek'" (לְךָ לִבִּי אָמַר: בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי. וְלִבּוֹ מְשִׁיב: אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ ה׳ אֲבַקֵּשׁ). This interpretation treats the verse as a three-part exchange: (1) God issues a command, (2) the heart internalizes and relays it, (3) the self responds in obedience. This psychological reading transforms the grammatical difficulty into a theological strength—the verse enacts the process of inner conversion, where divine imperative becomes human volition.

The Septuagint translates the first clause as *soi eipen hē kardia mou* ("To You my heart has said"), followed by *se ezētēsen to prosōpon mou* ("my face has sought You"). The Greek translator apparently read the Hebrew differently, taking *panekha* (Your face) as the subject rather than the object and *avaqesh* as a third-person verb rather than first-person. This yields: "To You my heart has said; my face has sought You—Your face, O LORD, I will seek." The result is smoother syntactically but theologically flatter—it loses the Hebrew's dramatic "Seek My face" command and the chiastic exchange between divine imperative and human response.

In Jewish liturgy, this verse has become central to the spirituality of the High Holy Days. The medieval custom of reciting Psalm 27 daily from Elul through Sukkot frames the penitential season as a time of "seeking God's face"—not in the sense of mystical vision but in the juridical sense of standing before the divine Judge and pleading one's case. The *baqshu fanai* ("Seek My face") is interpreted as God's invitation to approach during the Days of Awe, while the response *et-panekha YHWH avaqesh* ("Your face, O LORD, I will seek") becomes the worshiper's commitment to attend to the work of repentance (*teshuvah*).

The phrase *baqshu panai* appears in the Yom Kippur *Ne'ilah* service (the concluding service as the gates of heaven "close"), where it functions as a final plea for admission into the divine presence. The liturgy quotes Hosea 5:15: אֵלֵךְ אָשׁוּבָה אֶל־מְקוֹמִי עַד אֲשֶׁר־יֶאְשְׁמוּ וּבִקְשׁוּ פָנָי בַּצַּר לָהֶם יְשַׁחֲרֻנְנִי ("I will return to My place until they acknowledge their guilt and seek My face; in their distress they will seek Me earnestly"), combining it with Psalm 27:8 to create a liturgical call-and-response: God withdraws, declaring, "Seek My face"; Israel responds, "Your face, O LORD, we will seek."

**Verse 9**

אַל־תַּסְתֵּ֬ר פָּנֶ֨יךָ ׀ מִמֶּנִּי֮; אַ֥ל תַּט־בְּאַ֗ף עַ֫בְדֶּ֥ךָ; עֶזְרָתִ֥י הָיִ֑יתָ; אַֽל־תִּטְּשֵׁ֥נִי וְאַל־תַּ֝עַזְבֵ֗נִי, אֱלֹהֵ֥י יִשְׁעִֽי׃

If verse 8 is the theological center of the psalm, verse 9 is its emotional nadir. The fourfold negation—*al... al... al... ve'al* (אַל־... אַ֥ל... אַֽל־... וְאַל־, "Do not... do not... do not... and do not")—creates the most desperate moment in the composition. The quadruple anaphora of the negative particle *al* (used before imperfect verbs to express prohibition) pounds with increasing urgency, mimicking the psalmist's rising panic. The phonetic stress pattern highlights this desperation: `'al-tas-**TĒR** pā-**NEY**-khā mim-men-**NIY** 'al tat-bə-**'AF** ʿav-**DE**-khā`—the stresses fall on the verbs of rejection (*taster*, "hide"; *tat*, "turn away") and the pronouns of intimacy (*panekha*, "Your face"; *avdekha*, "Your servant"), aurally dramatizing the tension between closeness and abandonment.

The first prohibition—*al-taster panekha mimmeni* (אַל־תַּסְתֵּ֬ר פָּנֶ֨יךָ ׀ מִמֶּנִּי֮, "Do not hide Your face from me")—directly inverts the command of verse 8. There, the psalmist pledged to "seek Your face" (*et-panekha avaqesh*); here, the fear is that the face sought will be hidden. The verb *satar* (סָתַר, Hiphil of *s-t-r*) means "to hide, conceal," and it appears in the construct *hester panim* (הֶסְתֵּר פָּנִים, "hiding of the face"), a technical term in biblical theology for divine withdrawal. This idiom occurs in key covenantal contexts: Deuteronomy 31:17-18 warns that if Israel apostatizes, וְהִסְתַּרְתִּי פָנַי מֵהֶם... וְאָנֹכִי הַסְתֵּר אַסְתִּיר פָּנַי ("I will hide My face from them... Indeed, I will utterly hide My face"); Isaiah 54:8 describes divine wrath as בְּשֶׁצֶף קֶצֶף הִסְתַּרְתִּי פָנַי רֶגַע מִמֵּךְ ("In a surge of anger I hid My face from you for a moment"). The "hiding of the face" signifies the rupture of relationship, the withdrawal of favor, the suspension of covenant protection. It is the theological catastrophe worse than any military defeat—not to *lose* the battle but to *lose God*.

The textual relationship with Psalm 102:3 is striking. There, another desperate psalmist pleads: אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי בְּיוֹם צַר־לִי הַטֵּה־אֵלַי אׇזְנֶךָ ("Do not hide Your face from me in the day of my distress; incline Your ear to me"). The Near Eastern concordance reveals this as a lament formula—a standard plea in contexts of abandonment and crisis. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve both Psalm 27 and Psalm 102, and the shared vocabulary suggests a common liturgical tradition of "face theology" in Second Temple worship.

The second prohibition—*al-tat be'af avdekha* (אַ֥ל תַּט־בְּאַ֗ף עַ֫בְדֶּ֥ךָ, "Do not turn away Your servant in anger")—shifts the imagery from hiding to turning away. The verb *natah* (נָטָה, Hiphil imperative) means "to stretch out, turn aside, thrust away." BDB lists multiple nuances: to incline (favorably, as in Ps 40:2: וַיֵּט אֵלַי, "He inclined to me"), to bend down, or to thrust aside (as in rejection). Here, the context of *be'af* (בְּאַ֗ף, "in anger") clarifies the negative sense—the fear is being "thrust aside" or "pushed away" in divine wrath. The phrase *be'af* (in anger) employs the noun *af* (אַף), literally "nose" or "nostrils," which by metonymy signifies anger (since the nose flares in rage). The construct *be'af* appears frequently in prophetic judgment oracles (e.g., Jer 3:12: כִּי חָסִיד אָנִי... לֹא־אָטוֹר לְעוֹלָם, "For I am merciful... I will not be angry forever").

The self-designation *avdekha* (עַ֫בְדֶּ֥ךָ, "Your servant") invokes the covenantal language of vassalage. In ancient Near Eastern treaties, a "servant" (*eved* in Hebrew, *ardu* in Akkadian) was a vassal bound by loyalty to a suzerain king. By calling himself *avdekha*, the psalmist claims a legal relationship with YHWH—he is not a stranger pleading for mercy but a *covenant member* appealing to covenant obligations. The prophet Isaiah uses similar language when he calls Israel *avdi* (עַבְדִּי, "My servant," Isa 41:8-9). The term invokes both humility (servant status) and boldness (covenant rights).

The central clause of the verse—*ezrati hayita* (עֶזְרָתִ֥י הָיִ֑יתָ, "You have been my help")—interrupts the sequence of prohibitions with a past-tense affirmation. The noun *ezrah* (עֶזְרָה, "help, assistance") appears with the first-person singular suffix (*ezrati*, "my help"), and the verb *hayah* (הָיָה, "to be") is in the perfect tense (*hayita*, "You have been"). This grammatical shift to the perfect is rhetorically crucial—it grounds the plea in historical memory. The psalmist is not making an abstract theological claim but appealing to *experience*: "You *were* my help in the past; therefore, do not abandon me now." This argument from precedent is a common lament strategy (e.g., Ps 22:10-11: כִּי אַתָּה גֹחִי מִבָּטֶן... מִבֶּטֶן אִמִּי אֵלִי אָתָּה, "For You drew me from the womb... from my mother's womb You have been my God"; Ps 71:5-6: כִּי־אַתָּה תִּקְוָתִי אֲדֹנָי יְהֹוִה... עָלֶיךָ נִסְמַכְתִּי מִבֶּטֶן, "For You are my hope, O Lord GOD... upon You I have leaned from birth").

The final double prohibition—*al-titsheni ve'al-ta'azveni* (אַֽל־תִּטְּשֵׁ֥נִי וְאַל־תַּ֝עַזְבֵ֗נִי, "Do not forsake me and do not abandon me")—employs two verbs of abandonment in near-synonymous parallelism. The verb *natash* (נָטַשׁ, "to forsake, abandon") appears frequently in contexts of covenant rupture (Deut 32:15: וַיִּטֹּשׁ אֱלוֹהַּ עָשָׂהוּ, "He forsook the God who made him"; Jer 2:13: אֹתִי עָזְבוּ מְקוֹר מַיִם חַיִּים, "They have forsaken Me, the fountain of living waters"). The verb *azav* (עָזַב, "to leave, abandon, forsake") is equally dire (Deut 31:16: וְעָזַב אֹתִי, "And he will forsake Me"; Jer 1:16: אֲשֶׁר עֲזָבוּנִי, "who have forsaken Me"). The pairing of *natash* and *azav* creates hendiadys—a double expression for a single concept—but with intensification. The BDB lexicon distinguishes the two verbs subtly: *natash* often implies a deliberate casting off, while *azav* can mean simply leaving behind or neglecting. Together, they express total abandonment—both active rejection and passive neglect.

The verse concludes with the divine epithet *Elohei yish'i* (אֱלֹהֵ֥י יִשְׁעִֽי, "God of my salvation"). This phrase recalls the opening of the psalm: verse 1 declared YHWH as *yish'i* (יִשְׁעִי, "my salvation"), and now verse 9 repeats the term, creating an inclusio that binds the psalm's halves together. The epithet *Elohei yish'i* (God of my salvation) asserts that salvation is not an abstract hope but a divine identity—God *is* the psalmist's deliverance. The phrase appears elsewhere in Psalms (Ps 18:47: חַי־יְהֹוָה וּבָרוּךְ צוּרִי וְיָרוּם אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי, "The LORD lives! Blessed be my rock, and exalted be the God of my salvation"; Ps 51:16: אֱלֹהִים אֱלֹהֵי תְשׁוּעָתִי, "O God, God of my salvation"), establishing it as a confession of dependence.

The Septuagint's rendering is revealing: *mē apostrepēs to prosōpon sou ap' emou* ("Do not turn Your face away from me") for the first clause, and *mē enkataleipēs me mēde hyperidēs me, ho theos ho sōtēr mou* ("Do not forsake me nor despise me, O God my Savior") for the final clauses. The Greek *enkataleipēs* (ἐγκαταλείπω, "to forsake utterly") and *hyperidēs* (ὑπεροράω, "to overlook, despise") are intensely emotional, capturing the pathos of the Hebrew. The phrase *ho theos ho sōtēr mou* (ὁ θεὸς ὁ σωτήρ μου, "the God, the Savior of me") translates *Elohei yish'i* and introduces the Greek term *sōtēr* (Savior), which will become central to Christian soteriology (e.g., Luke 1:47: καὶ ἠγαλλίασεν τὸ πνεῦμά μου ἐπὶ τῷ θεῷ τῷ σωτῆρί μου, "and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior").

The verse's liturgical afterlife is profound. In Jewish tradition, it appears in the *Aneinu* prayer recited on fast days, where the congregation pleads: אַל־תֵּפֶן אֶל־רִשְׁעֵנוּ וְאַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנּוּ וְאַל־תִּתְעַלַּם מִתְּחִנָּתֵנוּ ("Do not turn to our wickedness, do not hide Your face from us, and do not ignore our supplication"). The phrase "Do not hide Your face" has become the quintessential expression of the fear of divine abandonment, the ultimate petition of penitential prayer.

**Verse 10**

כִּֽי־אָבִ֣י וְאִמִּ֣י עֲזָב֑וּנִי; וַֽיהֹוָ֣ה יַאַסְפֵֽנִי׃

This verse interrupts the fourfold negation of verse 9 with a stunning affirmation. The causal conjunction *ki* (כִּֽי, "for/because") signals that what follows provides the *rationale* for the preceding plea. The structure is adversative: "For though my father and mother have forsaken me, yet the LORD will gather me." The verse employs a Ugaritic-attested merism—*av* (אָב, "father") and *em* (אֵם, "mother")—to represent the totality of human kinship bonds. The father-mother pair appears in ancient Near Eastern legal and wisdom texts as the archetypal human relationship, the most fundamental social bond. In Ugaritic literature (e.g., KTU 1.5 vi 11-12), the phrase *ab // um* (father // mother) functions as a merism for "parents" and, by extension, "family" or "all human protectors." To say "father and mother" is to invoke the strongest conceivable human loyalty.

The verb *azavuni* (עֲזָב֑וּנִי, "they have forsaken me") uses the perfect tense, indicating completed action: "they *have* forsaken" or "they *did* forsake." The verb *azav* (עָזַב) is the same verb used in verse 9 (*al-ta'azveni*, "do not abandon me"), creating lexical continuity. But here it describes human abandonment, not divine. The choice of the perfect tense is grammatically significant: unlike the imperfect/jussive verbs of verses 7-9 (which express ongoing fear or petition), the perfect here asserts a *fait accompli*—the abandonment has already occurred or is presented as such for rhetorical effect. This grammatical shift from hypothetical fear (v. 9: "do not forsake me") to categorical fact (v. 10: "they have forsaken me") heightens the pathos: parental abandonment is *real*, but divine abandonment is *unthinkable*.

The second clause—*va-YHWH ya'asfeni* (וַֽיהֹוָ֣ה יַאַסְפֵֽנִי, "but the LORD will gather me")—employs the verb *asaf* (אָסַף, "to gather, collect, take in"). The BDB lexicon defines *asaf* with a range of meanings: "to gather, assemble" (Gen 29:22: וַיֶּאֱסֹף יַעֲקֹב אֶת־כָּל־אַנְשֵׁי הַמָּקוֹם, "Jacob gathered all the men of the place"); "to gather to one's fathers" (i.e., to die, Gen 25:8: וַיֶּאֱסַף אֶל־עַמָּיו, "and he was gathered to his people"); and "to take in, receive, adopt" (as here). The verb *ya'asfeni* (יַאַסְפֵֽנִי) is in the imperfect tense, contrasting with the perfect *azavuni*: "they *have* forsaken" (past) but "the LORD *will* gather" (future or present habitual). This tense contrast creates theological drama: human abandonment is past and completed, but divine reception is ongoing or imminent.

The verb *asaf* has legal and social connotations. In ancient Near Eastern adoption formulas, a person who "takes in" (*asaf*) another assumes kinship responsibility. The verb thus implies more than mere rescue—it signifies *adoption* or *covenantal incorporation*. This reading is supported by Zechariah 10:8, where YHWH declares: אֶשְׁרְקָה לָהֶם וַאֲקַבְּצֵם כִּי פְדִיתִים ("I will whistle for them and gather them, for I have redeemed them"). The parallel with *padah* (פָּדָה, "to redeem") confirms that *asaf* (to gather) is a redemptive act, not just a spatial movement. In Psalm 27:10, YHWH "gathers" the orphaned psalmist into the divine household, compensating for the failure of biological parents.

The dramatic force of this verse has generated multiple interpretive traditions. Rashi offers a biological reading: "For my father and my mother have forsaken me—at the moment of conjugal union they intended their own pleasure; once their pleasure is complete, this one turns his face one way and that one turns her face the other way. But the Holy One, blessed be He, guards the droplet and forms the fetus" (כִּי אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי—בִּשְׁעַת תַּשְׁמִישׁ לַהֲנָאָתָן נִתְכַּוְּנוּ; כֵּיוָן שֶׁגָּמְרוּ הֲנָאָתָן, זֶה הוֹפֵךְ פָּנָיו אֵילָךְ וְזֶה הוֹפֵךְ פָּנָיו אֵילָךְ. וַיהוָה יַאַסְפֵנִי—הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא שׁוֹמֵר אֶת הַטִּפָּה וְצָר אֶת הָעֻבָּר). Rashi's reading transforms the verse into a statement about divine providence in embryonic formation: human parents "abandon" the child at conception (having no further biological role), while God sustains the fetus. This interpretation, rooted in b. Niddah 31a, reframes "abandonment" as inevitable human limitation rather than moral failure.

Ibn Ezra, however, reads the verse hypothetically: "Though my father and mother, who were the causes of my coming into this world, should forsake me through death, You have gathered me and sustained me always" (כִּי אָבִי וְאִמִּי—שֶׁהָיוּ סִבּוֹת בּוֹאִי בָּעוֹלָם הַזֶּה עֲזָבוּנִי בְּמוֹתָם, וְאַתָּה אֲסַפְתַּנִי תָּמִיד). This reading treats parental death as a natural severing of kinship bonds, making the verse a meditation on divine permanence: human relationships are temporary, but God remains. Radak similarly interprets *azavuni* as referring to the psalmist's exit from parental care: "After I left their jurisdiction, You gathered me—that is, You provided for my sustenance and needs" (אַחַר שֶׁיָּצָאתִי מִגְּבוּלָם אֲסַפְתַּנִי אַתָּה, כְּלוֹמַר: זִמַּנְתָּ לִי מְחִיָּתִי וְצְרָכַי).

Modern scholars have proposed that the verse reflects a specific social crisis—literal parental abandonment or orphanhood. The legal codes of the ancient Near East address orphan protection (e.g., the Code of Hammurabi §§185-186 on adoption), and the Hebrew Bible repeatedly commands care for the *yatom* (יָתוֹם, "orphan"). Deuteronomy 10:18 declares that God עֹשֶׂה מִשְׁפַּט יָתוֹם וְאַלְמָנָה ("executes justice for the orphan and the widow"), and Hosea 14:4 promises that בְּךָ יְרֻחַם יָתוֹם ("in You the orphan finds mercy"). If the psalmist is literally an orphan—whether through death, abandonment, or social ostracism—then verse 10 becomes a confession of divine adoption: "Even if my parents cast me off, YHWH takes me in." This reading aligns with the broader theme of sanctuary in Psalm 27: the temple is the place where the socially vulnerable (orphans, refugees, accused persons) find protection.

The figurative language database reveals a powerful parallel in Zechariah 10:8-10, where YHWH declares: אֶשְׁרְקָה לָהֶם וַאֲקַבְּצֵם כִּי פְדִיתִים וְרָבוּ כְּמוֹ רָבוּ... כִּי אֲנִי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיהֶם וְאֶעֱנֵם ("I will whistle for them and gather them, for I have redeemed them, and they shall multiply as they multiplied... for I am the LORD their God, and I will answer them"). The verb *qabatz* (קָבַץ, "to gather") is synonymous with *asaf* (אָסַף), and both are paired with redemptive language (*padah*, פָּדָה, "to redeem"). This prophetic usage confirms that "gathering" is a covenantal act—YHWH gathers the scattered, redeems the abandoned, and reconstitutes the broken family as the people of God.

The Septuagint translates verse 10 as *hoti ho patēr mou kai hē mētēr mou enkatelipon me, ho de kyrios proselabeto me* (ὅτι ὁ πατήρ μου καὶ ἡ μήτηρ μου ἐγκατέλιπόν με, ὁ δὲ κύριος προσελάβετό με, "For my father and my mother have utterly forsaken me, but the Lord has taken me to Himself"). The Greek *proslambanō* (προσλαμβάνω, "to take to oneself, receive, accept") is the standard term for adoption and incorporation into community (cf. Acts 18:26: καὶ προσελάβοντο αὐτὸν, "and they took him aside"; Rom 14:1: τὸν δὲ ἀσθενοῦντα τῇ πίστει προσλαμβάνεσθε, "As for the one weak in faith, welcome him"). The LXX thus emphasizes the adoption motif, making YHWH the adoptive father who compensates for biological parental failure.

This verse has become a foundational text for Christian orphan theology and ministry. The Church Fathers interpreted it typologically: the "father and mother" who forsake represent the old covenant or the Law, while the LORD who gathers represents the new covenant in Christ. Augustine, in *Enarrationes in Psalmos*, reads the verse eschatologically: the abandonment refers to the soul's separation from God through sin, and the "gathering" is the soul's reception into the Church. In modern Christian liturgy, the verse is often used in adoption ceremonies and orphan ministry contexts, proclaiming God as the ultimate Father who takes in the fatherless.

The verse's brevity belies its emotional and theological density. It is a confession of ultimate dependence: even the strongest human bond (parent-child) can fail, but the divine-human bond remains unbreakable. The psalmist does not ask *if* God will gather; he declares that God *will* gather (*ya'asfeni*), using the imperfect as a modal of certainty. The verse thus serves as the hinge between the desperate petition of verse 9 and the confident petition for guidance in verse 11: the abandonment by humans has been absorbed into the adoption by God, creating the security necessary to ask for the "path."

**Verses 11-12**

הוֹרֵנִי יְהֹוָה דַּרְכֶּךָ, וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, לְמַעַן שֽׁוֹרְרָֽי׃ אַֽל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי; כִּי קָמוּ־בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, וִיפֵחַ חָמָֽס׃

Verses 11-12 form a couplet that shifts from confident affirmation (v. 10) back to urgent petition. The pairing is thematically unified: verse 11 requests divine guidance, and verse 12 explains why such guidance is necessary—the psalmist faces juridical enemies who threaten his life through false testimony.

Verse 11 opens with the imperative *horeni* (הוֹרֵנִי, "teach me" or "instruct me"), from the root *y-r-h* (Hiphil: "to throw, shoot, instruct"). This is the same root that generates the noun *torah* (תּוֹרָה, "instruction, law"). BDB defines the Hiphil of *yarah* as "to point out, direct, teach," often used for divine instruction (Exod 4:12: וְהוֹרֵיתִיךָ אֲשֶׁר תְּדַבֵּר, "and I will teach you what you shall speak"; Ps 25:8: יוֹרֶה חַטָּאִים בַּדָּרֶךְ, "He instructs sinners in the way"). The phrase *horeni YHWH darkhekha* (הוֹרֵנִי יְהֹוָה דַּרְכֶּךָ, "Teach me, O LORD, Your way") is a standard Psalmic formula for requesting moral and existential guidance (Ps 25:4: דְּרָכֶיךָ יְהֹוָה הוֹדִיעֵנִי אֹרְחוֹתֶיךָ לַמְּדֵנִי, "Make me know Your ways, O LORD; teach me Your paths"; Ps 86:11: הוֹרֵנִי יְהֹוָה דַּרְכֶּךָ אֲהַלֵּךְ בַּאֲמִתֶּךָ, "Teach me Your way, O LORD, that I may walk in Your truth").

The noun *derekh* (דֶּרֶךְ, "way, road, path") appears over 700 times in the Hebrew Bible with both literal and metaphorical senses. Literally, it denotes a physical road or journey; metaphorically, it signifies a "course of life" or "moral conduct." The phrase "the way of the LORD" (*derekh YHWH*, דֶּרֶךְ יְהוָה) refers to the path of righteousness prescribed by God (Gen 18:19: כִּי יְדַעְתִּיו לְמַעַן אֲשֶׁר־יְצַוֶּה אֶת־בָּנָיו... וְשָׁמְרוּ דֶּרֶךְ יְהוָה לַעֲשׂוֹת צְדָקָה וּמִשְׁפָּט, "For I have known him, in order that he may command his children... to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice"). In wisdom literature, the "way" becomes a central metaphor for the life well-lived versus the life misdirected (Prov 2:8: לִנְצֹר אָרְחוֹת מִשְׁפָּט וְדֶרֶךְ חֲסִידָו יִשְׁמֹר, "guarding the paths of justice and preserving the way of His faithful ones").

The second clause—*uncheni be'orach mishor* (וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, "and lead me in a level path")—uses synonymous parallelism. The verb *nachah* (נָחָה, Qal imperative with waw-consecutive, "lead, guide") often describes God leading His people (Exod 15:13: נָחִיתָ בְחַסְדְּךָ עַם־זוּ גָּאָלְתָּ, "You have led in Your steadfast love the people whom You have redeemed"; Ps 23:3: יַנְחֵנִי בְמַעְגְּלֵי־צֶדֶק, "He leads me in paths of righteousness"). The noun *orach* (אֹרַח, "path, way") is a poetic synonym of *derekh*, and the two appear in parallel in multiple texts (e.g., Ps 25:4: דְּרָכֶיךָ... אֹרְחוֹתֶיךָ, "Your ways... Your paths"; Prov 2:13: הָעֹזְבִים אָרְחוֹת יֹשֶׁר לָלֶכֶת בְּדַרְכֵי־חֹשֶׁךְ, "who forsake the paths of uprightness to walk in the ways of darkness").

The adjective *mishor* (מִישׁוֹר, "level, straight, upright") is both literal and moral. BDB defines it as "a level place, plain" (geographically) and "evenness, uprightness" (morally). In Deuteronomy 32:4, YHWH is described as צַדִּיק וְיָשָׁר הוּא ("righteous and upright is He"), using the root *y-sh-r* from which *mishor* derives. To request an *orach mishor* (level path) is thus to request a *morally straightforward* path—a way that is clear, safe, and righteous, free from the crooked detours of deceit and sin. The figurative language database notes that "level path" imagery contrasts with "crooked ways" throughout Proverbs (e.g., Prov 2:15: אֲשֶׁר אָרְחֹתֵיהֶם עִקְּשִׁים וּנְלוֹזִים בְּמַעְגְּלוֹתָם, "whose paths are crooked, and who are devious in their ways").

The purpose clause—*lema'an shorerai* (לְמַעַן שֽׁוֹרְרָֽי, "because of my watchful enemies" or "for the sake of those who watch me")—provides the motive for the request. The noun *shorerai* (שֽׁוֹרְרָֽי, plural with first-person singular suffix) comes from the root *sh-r-r* (to watch, observe, lie in wait). BDB translates *shorer* as "watcher, adversary," often with hostile intent. The term appears rarely in the Hebrew Bible, but its usage in Psalm 54:7 is instructive: בְּרָעָה יָשִׁיב לְשֹׁרְרָי ("He repays my enemies with evil"). The "watchers" are thus not neutral observers but *hostile watchers*—those who scrutinize the psalmist's conduct, looking for missteps, ready to testify falsely or exploit moral failures.

Radak interprets *shorerai* as "those who watch me with hostile intent, seeking to accuse me if I stumble" (שֽׁוֹרְרָֽי—הַמְּצֹפִים אוֹתִי בְּעַיִן רָעָה, מְבַקְּשִׁים לְהַאֲשִׁימֵנִי אִם אֶכָּשֵׁל). This reading connects verse 11 to verse 12: the psalmist needs a "level path" precisely because enemies are watching, ready to use any deviation as evidence. The juridical context becomes clearer—the psalmist is not just fearing physical attack but legal assault. He must walk "uprightly" (*mishor*) because any moral ambiguity will be weaponized by "false witnesses."

Verse 12 explicitly introduces the juridical crisis: *al-titneneni benefesh tzarai* (אַֽל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי, "Do not deliver me over to the desire of my adversaries" or "Do not give me into the throat of my enemies"). The verb *natan* (נָתַן, "to give, deliver, hand over") in the Hiphil with the preposition *be-* indicates transfer of control. The noun *nefesh* (נֶפֶשׁ) has a vast semantic range in Hebrew: "soul, life, self, person, desire, appetite, throat." BDB notes that *nefesh* can mean the physical throat (Jonah 2:6: אֲפָפוּנִי מַיִם עַד־נֶפֶשׁ, "The waters closed in over me up to the throat"), the seat of desire (Prov 23:2: וְשַׂמְתָּ שַׂכִּין בְּלֹעֶךָ אִם־בַּעַל נֶפֶשׁ אָתָּה, "and put a knife to your throat if you are given to appetite"), or the life force itself (Gen 35:18: וַיְהִי בְּצֵאת נַפְשָׁהּ, "as her life was departing"). The phrase *benefesh tzarai* (בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי, "in/to the nefesh of my adversaries") thus carries a double sense: do not hand me over to their *desire* (to harm me) or to their *throat* (to devour me).

This ambiguity is theologically rich. The figurative language database shows that "throat" imagery often denotes insatiable consumption or violent speech (Ps 5:10: קֶבֶר־פָּתוּחַ גְּרוֹנָם, "Their throat is an open grave"; Jer 2:25: וַתֹּאמְרִי נוֹאָשׁ לֹא כִּי־אָהַבְתִּי זָרִים וְאַחֲרֵיהֶם אֵלֵךְ, "But you said, 'It is hopeless, for I have loved foreigners, and after them I will go'"). In Psalm 27:12, the enemies' *nefesh* is the locus of their destructive intent—whether as desire (legal aggression) or throat (violent consumption). The verse thus collapses the two threats seen earlier in the psalm: military attack ("devour my flesh," v. 2) and juridical attack ("false witnesses," v. 12).

The causal clause—*ki qamu-vi edei-sheqer veyafeach chamas* (כִּי קָמוּ־בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, וִיפֵחַ חָמָֽס, "for false witnesses have risen against me, and he who breathes violence" or "and a witness of violence")—makes explicit the legal crisis. The verb *qum* (קוּם, "to rise, stand up") in the perfect tense (*qamu*, "they have risen") indicates that the hostile action is underway. The phrase *qamu-vi* (קָמוּ־בִי, "they have risen against me") is juridical language, echoing the courtroom setting (Deut 19:16: כִּי־יָקוּם עֵד־חָמָס בְּאִישׁ לַעֲנוֹת בּוֹ סָרָה, "If a malicious witness arises against anyone to accuse him of wrongdoing").

The noun *edei-sheqer* (עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, "false witnesses" or "lying witnesses") is a technical legal term. The Hebrew *ed* (עֵד, "witness") appears in legal codes as the person whose testimony establishes truth in court (Deut 17:6: עַל־פִּי שְׁנַיִם עֵדִים... יוּמַת הַמֵּת, "On the evidence of two witnesses... the one who is to die shall be put to death"; Deut 19:15: עַל־פִּי שְׁנֵי עֵדִים... יָקוּם דָּבָר, "On the evidence of two witnesses... shall a charge be established").
