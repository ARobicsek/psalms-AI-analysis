# Editorial Assessment - Psalm 27

The existing introduction already does important work: it names the jarring tonal shift between confidence and plea; foregrounds the “one thing” of verse 4 as the psalm’s pivot; and reads Psalm 27 through the lens of temple-as-refuge and “threshold” theology. It also perceptively highlights asylum law and the legal threat of “false witnesses” in verse 12 and recognizes verse 9’s “Do not hide Your face” as the crisis point.

What’s missing is fuller use of the deep textual and reception history now available. The current essay barely touches the textual oddity of לוּלֵא (“lulei,” “unless”) in verse 13 and its *puncta extraordinaria*, or the Septuagint’s tendency to smooth the psalm’s tensions (e.g., turning hoped‑for deliverance into accomplished fact). It alludes to Ancient Near Eastern background but could say more concretely how Ugaritic *sukkah* imagery, solar deities, and royal audience language sharpen particular phrases like יְהוָה אוֹרִי (“YHWH is my light”) and בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי (“Seek My face”).

There are also rich “aha” links to related psalms (Psalm 13 and Psalm 102 on hiding God’s face; Psalm 55 on enemies and legal peril) and to modern reception (Rabbi Sacks on verse 10; liturgical Elul usage) that can be interwoven more tightly into the macro argument. A few technical terms (e.g., *aposiopesis*, *Vorlage*) need clear definitions for non‑specialists. Finally, the verse‑by‑verse notes are strong but can be more explicitly tied back to the overarching thesis about presence, asylum, and waiting.
