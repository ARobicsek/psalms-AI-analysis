The most unsettling word in Psalm 27 is also the most visibly disturbed. In verse 13, every letter of לוּלֵא (*lulei*), “unless” or “had I not,” is pricked with the scribes’ *puncta extraordinaria*—dots above (and in this unique case, below) the word:

> לׅׄוּׅׄלֵׅ֗ׄאׅׄ הֶ֭אֱמַנְתִּי לִרְא֥וֹת בְּטוּב־יְהוָ֗ה בְּאֶ֣רֶץ חַיִּֽים  
> “**Had I not** believed to see the goodness of YHWH in the land of the living…” (v. 13)

The sentence never finishes. “Had I not believed…,” and then silence. Classical rhetoric calls this an *aposiopesis*—a breaking off mid-sentence when the consequence is too dreadful to name. The Masoretes, unsure whether the word itself belongs, dotted it from every side. If you omit לוּלֵא, the line reads simply: הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת (“I believed to see…”), a straightforward affirmation of trust. With לוּלֵא, the verse trembles on the edge of an unspoken abyss. The psalm can be heard as pure confidence or as a confession that only belief stands between the speaker and collapse.

That tension runs through the whole poem. The opening is almost swaggering:

> יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי; מִמִּי אִירָא. יְהוָה מָעוֹז־חַיַּי; מִמִּי אֶפְחָד.  
> “YHWH is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?  
> YHWH is the stronghold of my life; of whom shall I be afraid?” (v. 1)

Yet before long we hear, with equal intensity:

> אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי… אַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי  
> “Do not hide Your face from me… do not abandon me, O God of my salvation” (v. 9).

Hermann Gunkel, the great form critic, concluded that we are dealing with two different psalms stitched together: a “Song of Trust” (vv. 1–6) and an “Individual Lament” (vv. 7–14). The shift from “Whom shall I fear?” to “Do not forsake me” is indeed abrupt. But the more closely you listen, the harder it is to keep the halves apart. Vocabulary, imagery, even small turns of phrase cross the supposed seam. Psalm 27 is less a clumsy splice than a carefully designed tension—an architecture for living in the “unless” of verse 13.

#### From sunlight to sanctuary

Verse 1 begins with a daring predicate: יְהוָה אוֹרִי, “YHWH is my light.” Nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible is God so baldly called “my light.” Light in the Ancient Near East was the special province of solar deities. Shamash in Mesopotamia, Re in Egypt, Shapash in Ugarit: these sun-gods both illuminate and judge. Their rays expose hidden wrongdoing. To claim “YHWH is my light” is not just to say “God comforts me”; it is to relocate the entire solar theology of justice and visibility from the icon-filled temples of Mesopotamia and Egypt into the imageless sanctuary of Jerusalem. The psalmist then adds, in quick succession, “my salvation” (יְשׁוּעָה, deliverance in battle) and “the stronghold of my life” (מָעוֹז־חַיַּי, a fortified refuge carved into rock). The rhetorical questions—“Whom shall I fear? Of whom shall I be afraid?”—summarize this triple metaphor. If the ultimate light, rescuer, and fortress is on my side, what is left to fear?

Verses 2–3 explore the worst that could happen: predators who “devour my flesh” (לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי, v. 2), siege camps (מַחֲנֶה), war itself (מִלְחָמָה). The “flesh-eating” idiom echoes prophets who accuse corrupt leaders of cannibalizing the people—“who eat the flesh of my people” (מֹאכְלֵי בְשַׂר עַמִּי, Mic 3:3)—and evokes the stock ANE taunt of making enemies “food for the sword.” Yet in Psalm 27 the imagery is suddenly reversed: “They stumbled and fell” (הֵמָּה כָּשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ, v. 2). The perfect verbs can describe past deliverances or, as many have noted, a future defeat narrated as already accomplished—a “prophetic perfect” of confidence.

So far, the psalm reads like a straightforward trust psalm. Then verse 4 arrives and quietly changes the subject.

#### The “one thing” redefines everything

> אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהוָה, אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ:  
> שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהוָה כָּל־יְמֵי חַיַּי;  
> לַחֲזוֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְהוָה, וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ.  
> “One thing I have asked of YHWH, that will I seek:  
> to dwell in the house of YHWH all the days of my life,  
> to gaze upon the beauty of YHWH, and to inquire in His temple” (v. 4).

אַחַת (“one [thing]”) is not a counting term here but a literary spotlight. In a world of many threats and many possible prayers—for victory, vengeance, health, prosperity—the psalmist claims a single desire. But it is not, as we might expect, “Deliver me from my enemies.” It is to “dwell” (שִׁבְתִּי) in the house of YHWH, to become, in effect, a permanent temple guest.

Everything in the verse is cultic, technical. To “gaze” (לַחֲזוֹת) is the verb used of prophetic vision—חָזָה is the root of חָזוֹן, “vision,” in the prophets. נֹעַם־יְהוָה (“the beauty/pleasantness of YHWH”) is a rare phrase. It can describe aesthetic delight, but in Psalm 90:17, נֹעַם אֲדֹנָי (“the beauty/favor of the Lord”) clearly means gracious favor. The psalmist wants both: the sensory splendor of the sanctuary and the favorable verdict that issues from it.

Then comes the difficult infinitive לְבַקֵּר. Rashi hears a pun and relates it to בֹּקֶר, “morning”: to visit the temple each morning. Modern philology, however, points to a more juridical meaning. In 2 Kings 16:15, King Ahaz dedicates the bronze altar “to inquire by” (לְבַקֵּר), that is, as an oracular device. In Leviticus 27:33, the same root means “to examine closely.” On that reading, the psalmist longs not merely to linger aesthetically in the temple but “to inquire in His palace”—to seek a divine ruling on his case.

That case surfaces explicitly in verse 12: “False witnesses have risen against me” (קָמוּ־בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר). The temple is not just a worship hall but a courthouse. In the Ancient Near East, to “see the face” of the god (*amāru pān īli* in Akkadian) meant gaining access to the divine statue for judgment or blessing. Israel, with no statue, kept the idiom but filled it with a different content. To “gaze upon the beauty of YHWH” and “inquire in His temple” is to be admitted to a non-iconic audience chamber, the holy of holies made of space and word rather than stone and gold.

Suddenly, the psalm’s earlier confidence is reoriented. The “stronghold” (מָעוֹז) of verse 1 is now located in the courts of God. Safety is no longer the absence of enemies but the presence of YHWH.

#### Sukkah, tent, rock: shelter as war-tent and high ground

Verse 5 develops this cultic turn with a triad of protective images:

> כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְּסֻכּוֹ בְּיוֹם רָעָה;  
> יַסְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵתֶר אׇהֳלוֹ;  
> בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי.  
> “For He will hide me in His *sukkah* on the day of trouble;  
> He will conceal me in the shelter of His tent;  
> He will raise me high upon a rock” (v. 5).

A *sukkah* (סֻכָּה) is a fragile booth made of branches—a festival hut, a shepherd’s shade, a field-shelter. Hardly an image of impregnability. Yet it is “His booth.” Ugaritic texts use the cognate *sk* for a deity’s pavilion on the divine mountain. The psalmist is thinking not of a backyard sukkah but of a celestial war-tent, an officer’s command post. The parallel phrase “the secrecy of His tent” (בְּסֵתֶר אׇהֳלוֹ) strengthens this: *ohel* is both the tabernacle word and the general’s tent.

The final line shifts from concealment to exposure: “On a rock He will raise me.” Instead of burying the suppliant in some crevice, God puts him on the high ground—out of reach, yes, but also conspicuously vindicated. In David’s own stories, rocks and strongholds in the Judean wilderness provide both safety and tactical advantage. The psalm turns that geography into theology.

Verse 6 then pictures the appropriate response: “I will offer in His tent sacrifices with *teru‘ah*” (זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה)—sacrifices accompanied by shofar blasts or war-shouts—and “I will sing and make music to YHWH” (אָשִׁירָה וַאֲזַמְּרָה לַי־הוָה). The Septuagint quietly changes “my head will be lifted high” (יָר֪וּם רֹאשִׁי, future) to “He lifted up” (ὕψωσεν, past), as if the ritual action already enacts the victory. In the Greek imagination, worship is a liturgical present in which the hoped-for triumph is celebrated as though accomplished.

#### “Seek my face”: presence as the true crisis

Against that backdrop, the shift in verse 7 is less a genre break than a change of camera angle. What had been recited as creed now becomes prayer:

> שְׁמַע־יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא;  
> וְחׇנֵּנִי וַעֲנֵנִי.  
> “Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call;  
> be gracious to me, and answer me” (v. 7).

Where verse 1 declared “YHWH is my salvation,” verse 7 asks whether YHWH will in fact hear. The internal drama is voiced in verse 8:

> לְךָ אָמַר לִבִּי ‘בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי’;  
> אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ יְהוָה אֲבַקֵּשׁ.  
> “On Your behalf my heart says, ‘Seek my face’;  
> Your face, YHWH, I seek” (v. 8).

The syntax is famously knotted. Rashi hears the heart acting “on Your behalf,” delivering a divine command to the self. Others read: “Concerning You my heart has said, ‘Seek His face.’” Either way, God’s imperative—בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי, “Seek My face”—and the human response—אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ… אֲבַקֵּשׁ, “Your face I seek”—form a miniature call and response. What God asks and what the heart most wants coincide.

Which is why verse 9 is so anguished:

> אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי;  
> אַל־תַּט בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ…  
> אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי וְאַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי, אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי.  
> “Do not hide Your face from me;  
> do not turn Your servant away in anger…  
> do not cast me off and do not forsake me, O God of my salvation” (v. 9).

To “hide the face” (הִסְתִּיר פָּנִים) is biblical shorthand for covenantal eclipse. Deuteronomy 31:17–18 has God warn Israel, “I will hide My face from them” (וְהִסְתַּרְתִּי פָנַי מֵהֶם) as the ultimate curse; Psalm 13:2 cries, “How long… will You hide Your face from me?” (עַד־אָנָה תַּסְתִּיר אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי). Psalm 102, associated with exile, uses almost identical words to ours: “Do not hide Your face from me on the day of my distress” (אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי בְּיוֹם צַר־לִי, Ps 102:3). The worst thing that can happen is not enemy attack but divine withdrawal. For a suppliant who has staked everything on “dwelling in the house of YHWH,” the nightmare is being turned away from the door.

The fourfold “do not”—hide, turn away, cast off, forsake—registers that terror. Yet, in the middle of this fragility, verse 10 flashes a strange, serene confidence:

> כִּי־אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי;  
> וַיהוָה יַאַסְפֵנִי.  
> “For my father and my mother have forsaken me,  
> but YHWH will gather me in” (v. 10).

Whether this is literal orphanhood or hyperbolic comparison, it imagines every foundational human bond snapping—and in that vacuum, a new kinship: “YHWH will gather me” (יַאַסְפֵנִי). The verb אָסַף (“gather”) is used of gathering exiles, harvest, even the dead being “gathered to their fathers.” Here it sounds like adoption. Later Jewish thinkers, from Radak to Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, have heard in this line the conviction that when human loyalties fail—family, community—divine loyalty does not. “Human parents may lose faith in a child,” Sacks wrote, “but God never does.” 

The psalm’s trust is not self-generated optimism; it is anchored in being “taken in” by a God who does not let go.

#### Law, lies, and the unspeakable “unless”

Verses 11–12 return to the legal texture hinted at earlier:

> הוֹרֵנִי יְהוָה דַרְכֶּךָ;  
> וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי.  
> “Teach me, YHWH, Your way;  
> lead me on a level path, because of my watchful foes” (v. 11).

The vocabulary—“way” (דֶּרֶךְ), “path” (אֹרַח), “level ground” (מִישׁוֹר)—belongs to Israel’s wisdom tradition. The problem is no longer just survival but navigating rightly under hostile surveillance. שׁוֹרְרַי (“those watching me”) seems to mean not merely “enemies” but those lying in wait for missteps.

> אַל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי;  
> כִּי קָמוּ־בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, וִיפֵחַ חָמָס.  
> “Do not give me over to the *nephesh* of my adversaries,  
> for false witnesses have risen up against me, and one who breathes out violence” (v. 12).

נֶפֶשׁ here is wonderfully ambiguous: it can mean “life,” “desire,” or even “throat.” To “give me over to the *nephesh* of my foes” is to hand me into the power of their appetite—recalling the flesh-devourers of verse 2. The threat has moved from battlefield to courtroom. עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר (“witnesses of falsehood”) is a technical legal term; Deuteronomy 19:16–19 legislates penalties for just such malicious testimony. The puzzling phrase וִיפֵחַ חָמָס, often translated “who breathes out violence,” likely hides another legal nuance. Ugaritic texts use a cognate root *pḥ* for “witness,” suggesting a parallel to עֵד שֶׁקֶר: a “witness of violence.” On this reading, the psalmist faces a stacked court in which both lies and legal process itself have become weapons.

Then comes the broken-backed verse 13. The Masoretic dots over לוּלֵא tell us that something is unstable here—either a word that crept in, or a word some ancient scribe was reluctant to strike out. Jewish tradition accepted the ambiguity and made it theology. The Talmud imagines David saying: “I knew that You pay a good reward to the righteous in the world to come, but I did not know whether I would have a share among them” (Berakhot 4a). Even David’s confidence is edged with “unless.”

Yet the line insists, even in its stammering syntax, that it is precisely emunah—faith, trust—that has kept the speaker from falling into that unspoken alternative.

#### Waiting as a taut hope

The psalm ends not with narrative closure but with an imperative:

> קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה; חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ; וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה.  
> “Wait for YHWH; be strong, and let your heart take courage; and wait for YHWH” (v. 14).

The verb קַוֵּה (from קוה) does not mean passive loitering. Its root evokes a cord pulled tight. To “wait” is to live in stretched expectancy. Isaiah uses the same root: “Those who wait for YHWH (קֹוֵי יְהוָה) shall renew their strength” (Isa 40:31). The middle clause—“be strong and let your heart take courage”—echoes God’s words to Joshua (חֲזַק וֶאֱמָץ, Josh 1:6–9) as he faces the daunting inheritance of the land. Here, that military exhortation is turned inward, aimed at the heart that must hold both “YHWH is my light” and “Do not hide Your face.”

Who speaks verse 14? It may be the psalmist addressing himself; it may be a priestly or prophetic voice answering the prayer; in Jewish liturgy it effectively becomes the community speaking to itself from Elul to Sukkot. Either way, it frames the entire psalm as a practice in disciplined waiting: not waiting for enemies to vanish, but waiting inside the presence that makes them, finally, non-ultimate.

The later Jewish custom of reciting Psalm 27 daily from the first of Elul through Hoshana Rabbah plays with this. Homilists noticed that לוּלֵא (lulei, “unless”) read backwards spells אֱלוּל (Elul). The dotted word in verse 13 thus becomes a seasonal emblem: a reminder that we live, for 51 days and well beyond, in the space between promise and fulfillment, fear and trust. In that space, Psalm 27 offers not a solution but an architecture: light, house, face, and finally, the taut line of hope—קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה—strung between them.

#### Full psalm

Psalm 27 is recited in full twice daily—from the first day of Elul through Hoshana Rabbah—in most Ashkenazi and many Sephardi and Mizraḥi communities. It is usually said after the morning and evening services. The opening line,

> יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי; מִמִּי אִירָא  
> “YHWH is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?”

is heard throughout the penitential season, with the midrashic reading: אוֹרִי (“my light”) for Rosh Hashanah, יִשְׁעִי (“my salvation”) for Yom Kippur, and “For He will hide me in His *sukkah*” (כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְּסֻכּוֹ, v. 5) for Sukkot. The psalm thus becomes a liturgical arc over the High Holy Days, emphasizing not only judgment and forgiveness but the desire to “dwell in the house of YHWH” (שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהוָה, v. 4).

#### Key verses

**Psalm 27:1 – יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי**

> יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי; מִמִּי אִירָא. יְהוָה מָעוֹז־חַיַּי; מִמִּי אֶפְחָד.  
> “YHWH is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear? YHWH is the stronghold of my life; of whom shall I be afraid?”

In many Sephardi rites, this verse is quoted in the introductory penitential verses of the Selichot for Yom Kippur eve, framing the night as one of trusting approach rather than sheer dread: 

> הִכּוֹן לִקְרַאת אֱלֹהֶיךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל: יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי מִמִּי אִירָא…  
> “Prepare to meet your God, O Israel: ‘YHWH is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear…’”

**Psalm 27:4 – אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי**

> אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהוָה, אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ…  
> “One thing I have asked of YHWH, that will I seek…”

This verse is widely set to song in contemporary Jewish practice and sung at Shabbat meals and gatherings as an expression of longing for closeness to God. In some High Holiday prayerbooks it appears as a meditative refrain between sections of Selichot, shifting the emphasis from fear of punishment to desire for presence.

**Psalm 27:7 – שְׁמַע־יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא**

> שְׁמַע־יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא; וְחׇנֵּנִי וַעֲנֵנִי.  
> “Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call; be gracious to me and answer me.”

In Eastern (Edot ha-Mizrach) Yom Kippur Selichot, this verse anchors a string of pleas beginning with שְׁמַע (“hear”): 

> שְׁמַע־יְהוָה וְחׇנֵּנִי, יְהוָה הֱיֵה־עֹזֵר לִי; שְׁמַע־יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא…  
> “Hear, YHWH, and be gracious to me; YHWH, be my helper. Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call…”

The liturgy turns the psalmist’s solitary cry into a communal chorus.

**Psalm 27:14 – קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה**

> קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה; חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ; וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה.  
> “Wait for YHWH; be strong, and let your heart take courage; and wait for YHWH.”

This verse is quoted before the hymn אֵין כֵּאלֹהֵינוּ (“There is none like our God”) in many siddurim:

> קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה. אֵין קָדוֹשׁ כַּי־הוָה כִּי אֵין בִּלְתֶּךָ…  
> “Wait for YHWH… and wait for YHWH. There is none holy like YHWH, for there is none besides You…”

Here, the psalm’s private self-exhortation becomes a communal summons to resilient faith.

#### Phrases

**לוּלֵא (v. 13) and אֱלוּל (Elul)**

> לוּלֵא הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת בְּטוּב־יְהוָה בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים.  
> “Had I not believed to see the goodness of YHWH in the land of the living…”

Homiletically, many teachers note that לוּלֵא backwards spells אֱלוּל (Elul). In Elul sermons and study sessions, this is used to frame the entire penitential period as dwelling in that fragile “unless”—sustained by faith in divine goodness despite uncertainty.

**בְּסֵתֶר אׇהֳלוֹ (v. 5)**

> יַסְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵתֶר אׇהֳלוֹ  
> “He will conceal me in the secrecy of His tent.”

In some Eastern memorial prayers (*hashkavah*), this phrase is adapted into a plea for the deceased:

> יַסְתִּירֵהוּ בְּצֵל כְּנָפָיו וּבְסֵתֶר אָהֳלוֹ…  
> “May He hide him in the shadow of His wings and in the secrecy of His tent…”

The temple-as-refuge becomes an image for post-mortem shelter in God.

**קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה**

As noted, this phrase is woven into weekday and Shabbat endings around אֵין כֵּאלֹהֵינוּ. There it no longer functions as the conclusion to a single psalm but as a bridge from prayer to daily life: a reminder, as the service ends, that religious experience continues as a practice of courageous waiting.
