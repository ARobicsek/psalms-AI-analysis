**Verse 1**

לְדָוִד ׀ יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי; מִמִּי אִירָא. יְהוָה מָעוֹז־חַיַּי; מִמִּי אֶפְחָד׃  

“Of David. YHWH is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear? YHWH is the stronghold of my life; of whom shall I be afraid?”

The verse piles three striking predicates onto the divine name: “my light” (אוֹרִי), “my salvation” (יִשְׁעִי), and “the stronghold of my life” (מָעוֹז־חַיַּי). Calling God light is unusually direct. Elsewhere light is associated with God’s action or presence (e.g., “YHWH will be my light,” מִי יְהוָה אוֹר לִי, Mic 7:8), but here God *is* light. In the Ancient Near Eastern imagination, light is the realm of solar deities like Shamash, whose rays reveal crime and render judgment. Psalm 27 appropriates that imagery polemically: the true source of illumination, including moral illumination, is YHWH.

“Salvation” (יְשׁוּעָה) is the language of rescue, often in battle. At the Red Sea, Moses promises Israel, “Stand firm and see the salvation of YHWH” (וּרְאוּ אֶת־יְשׁוּעַת יְהוָה, Exod 14:13). The psalmist compresses that assurance into “my salvation.” It is not an abstract principle but a personal, repeated experience.

מָעוֹז (ma‘oz) evokes a fortified place: a mountain stronghold or walled city. The Judean landscape, with its caves and crags, gave David literal strongholds in his flight from Saul (cf. En-Gedi). Radak notes that as enemies might lay siege to a tower, so enemies surround the psalmist—but the “tower” that shelters him is God’s own presence.

The two colons mirror one another syntactically (“YHWH is X… whom shall I fear? YHWH is Y… of whom shall I be afraid?”), intensifying the claim by variation. The verbs for fear, יִירָא and אֶפְחָד, shade slightly differently: ירא is reverent or rational fear; פחד is dread. The psalmist claims immunity to both measured apprehension and paralyzing terror.

Liturgically, this line’s prominence in Elul and Tishrei has led rabbis to read it temporally: “my light” for Rosh Hashanah, when God “illuminates” deeds; “my salvation” for Yom Kippur; the “stronghold” for Sukkot as festival of fragile protection. In that setting, the question “Whom shall I fear?” is not bravado but a seasonal task: to approach days of judgment from within a sanctuary of trust.

**Verse 2**

בִּקְרֹב עָלַי מְרֵעִים לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי; צָרַי וְאֹיְבַי לִי—הֵמָּה כָּשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ׃  

“When evildoers draw near against me to devour my flesh, my adversaries and my foes—it is they who stumbled and fell.”

The first colon brings the threat right up to the body. מְרֵעִים (“evildoers”) are not abstract wrongdoers but a specific group advancing “against me” (עָלַי). The striking phrase לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי, “to eat my flesh,” is visceral. Elsewhere “eating flesh” describes rapacious exploitation: “who eat my people’s flesh” (אֹכְלֵי בְשַׂר עַמִּי, Mic 3:3) or social cannibalism in siege conditions (Zech 11:9). Psalm 53:5 images enemies as “eaters of my people” (אֹכְלֵי עַמִּי), treating people like bread (אָכְלוּ לֶחֶם). Here, the figure compresses physical violence, slander (“devouring” reputation), and legal predation into one metaphor of consumption.

The second half names “my adversaries and my foes” (צָרַי וְאֹיְבַי) and snaps back with a perfect-verb doublet: “they stumbled and fell.” The emphasis on הֵמָּה (“they themselves”) underscores the reversal. As Malbim notes, the psalmist does not say, “I struck them”; their downfall is almost self-inflicted in the presence of divine protection.

The temporal sense of the perfects is fluid. They can refer to remembered past deliverances, lending substance to present trust. Or they can be “prophetic perfects,” narrating future divine action as already accomplished. The ambiguity is part of the effect: as soon as the enemies “draw near” (בִּקְרֹב), the psalmist’s imagination already sees them prostrate.

This verse quietly prepares two later themes. First, the devouring imagery anticipates “false witnesses” and the “throat/desire” (נֶפֶשׁ) of foes in verse 12: life and reputation are at stake. Second, the combination “adversaries/foes” returns in verse 6, where the psalmist’s head is raised “over my enemies all around.” The movement from being food to standing over them is the arc the psalm will traverse through worship.

**Verse 3**

אִם־תַּחֲנֶה עָלַי מַחֲנֶה—לֹא יִירָא לִבִּי; אִם־תָּקוּם עָלַי מִלְחָמָה—בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ׃  

“Though an army encamp against me, my heart will not fear; though war rise up against me, in this I trust.”

Here the imagined threat escalates from attackers to a full encamped army (מַחֲנֶה), then to outright war (מִלְחָמָה). The two clauses introduced by אִם (“if/even if”) form a kind of poetic thought experiment: What if the worst happens? Even then, says the psalmist, “my heart” (לִבִּי—the inner core of thought and will) is steady, and “in this” (בְּזֹאת) he trusts.

Commentators disagree on בְּזֹאת. Rashi and Metzudat David take it as pointing back: “in what I have just said—that YHWH is the stronghold of my life—I trust.” Others, like Malbim, suggest it anticipates the “one thing” of verse 4: the choice to set one’s desire on dwelling with God is the ground of composure even under siege. The deliberate ambiguity allows both: the “this” is the whole posture laid out in verses 1–4.

The structure displays *climactic parallelism*: the second colon doesn’t restate the first but increases the stakes (army → war) while deepening the response (not just freedom from fear but positive trust). The interiorization is important. Fearlessness is not numbness; it is the heart’s considered stance in the face of contingency.

The language also echoes the Deuteronomic charge to Israel’s warriors: “Do not let your hearts be faint, do not fear… for YHWH your God is the one who goes with you” (Deut 20:3–4). Later, Joshua is told repeatedly, “Be strong and courageous, do not be afraid” (חֲזַק וֶאֱמָץ, Josh 1:9). Psalm 27 will quote that idiom in its final verse, returning martial exhortation to the inner life.

**Verse 4**

אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהוָה, אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ: שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהוָה כָּל־יְמֵי חַיַּי; לַחֲזוֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְהוָה, וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ׃  

“One thing I have asked of YHWH, that will I seek: to dwell in the house of YHWH all the days of my life, to gaze upon the beauty of YHWH, and to inquire in His temple.”

As the commentary above suggested, this is the psalm’s hinge. The verbs move from martial imagery to the language of the temple and contemplation. שָׁאַלְתִּי (“I have asked”) is perfect; אֲבַקֵּשׁ (“I seek”) is ongoing. The psalmist has already made this request and continues to pursue it.

שִׁבְתִּי (from יָשַׁב, “sit/dwell”) is an infinitive with first-person suffix: “my dwelling.” As in Psalm 23:6—“I will dwell (וְשַׁבְתִּי) in the house of YHWH for length of days”—it implies more than occasional visits. Since laypeople could not literally live in the sanctuary, Radak reads this as desiring constant access and freedom from war so that body and mind may attend to “the needs of the soul.”

לַחֲזוֹת (from חָזָה) suggests more than looking. It is the verb for prophetic sight: “The vision of Isaiah” is חֲזוֹן יְשַׁעְיָהוּ (Isa 1:1), from the same root. To “gaze upon the נֹעַם of YHWH” is to behold both aesthetic and moral beauty—His gracious favor, as in “May the favor (נֹעַם) of the Lord our God be upon us” (Ps 90:17).

וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ adds a second mode of encounter. Rashi, hearing בֹּקֶר (“morning”), thinks of daily visits. But 2 Kings 16:15 shows לְבַקֵּר used for seeking oracular decisions: “The bronze altar shall be for me to inquire by” (לְבַקֵּר). The *heikhal* (palace/temple) here is not only a beautiful building but a throne room where judgments are issued.

In that reading, verse 4 is not escapist: the psalmist is not asking to be relieved from a dangerous world into some pure interiority. He wants to stand in the place where reality is interpreted—where God’s “face” is encountered and a true verdict on his contested life is heard. The enemies of verses 2–3 are real, but the “one thing” redefines what counts as safety: not their elimination, but the assurance that he is seen and known in the presence of YHWH.

**Verse 5**

כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְּסֻכּוֹ בְּיוֹם רָעָה; יַסְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵתֶר אׇהֳלוֹ; בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי׃  

“For He will hide me in His booth on the day of trouble; He will conceal me in the secrecy of His tent; on a rock He will raise me high.”

The three cola offer a miniature progression of images. First, “His sukkah” (סֻכּוֹ)—His booth or pavilion—on a “day of trouble” (בְּיוֹם רָעָה). The phrase “day of trouble” recurs in Psalms and prophets as a shorthand for crisis (cf. Ps 20:2; Jer 17:17). A סֻכָּה is by nature flimsy; Leviticus 23 makes its very fragility the point of Sukkot. Yet when it is “His,” it becomes an unassailable booth. In Ugaritic myth, the gods dwell in *sk* pavilions on the divine mountain. The psalmist collapses that mythic architecture into Zion’s temple.

Second, “the secrecy of His tent” (בְּסֵתֶר אׇהֳלוֹ) points to the inner protected space—סֵתֶר, “hiding place”—of the divine dwelling. Psalm 31:21 uses almost the same phrase: “You hide them in the secrecy of Your presence” (תִּצְפִּנֵם בְּסֵתֶר פָּנֶיךָ). Here, as there, concealment is a form of favor: to be taken behind the curtain.

Third, “on a rock He will raise me up” (בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי). The verb רוּם in the causative suggests purposeful elevation. Whereas the first two lines hide, the third exposes: the suppliant is placed on a high crag, visible and unassailable. The “rock” (צוּר) is one of the central metaphors for God Himself (e.g., “There is no rock like our God,” אין צוּר כֵּאלֹהֵינוּ, 1 Sam 2:2), so being set “on a rock” is another way of saying “planted in God.”

Jewish tradition reads this verse into Sukkot both literally and mystically. Saying “He will hide me in His sukkah” while sitting in a sukkah turns the festival hut into a theatrical prop for God’s own shelter, locating our vulnerability within His hospitality.

**Verse 6**

וְעַתָּה יָר֪וּם רֹאשִׁי עַל אֹיְבַי סְבִיבוֹתַי; וְאֶזְבְּחָה בְאׇהֳלוֹ זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה; אָשִׁירָה וַאֲזַמְּרָה לַי־הוָה׃  

“And now my head will be lifted up above my enemies all around; and I will sacrifice in His tent sacrifices with shouts of joy; I will sing and make music to YHWH.”

The interjection וְעַתָּה (“and now”) marks a shift from desire to enacted confidence. “My head will be lifted up” (יָר֪וּם רֹאשִׁי) echoes Joseph’s phrase to the cupbearer, “Pharaoh will lift up your head” (יִשָּׂא פַרְעֹה אֶת־רֹאשֶׁךָ, Gen 40:13), a courtroom idiom for restored status. Here, the lifting is “above my enemies all around” (עַל אֹיְבַי סְבִיבוֹתַי): a vertical hierarchy of honor replacing the earlier sense of being encircled and threatened.

The response is liturgical: “I will sacrifice in His tent sacrifices with *teru‘ah*” (זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה). תְרוּעָה is the shouted blast of trumpets or shofars, both in battle (Josh 6:5) and in worship (Num 10:10; Ps 47:2). BDB notes its dual sense: war cry and festal shout. The phrase here likely means thank-offerings accompanied by loud, jubilant cries—a blending of martial victory joy and temple celebration.

The verbs אָשִׁירָה (“I will sing”) and וַאֲזַמְּרָה (“I will make music”) are cohortatives, expressing resolve. Malbim reads this as profound: the psalmist does not wait for rescue in order to worship; his commitment to worship *is itself* the way he inhabits the promised victory. The Septuagint, by rendering “my head will be lifted” as “He lifted up my head” (ὕψωσεν), shortens that gap, turning hoped-for deliverance into remembered fact, perhaps reflecting a different Hebrew Vorlage or a liturgical instinct to narrate as already–accomplished what is ritually enacted.

The verse also foreshadows the shift to petition. The enemies have not disappeared; they are still “around” the psalmist. What has changed is his relation to them, mediated by the “tent” of YHWH. That tent—scene of both protection (v. 5) and sacrifice (v. 6)—will shortly become the arena where he pleads not to be turned away (v. 9).

**Verse 7**

שְׁמַע־יְהוָה קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא; וְחׇנֵּנִי וַעֲנֵנִי׃  

“Hear, YHWH, my voice when I call; be gracious to me and answer me.”

With verse 7 comes the tonal shift that has vexed commentators. The psalmist now addresses God directly in the imperative: “Hear!” (שְׁמַע). The confident third-person declarations (“YHWH is my light… He will hide me…”) give way to a threefold request: hear, show grace (חׇנֵּן), answer (עָנָה).

Importantly, what he asks God to hear is not the content of his argument but “my voice” (קוֹלִי) itself. The emphasis falls on the fact of relationship: that his cry reaches God. Psalm 102 begins similarly, “YHWH, hear my prayer, and let my cry come to You” (יְהוָה שִׁמְעָה תְּפִלָּתִי, וְשַׁוְעָתִי אֵלֶיךָ תָבוֹא, Ps 102:2), and then, like our psalm, pleads, “Do not hide Your face from me” (אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ, Ps 102:3). The liturgy uses both together on fast days, allowing the community to inhabit these overlapping voices of need.

חׇנֵּנִי (“be gracious to me”) appeals to divine ḥen, unmerited favor; וַעֲנֵנִי (“and answer me”) asks not only for audience but for response. Ibn Ezra notes that from this point on the psalm sounds like a corporate prayer, as if the king or individual speaks “for Israel” under siege. Whether private or communal, the move from creed to plea is significant. It suggests that trust is not the absence of petition but its precondition; one cries out precisely because one believes Someone hears.

Rather than treat this as a separate psalm, later interpreters—from Mowinckel to modern Jewish prayerbooks—hear verses 7–14 as the necessary counterpoint to verses 1–6: the inner fragility that must be named even as one sings “YHWH is my light.”

**Verse 8**

לְךָ אָמַר לִבִּי ‘בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי’; אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ יְהוָה אֲבַקֵּשׁ׃  

“On Your behalf my heart has said, ‘Seek My face’; Your face, YHWH, I seek.”

This dense verse stages an interior dialogue. לְךָ (“to You/for You”) could mean “concerning You” or “on Your behalf.” Rashi famously parses: “On Your behalf, as Your emissary, my heart says, ‘Seek My face’; and I answer, ‘Your face, YHWH, I seek.’” The heart becomes the site where divine command and human resolve meet.

The theme, “seeking the face” (בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי), is everywhere in the Psalter and liturgy. Psalm 105:4 exhorts, “Seek YHWH and His strength; seek His face continually” (דִּרְשׁוּ יְהוָה וְעֻזּוֹ; בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָיו תָּמִיד). In royal and cultic contexts across the ANE, to “see the face” of a king or god meant gaining audience—*amāru pān šarri* in Akkadian, “to see the king’s face,” is court language. Israel transfers this to its imageless God: seeking YHWH’s face is seeking a living encounter and a verdict.

The chiastic structure—“Seek My face” // “Your face I seek”—binds God’s initiative and human response. Radak suggests the psalmist is recounting a prophetic oracle that came “into his heart,” a call to worship that he obeys. Meiri, intriguingly, reads it politically: when David “seeks” that the people seek his own face, he claims, his ultimate intent is to direct them beyond himself to God’s face—to obedience to Torah.

In any case, this verse sets up the agony of verse 9. If the deepest summons is to seek God’s face, what happens when that face is hidden? Theologians across centuries have read here the paradox of spiritual life: the same God who invites intimacy may also seem to withdraw. Psalm 27 holds both without resolving the tension, moving from divine imperative to human petition.

**Verse 9**

אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי; אַל־תַּט בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ. עֶזְרָתִי הָיִיתָ; אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי וְאַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי, אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי׃  

“Do not hide Your face from me; do not thrust aside Your servant in anger. You have been my help; do not cast me off and do not forsake me, O God of my salvation.”

The plea not to have God “hide His face” (אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ) is loaded with covenantal resonance. It is the reversal of the priestly blessing, “May YHWH make His face shine upon you” (יָאֵר יְהוָה פָּנָיו אֵלֶיךָ, Num 6:25). To have that face turned away is to lose not only comfort but orientation. Psalm 13:2’s cry, “How long will You hide Your face from me?” hovers in the background.

The second clause, אַל־תַּט בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ, is literally “do not stretch/tilt [Yourself] in anger toward Your servant.” The imagery is of a king tipping someone out of his presence. The psalmist counters by reminding God, “You have been my help” (עֶזְרָתִי הָיִיתָ). Past faithfulness becomes the basis for present plea: having once taken me in, do not now “cast me off” (אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי) or “forsake me” (אַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי). נָטַשׁ and עָזַב together cover both abrupt rejection and slow abandonment.

The closing epithet, אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי (“God of my salvation”), recalls verse 1’s “my salvation” (יִשְׁעִי) and creates an inclusio. The God who is source of rescue in the creed is the same God whose absence is feared in prayer. That pairing undercuts any simplistic theology in which trust and lament are sequential stages; here they are simultaneous.

Psalm 102’s nearly identical petition—“Do not hide Your face from me on the day of my distress” (אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי בְּיוֹם צַר־לִי)—is used in fast-day liturgy alongside Psalm 27, signaling that Israel has long read such cries as paradigmatic language for communal as well as individual crisis.

**Verse 10**

כִּי־אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי; וַיהוָה יַאַסְפֵנִי׃  

“For my father and my mother have forsaken me, but YHWH will gather me in.”

This compact verse interrupts the fourfold “do not” with a declaration of unexpected confidence. The parental pair אָבִי וְאִמִּי (“my father and my mother”) evokes the most basic human bond. Whether the abandonment is literal—an orphan’s reality—or hypothetical hyperbole (“even if my father and mother abandoned me”), the statement imagines total human support gone.

The second colon introduces YHWH as alternative kin: “YHWH will gather me” (יַאַסְפֵנִי). The verb אָסַף is used of God gathering exiles (Deut 30:3), of people being “gathered to their people” in death, and of a shepherd collecting his flock (Zech 10:8: “I will whistle for them and gather them, for I have redeemed them”). Here, as Malbim notes, it suggests something like adoption: God takes the abandoned into His household.

The tenses are telling. “Have forsaken me” (עֲזָבוּנִי) is perfect, either fact or imaginative completed act; “will gather me” (יַאַסְפֵנִי) is imperfect, a future stretching forward. All human relationships, even the best, are marked by mortality and failure; God’s relationship is projected as steadfast into the unknown.

Later Jewish and Christian writers have drawn deep comfort from this line. Rabbi Sacks used it to articulate a theology of God’s unwavering faith in us: even when we—or those closest to us—give up, God does not. The verse speaks with particular poignancy in contexts of trauma and alienation, where familial structures have indeed failed. It also sharpens the psalm’s central conviction: that identity and security are ultimately grounded not in social placement but in divine reception.

**Verse 11**

הוֹרֵנִי יְהוָה דַּרְכֶּךָ; וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי׃  

“Teach me, YHWH, Your way; and lead me in a level path, because of my watchful foes.”

The focus shifts from God’s presence to God’s instruction. הוֹרֵנִי (“teach me”) comes from יָרָה, the root behind תּוֹרָה (“instruction”), and דַּרְכֶּךָ (“Your way”) in wisdom literature means God’s characteristic mode of acting and the ethical path aligned with it. Psalm 86:11 uses almost the same words: “Teach me, YHWH, Your way; I will walk in Your truth” (הוֹרֵנִי יְהוָה דַרְכֶּךָ).

The second colon asks for being led (נְחֵנִי) “in a level path” (בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר). מִישׁוֹר denotes flatness—both physical smoothness and moral uprightness. Psalm 143:10 has, “Let Your good spirit lead me on level ground” (בְּאֶרֶץ מִישׁוֹר). Here, the level path is requested “because of my שׁוֹרְרָי”—a rare word derived from a root meaning “watch, lie in wait.” Klein glosses שׁוֹרֵר as “insidious watcher, enemy.” The psalmist imagines opponents scrutinizing his steps, waiting for him to stumble.

Ethical rectitude thus becomes practical protection. If he veers from the level path, enemies will interpret it as evidence that “God has abandoned him,” as Radak suggests, or will pounce legally or politically. Guidance is needed not in a neutral terrain but on a contested stage. This verse therefore links the wisdom motif (“Your way”) to the earlier legal and martial context: to be taught by YHWH is not a private luxury but a survival skill under surveillance.

**Verse 12**

אַל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי; כִּי קָמוּ־בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, וִיפֵחַ חָמָס׃  

“Do not give me over to the *nephesh* of my adversaries, for false witnesses have risen up against me, and one who breathes out violence.”

נֶפֶשׁ is here best taken as “desire, appetite,” or concretely, “throat.” To be delivered into the נֶפֶשׁ of one’s enemies is to be fed to their voracious will—an echo of “devouring my flesh” in verse 2. The syntax again frames the main threat as judicial: “false witnesses” (עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר) rising “against me” (בִּי). Deuteronomy 19:16–19 condemns such עֵד שֶׁקֶר (false witness) and prescribes that what they planned for their brother be done to them instead. Here, the psalmist feels that machinery turned against him.

The closing וִיפֵחַ חָמָס is difficult. The Masoretic vocalization suggests a participle from a root meaning “to blow, to puff”: “one who breathes out violence.” Proverbs uses a similar collocation: “a false witness who breathes out lies” (יָפִיחַ כְּזָבִים, Prov 6:19). But Ugaritic texts contain a noun *pḥ* meaning “witness,” and BDB notes that many scholars read our phrase as “a witness of violence,” parallel to “witnesses of falsehood.”

Either way, violence (חָמָס)—a key term for systemic injustice and cruelty—has invaded the courtroom. The enemies now are not marauders but litigants; their weapon is the tongue. The psalmist’s plea “Do not give me over” thus asks for God’s intervention not only in military but in legal space, anticipating later “plead my cause” psalms (e.g., Ps 35:1: רִיבָה יְהוָה אֶת־יְרִיבַי, “Contend, YHWH, with those who contend with me”).

**Verse 13**

לוּלֵא הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת בְּטוּב־יְהוָה בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים׃  

“Had I not believed to see the goodness of YHWH in the land of the living…”

As we saw in the introduction, this verse is grammatically incomplete. לוּלֵא (“if not, unless”) normally introduces a condition whose consequence follows (“If YHWH had not been for us…” in Ps 124:1–2). Here, no apodosis comes. Ancient translators were uneasy. The Septuagint omits לוּלֵא and reads simply, “I believe that I shall see…” (πιστεύω τοῦ ἰδεῖν). The Masoretes, instead of excising, dotted every letter of לוּלֵא to signal uncertainty but preserve the reading.

Rabbinic tradition turned this into spiritual autobiography. The Talmud (Berakhot 4a) imagines David saying: “I know You pay good reward to the righteous in the world to come, but I do not know whether I have a share among them.” The broken syntax enacts that anxiety. “If I had not believed… [that I would see God’s goodness],” what would be left is too bleak to articulate.

The phrase “the goodness of YHWH” (בְּטוּב־יְהוָה) recalls the “beauty of YHWH” (בְּנֹעַם־יְהוָה) in verse 4; to “see” it “in the land of the living” (בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים) ties the psalm back to history, not heaven. The same expression appears in Psalm 116:9, “I shall walk before YHWH in the lands of the living” (בְּאַרְצוֹת הַחַיִּים), and in Ezekiel’s oracles where nations that terrorized “the land of the living” are consigned to Sheol (Ezek 32). It denotes this life as the arena where God’s favor and justice are (or ought to be) visible.

Thus the psalm refuses both despair and escapism. The speaker’s faith is not in some ethereal afterlife alone but in seeing—here, in the land of the living—manifestations of YHWH’s goodness. That this is couched in an unfinished conditional underscores how precarious that faith can feel.

**Verse 14**

קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה; חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ; וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה׃  

“Wait for YHWH; be strong, and let your heart take courage; and wait for YHWH.”

The psalm closes with a threefold exhortation. קַוֵּה (“wait, hope”) carries, as Klein notes, the sense of being stretched, like a cord (קַו). It is not idle delay but tension toward a future. The doubling—“wait… and wait”—forms a kind of inclusio with the doubled divine name in verse 1 and with the repeated trust theme throughout.

Between the two קַוֵּה’s stands the command: “Be strong and let your heart take courage” (חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ). These verbs recall God’s and Moses’ words to Joshua on the cusp of entering Canaan: “Be strong and courageous… do not be frightened or dismayed, for YHWH your God is with you” (Josh 1:9). Applied here to the “heart,” they reframe battlefield valor as interior resilience. The enemies, false witnesses, and hidden face have not been removed; what is needed is steadfastness in the waiting.

Who is speaking? Grammatically, a singular “you” is addressed. Many read it as the psalmist speaking to himself, ending as he began—with a kind of self-preaching. Others hear a liturgical voice—priest, choir, community—answering the individual’s lament with communal encouragement. In practice, the synagogue turns it into collective counsel: as the penitential season begins and ends, congregants say to one another, “קַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה… וְקַוֵּה אֶל־יְהוָה.”

Structurally, verse 14 answers the unsaid half of verse 13. If verse 13 trails off at “Had I not believed…,” verse 14 supplies the path of that belief: to keep waiting, to cultivate courage, to return again to hope. The psalm has not resolved the problem of evil or the fear of abandonment. Instead, it has built an “architecture of waiting” in which confidence and petition, light and hiddenness, coexist without cancelling one another. It ends not with “and they all lived happily ever after” but with an imperative: Stay in the tension. Wait for YHWH.
