### INTRODUCTION ESSAY

Every year on Rosh Hashanah, at the climax of the Musaf service, the synagogue liturgy makes its case for blowing the ram's horn by quoting two verses from Psalm 81. Right after the cantor chants אַתָּה נִגְלֵיתָ בַּעֲנַן כְּבוֹדֶךָ עַל עַם קָדְשְׁךָ, לְדַבֵּר עִמָּם, מִן הַשָּמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּם קוֹלֶךָ, "You were revealed in Your cloud of glory upon Your holy people to speak with them; from heaven You made them hear Your voice," the *Shofarot* blessing cites verses 4 and 5 of our psalm: תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר, בַּכֵּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ. כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא, מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "Blow the horn on the new moon, on the appointed time for our feast day. For it is a law for Israel, a ruling of the God of Jacob." And there the prayer book stops quoting. It cuts off three words before the middle of verse 6, where the psalm springs its trap. For in the psalm itself, no sooner has the congregation blown its horn and struck its timbrels than a voice breaks in from the thunder to silence the musicians and tell them that God has had enough of their noise. Psalm 81 is a festival liturgy interrupted by the God it is celebrating. It takes a crowd that has gathered to fill the sanctuary with song for "our feast day," strips away their collective voice, and teaches them that what covenant worship asks of a human mouth is not to emit sound, but to listen and be fed.

The architecture of the poem enacts this interruption. For four and a half verses (vv. 2–6b), a liturgical leader marshals the assembly with five plural commands—sing, shout, lift up song, strike the drum, blow the horn—and grounds the celebration in the memory of the Exodus. Then, in the final three words of verse 6, an unnamed "I" cuts across the music: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "A language I did not know I hear." From verse 7 to the end of the psalm, God speaks alone. The divine monologue moves from a tender, physical memory of lifting Egyptian brick-baskets off Israel's shoulders and answering them in Sinai's thunder (vv. 7–8), to an urgent plea built out of the Ten Commandments (vv. 9–11), to the flat grief of Israel's refusal and God's decision to let them go (vv. 12–13), and finally to an aching counterfactual daydream—"If only my people were listening to me!"—that ends by holding out the finest wheat and honey from the rock to a silent listener (vv. 14–17). Once God begins to speak in verse 7, the congregation never utters another word.

### From Making Noise to Opening the Mouth

The opening verses sound, at first, like any other temple hymn, and that is how they lull the hearer. The choir leader piles up instruments—תֹף, "timbrel," כִּנּוֹר נָעִים, "melodious lyre," נָבֶל, "harp," שׁוֹפָר, "ram's horn"—and binds the worshipers together with the first-person plural suffix **־נוּ** (*-nū*, "our"): God is לֵאלֹהִים עוּזֵּ**נוּ**, "God, our strength" (v. 2), and the autumn pilgrimage is לְיוֹם חַגֵּ**נוּ**, "for our feast day" (v. 4). The vocabulary deliberately echoes the Song of the Sea in Exodus 15, where Israel sang עָזִּי וְזִמְרָת יָהּ, "My strength and song is Yah" (Exod 15:2), and Miriam took her תֹּף, "timbrel" (Exod 15:20); our psalm's עוּזֵּנוּ, "our strength" (v. 2), and שְׂאוּ־זִמְרָה וּתְנוּ־תֹף, "Take up the song, sound the timbrel" (v. 3), recreate that victory party in the temple courtyard. Notice, too, how the human speakers name God. They call Him אֱלֹהִים, "God," and אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "the God of Jacob" (vv. 2, 5), the phrase that frames the entire opening hymn, but they never pronounce His personal name, יְהֹוָה. It is "our" feast, and God is our patron.

Yet the congregation's own ritual summons what it cannot control. In verse 2 they are told הָרִיעוּ (`hā-RIY-ʿū`), "raise a shout," and in verse 4 they are told תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר, "Blow the horn on the new moon." The shofar is not merely a festival instrument; it is the acoustic trigger of Mount Sinai, where קוֹל הַשֹּׁפָר הוֹלֵךְ וְחָזֵק מְאֹד, מֹשֶׁה יְדַבֵּר וְהָאֱלֹהִים יַעֲנֶנּוּ בְקוֹל, "the sound of the horn grew louder and louder; Moses would speak, and God would answer him in thunder" (Exod 19:19). When the worshipers reenact Sinai with their horns and their shouts (`hā-RIY-ʿū`), Sinai answers back—and in verse 8, God will remind them of His רַעַם (`RA-ʿam`), "thunder," whose consonants echo their shout.

The hinge snaps in verse 6c: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "A language I did not know I hear." Ancient and medieval readers were so startled by this sudden "I" that many tried to smooth it away. The Septuagint translates γλῶσσαν ἣν οὐκ ἔγνω ἤκουσεν (*glōssan hēn ouk egnō ēkousen*), "a tongue which he knew not he heard," turning the line into a third-person remark about Joseph hearing Egyptian in Pharaoh's court (a reading Rashi and the Talmud in *b. Sotah* 36b develop into the legend of the angel Gabriel teaching Joseph seventy languages overnight). But the Hebrew verb אֶשְׁמָע is first-person imperfect—"I hear," right now—and it marks the moment the Levitical prophet in the temple is overtaken by a voice that is not the choir's.

The instant that voice takes over, the grammar of possession flips. After חַגֵּנוּ, "our feast day," in verse 4, the human "we" and "our" disappear from the psalm. God never says "our," and He never calls Himself "the God of Jacob." Instead, He claims the personal name יְהֹוָה (vv. 11, 16) and addresses the crowd three times with the paired title עַמִּי, "my people," and יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Israel," each time ending the line on the syllable **לִי** (*lī*, "to me"):

> שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, יִשְׂרָאֵל אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־**לִי**. (v. 9)
> וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי, וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה **לִי**. (v. 12)
> לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ **לִי**, יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ. (v. 14)
>
> "Hear, My people, and I will admonish you; Israel, if you would but listen **to Me**!"
> "But My people would not listen to My voice, Israel would not obey **Me**."
> "If only My people would listen **to Me**, if Israel would follow My paths!"

Even the causative verbs (the Hebrew *Hiphil* stem, which expresses making something happen) change hands. In verse 2, the human leader gave Israel two causative commands to make noise: הַרְנִינוּ, "make a ringing cry," and הָרִיעוּ, "raise a shout." Once the oracle begins, every causative action belongs to God—הֲסִירוֹתִי, "I removed" the burden (v. 7); וְאָעִידָה, "I will admonish" (v. 9); הַמַּעַלְךָ, "who brought you up" (v. 11); אַכְנִיעַ, "I would subdue," and אָשִׁיב יָדִי, "I would turn My hand" (v. 15); וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ, "He would feed him," and אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "I would sate you" (v. 17)—with a single exception. God gives Israel one *Hiphil* command of His own, beginning with the same *har-* sound as their opening הַרְנִינוּ (`har-NIY-nū`) and הָרִיעוּ (`hā-RIY-ʿū`): הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ (`har-ḥev-PIY-khā wa-'a-mal-'Ē-hū`), "Open your mouth wide and I will fill it" (v. 11).

Look at where that command sits. Verses 10 and 11 quote the opening of the Ten Commandments, but backwards. At Sinai (Exod 20:2–3), God first announces His liberating act—"I am YHWH your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt"—and then issues the prohibition: "You shall have no other gods before Me." Psalm 81 reverses the sequence. First comes the prohibition in verse 10: לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ אֵל זָר, וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְאֵל נֵכָר, "You shall have no foreign god in you, you shall not bow to an alien god." Then, in verse 11, comes the self-introduction: אָנֹכִי יְהֹוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "I the LORD am your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt; open your mouth wide and I will fill it." By inverting the Decalogue, the poet makes God's liberation from Egypt lead not into a restriction, but into a feeding. Prohibition is only clearing the mouth of stones so that food can go in. God remembers the slave's body in verse 7—הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ, כַּפָּיו מִדּוּד תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "I relieved his shoulder of the burden, his hands were freed from the basket," evoking the heavy clay-carrying baskets of Egyptian corvée labor—and asks for a different posture of the body now: not shoulders bent under Pharaoh's bricks, and not merely lipsnoisy with temple songs, but the gaping beak of a fledgling bird waiting to be fed.

The seventeenth-century English poet George Herbert staged this same theological discovery at the close of *Love (III)*, where the anxious sinner, convinced he must earn his keep, is disarmed by his host:

> "Truth, Lord, but I have marr'd them; let my shame
> Go where it doth deserve."
> "And know you not," says Love, "who bore the blame?"
> "My dear, then I will serve."
> "You must sit down," says Love, "and taste my meat."
> So I did sit and eat.

In Herbert's poem, the guest surrenders to grace and eats. The tragedy of Psalm 81 is that God sets the same table—"Open your mouth wide and I will fill it"—and the guest refuses to sit down.

### The God Who Cannot Keep His Distance

Why would a liberated people refuse an open-mouth feast? The psalm tracks the failure through five occurrences of the root שׁמע, "hear" or "listen," and their grammar tells the story. The only person in the entire psalm who actually hears in the indicative mood is the prophet at the hinge of verse 6c: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "A language I did not know I hear." When Israel cried out in Egypt, God heard and answered at once: בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ וָאֲחַלְּצֶךָּ, אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "In distress you called and I rescued you; I answered you from the secret place of thunder" (v. 8). Yet when God speaks in Israel's own tongue, the verb שׁמע slides steadily away from reality. In verse 9, it is an imperative followed by a sentence that breaks off mid-thought: שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, יִשְׂרָאֵל אִם־תִּֽשְׁמַֽע־לִֽי, "Hear, My people, and I will admonish you; Israel, if you would but listen to Me—" In the Masoretic text, each of the last two words carries a *meteg* (a secondary stress mark that slows the reader's voice down: `’im-tish-maʿ-LIY`), leaving the "if" hanging in the air without a "then." By verse 12, the conditional has collapsed into flat past-tense negation: וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי, וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה לִי, "But My people would not listen to My voice, Israel would not obey Me."

Notice where the psalm locates the rival to God's voice. Where the Decalogue says לֹא יִהְיֶה לְךָ, "You shall not have *for* you" other gods (Exod 20:3), verse 10 says לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ אֵל זָר, "There shall not be *in* you a strange god." In the Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Nedarim* 9:1), Rabbi Yannai pounced on that preposition: כָּל הַשּׁוֹמֵעַ לְיִצְרוֹ כְּאִילּוּ עוֹבֵד עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה. מַאי טַעֲמָא? לֹא יִהְיֶה בְךְ אֵל זָר וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְאֵל נֵכָר. זָר שֶׁבְּקִרְבָּךְ אַל תַּמְלִיכֵהוּ עָלֶיךָ, "Whoever listens to his impulse is as though he worshiped idols. What is the reason? 'In yourself there shall be no strange god, and you shall not bow to an alien god'—the stranger within you, do not make him king over you." That is not merely homiletical wit; it is what verse 13 confirms when it names the culprit inside them: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם, יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם, "So I let them go after their willful heart, that they might follow their own devices." The foreign god *in* them (בְךָ) turns out to be the stubbornness of their own heart (לִבָּם), which prefers generating its own counsels to opening its mouth in dependence.

God's punishment in verse 13 is contained in a single devastating verb: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ, "So I sent him away" or "I let them go." The verb שׁלח in the intensive *Piel* stem is the great watchword of the Exodus—שַׁלַּח אֶת־עַמִּי, "Let My people go" (Exod 5:1)—and it is also the legal term for handing a spouse a bill of divorce (Deut 24:1; Jer 3:8). Here God turns the verb of liberation into the verb of abandonment. Radak states the chill of the line plainly: סלקתי שמירתי מעליהם ושמתים הפקר, "I removed My protection from upon them and left them ownerless." Divine wrath here is not a thunderbolt; it is God stepping back and letting human beings have their own way.

In the twentieth century, the psychologist Rudolf Dreikurs built a famous theory of parenting around this exact move, advising parents to stop nagging a defiant child and instead allow "natural consequences" to do the teaching—provided, Dreikurs insisted, that the parent remains calm and emotionally detached while the child learns the lesson. In Jeremiah 11:7–8, which shares our psalm's rare spelling בִּשְׁרִירוּת, "in the stubbornness of" (found with a *yod* nowhere else in the Bible), God recites the same history—הָעֵד הַעִדֹתִי בַּאֲבוֹתֵיכֶם בְּיוֹם הַעֲלוֹתִי אוֹתָם מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרַיִם... שִׁמְעוּ בְּקוֹלִי. וְלֹא שָׁמְעוּ... וַיֵּלְכוּ אִישׁ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם הָרָע, "I solemnly warned your fathers on the day I brought them up from the land of Egypt... 'Listen to My voice.' Yet they did not listen... and walked each in the stubbornness of their evil heart"—and concludes with judicial finality: וָאָבִיא עֲלֵיהֶם אֶת־כׇּל־דִּבְרֵי הַבְּרִית־הַזֹּאת, "Therefore I brought upon them all the words of this covenant."

In Psalm 81, God tries to walk away in verse 13—and cannot keep it up for a single verse. In verse 14, the judge's composure breaks: לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי, יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ, "If only My people would listen to Me, if Israel would follow My paths!" The particle לוּ (*lū*, "would that," "if only") introduces an unfulfilled wish, what the Talmud in *b. Avodah Zarah* 5a calls לְשׁוֹן תַּחֲנוּנִים, "the language of supplication," when spoken by God. Yet instead of following לוּ with a past-tense verb ("If only my people *had* listened"), the poet writes the present participle שֹׁמֵעַ, "listening." As Rashi immediately hears, the participle keeps the door ajar in the present: עֲדַיִן אִם הָיוּ רוֹצִין לָשׁוּב לִי וְלִשְׁמֹעַ אֵלַי, "Still, even now, if they desired to return to Me and to hearken to Me!" Where Israel trudged away in the simple *Qal* stem in verse 13 (יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם, "they walk in their own devices"), God's voice catches on what their stride would look like in the intensive *Piel* stem: בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ, "in My paths they would walk."

### Honey After Rebellion, and the Unanswered "You"

What follows in verses 15–17 is God's wistful inventory of the blessings He is holding in His hands with no one to give them to. כִּמְעַט אוֹיְבֵיהֶם אַכְנִיעַ, וְעַל צָרֵיהֶם אָשִׁיב יָדִי, "Then would I subdue their enemies at once, strike their foes again and again" (v. 15). The human hands (כַּפָּיו) that God freed from the brick-basket in verse 7 would not even need to fight; God's own hand (יָדִי) would do the striking, turning the distress (בַּצָּרָה, v. 8) back upon their distressers (צָרֵיהֶם, v. 15). In verse 16, מְשַׂנְאֵי יְהֹוָה יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ, "Those who hate the LORD would cower before Him"—the verb יְכַחֲשׁוּ meaning to feign obedience out of terror, as Ibn Ezra notes (מרוב פחדם יכחשו שלא מרדו בך, "out of their great fear they would lie and say they never rebelled against You"). The irony stings: foreign enemies would at least offer YHWH lip-service, while His own people would not even pretend to listen, and the prepositional phrase לוֹ (`lō`, "to Him") echoes the sound of God's sigh לוּ (`lū`, "if only") two verses above.

Then comes the final verse (v. 17): וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה, וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "He fed them the finest wheat; I sated you with honey from the rock." Every phrase here is drawn from Moses' farewell poem in Deuteronomy 32, with which Psalm 81 is in quiet conversation from start to finish. Indeed, the psalm's very first word of praise, הַרְנִינוּ, "Sing joyously!" (v. 2), occurs elsewhere in the entire Hebrew Bible only once—at the end of the Song of Moses, הַרְנִינוּ גוֹיִם עַמּוֹ, "Sing joyously, O nations, of His people!" (Deut 32:43). And in Deuteronomy 32:13–14, Moses describes how God cared for Israel in the wilderness: וַיֵּנִקֵהוּ דְבַשׁ מִסֶּלַע וְשֶׁמֶן מֵחַלְמִישׁ צוּר... עִם־חֵלֶב כִּלְיוֹת חִטָּה וְדַם־עֵנָב תִּשְׁתֶּה־חָמֶר, "He suckled him with honey from the crag, and oil from the flinty rock... with the kidney-fat of wheat, and the blood of the grape you drank as foaming wine."

But compare the order of events in the two poems. In Deuteronomy (both in 31:20 and in 32:13–15), the sequence is tragic: first God feeds Israel with wheat and honey from the rock; then וַיִּשְׁמַן יְשֻׁרוּן וַיִּבְעָט, "Jeshurun grew fat and kicked" (Deut 32:15), turning to foreign gods (אֵל נֵכָר, Deut 32:12); and finally God pronounces judgment. In Deuteronomy, satiety comes *before* rebellion and causes it. Psalm 81 runs the entire sequence in reverse. Here the foreign god (אֵל נֵכָר, v. 10), the refusal to listen (v. 12), and the stubborn heart (v. 13) have *already* happened—and only after the rebellion does the poem arrive at the finest wheat and honey from the rock, stopping mid-verse in Deuteronomy 32:14 right before the heady wine and right before Jeshurun kicks. Even the grim test at the waters of Meribah in verse 8 (אֶבְחׇנְךָ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה סֶלָה, "I tested you at the waters of Meribah. Selah"—where the psalmist paused the music with the poem's only *Selah* over the memory of the rock struck in strife) is transformed in verse 17: the צוּר, "rock," of wilderness testing becomes a rock dripping with דְּבַשׁ, "honey." What Deuteronomy remembered as the fullness that spoiled Israel, Psalm 81 holds out as the grace still waiting after Israel has failed.

And that explains the sudden grammatical swerve on the last word of the poem. Since verse 12, God has been speaking *about* Israel in the third person, as someone who has walked out of the room: "My people would not listen... I let *him* go in the stubbornness of *their* heart, let *them* walk in *their* counsels... I would subdue *their* enemies... and He would feed *him* (וַיַּאֲכִילֵ**הוּ**) from the finest wheat." Both the Septuagint and the Roman Catholic Introit for Corpus Christi (*Cibavit eos ex adipe frumenti... et de petra, melle saturavit eos*) keep the whole of verse 17 in the third-person past tense: "He fed them... and sated them." Modern editors regularly propose emending the Masoretic text's third-person וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ, "and He fed him," or the final first-to-second-person אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "I would sate you," so that the two halves of the verse match. To "correct" that mismatch is to ruin the poem's ending. In the first half of verse 17, God is still speaking wistfully about Israel in the third person; then, in the final three words—וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶ**ךָ**, "and from the rock with honey I would sate **you**"—He turns His head and looks straight at the worshiper standing in the courtyard.

The loud plural "we" who began the psalm shouting about "our strength" and "our feast day" has been whittled down to a single, silent "you" (**־ךָ**). The psalm records no congregational response, no closing hymn, no promise to do better. It cannot, because the entire poem has argued that Israel's words and songs are too easily used to drown God out. The offer of verse 11—הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "Open your mouth wide and I will fill it"—and the rock-honey of verse 17 are left hanging in the air of the festival, where the only way to accept them is to stop singing and listen.
