**Verse 1**

לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־הַגִּתִּית, לְאָסָף.

> For the leader; upon the gittith, of Asaph.

The superscription pairs a mysterious musical term with a prophetic guild, and both clues pull the poem in opposite directions. Rashi explains עַל־הַגִּתִּית, "upon the gittith," as an instrument imported from the Philistine city of Gath—כְּלִי שִׁיר הַבָּא מִגַּת, "a musical instrument that comes from Gath"—which the Targum (the Aramaic translation/paraphrase) identifies more specifically as a lyre from Gath. The Septuagint, however, reads the noun גַּת, "winepress," and translates ὑπὲρ τῶν ληνῶν (*hyper tōn lēnōn*), "for the winepresses." Only three psalms carry this heading (Pss 8, 81, 84), and the vintage reading fits an autumn harvest festival at the end of the agricultural year, when the grapes have been trodden and the wheat of verse 17 has been gathered in. Yet a press in biblical poetry is also an image of judgment (Isa 63:2–3), an ambiguity Augustine exploited in his *Expositions of the Psalms* (Psalm 80 [81]) by reading the presses as the mystery of the Church, where through earthly trials the oil strains secretly into the vat while the lees run openly down the streets.

The attribution לְאָסָף, "of Asaph," signals what will happen in verse 6c. In Chronicles, the sons of Asaph are not merely temple choristers; they are Levites הַנִּבְּאִים בְּכִנֹּרוֹת, "who prophesied with lyres" (1 Chr 25:1–3). Across the Asaph collection (especially Psalms 50, 78, 81, and 82), a liturgical summons regularly gives way to a courtroom oracle in which God indicts His own worshipers. Even the opening word לַמְנַצֵּחַ, "For the leader," is set off in the Masoretic text (the standard medieval Hebrew text) by a vertical dividing line (*paseq*) that forces a brief pause before עַל־הַגִּתִּית, "upon the gittith," as though the heading itself were holding its breath before the music starts.

**Verses 2–3**

הַרְנִינוּ לֵאלֹקִים עוּזֵּנוּ; הָרִיעוּ לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב.

> Sing joyously to God, our strength; raise a shout for the God of Jacob.

שְׂאוּ־זִמְרָה וּתְנוּ־תֹף; כִּנּוֹר נָעִים עִם־נָבֶל.

> Lift up the melody and strike the timbrel, the sweet lyre with the harp.

As the introduction noted, the opening commands pile up human sound under the banner of עוּזֵּנוּ, "our strength." What makes this phrasing so evocative is that it lifts the exact vocabulary of Israel's first song of freedom on the shore of the Red Sea. In Exodus 15:2, Moses and the Israelites sang עָזִּי וְזִמְרָת יָ‑הּ, "My strength and melody is Yah," and in Exodus 15:20 Miriam led the women out with תֻּפִּים, "timbrels." By combining עוּזֵּנוּ, "our strength" (v. 2), with זִמְרָה, "melody," and תֹף, "timbrel" (v. 3), the choir leader invites the temple crowd to reenact the Exodus victory dance—unaware that in verses 7–8 God will step into the middle of their Red Sea party to recall their liberation from forced labor and the wilderness rebellion at Meribah that followed it.

The grammar of verse 3 hurries the instruments onto the stage. The verbs שְׂאוּ־זִמְרָה, "Lift up the melody," and וּתְנוּ־תֹף, "give [that is, strike] the timbrel," are unusual idioms; normally one "lifts up" a voice (קוֹל) and "takes" a timbrel. Then the second half of the verse—כִּנּוֹר נָעִים עִם־נָבֶל, "the sweet lyre with the harp"—has no verb at all. As Ibn Ezra points out (ומלת תנו תשרת בעבור אחרת, "and the word 'give' serves for another phrase as well"), the single verb תְּנוּ, "give," has to stretch across the pause to govern the strings. Rashi, drawing on a wordplay attributed to Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba in the Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Sukkah* 5:6), plays on the name of the last instrument, נָבֶל, "harp: לָמָּה נִקְרָא שְׁמוֹ נֵבֶל? שֶׁהוּא מְנַבֵּל כָּל מִינֵי זֶמֶר, Why is its name called *nevel*? Because it puts to shame (*menabbel*) all other kinds of music." Notice the progression of sound from verse 2 to verse 4: first the unaccompanied human throat (הַרְנִינוּ, הָרִיעוּ), then skin percussion (תֹף), then plucked strings (כִּנּוֹר, נָבֶל), and finally, in verse 4, the piercing blast of the ram's horn.

Later readers heard this noisy orchestra in sharply divided ways. For William Byrd in Elizabethan England, verses 2–5 (1–4 in English numbering) inspired *Sing joyfully unto God our strength*, a six-voice polyphonic anthem in which overlapping vocal entries mimic the fanfare of trumpets and strings; for Francis Poulenc, writing his motet *Exultate Deo* at his country house at Noizay in May 1941, the same verses became an act of defiant liturgical buoyancy. John Calvin, by contrast, used verses 2–4 in his 1557 *Commentary on the Psalms* to banish musical instruments from Christian worship altogether, arguing that timbrels, harps, and horns belonged to the "childish instruction" (*paedagogia puerilis*) of an immature people under the law, whereas the Gospel asks only for the unassisted human voice. Calvin was fighting a sixteenth-century Reformation battle, yet he sensed the very tension our psalm is setting up: the louder the instruments grow in verses 2–4, the sharper the silence when an unfamiliar voice interrupts them in verse 6 and God goes on to lament that no one is listening (vv. 9, 12).

Even the rabbinic tradition quietly inverted the triumphalism of verse 2's הָרִיעוּ, "raise a shout." That verb comes from the same root as תְּרוּעָה (*teru'ah*), the broken blast of the Rosh Hashanah shofar. In the Talmud (*b. Rosh Hashanah* 33b), the sages derive the sound of the *teru'ah* from the Aramaic word יְבָבָא (*yevava*), "weeping," linking it to the mother of the Canaanite general Sisera crying at her window when her son's chariot never comes home (Judg 5:28). Because the rabbis could not decide whether a person weeping groans in short heaves or sobs in rapid whimpers, Rabbi Abbahu in Caesarea enacted that the synagogue shofar must sound both patterns together. In actual Jewish practice, the exuberant festival shout of verse 2 (הָרִיעוּ) is performed as the sound of a mother's tears—anticipating the turn the psalm itself will take when God begins to weep over His children in verse 14.

**Verse 4**

תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר; בַּכֵּסֶה, לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ.

> Blow the horn on the new moon, on the full moon for our feast day.

Everything in this verse turns on the rare word בַּכֵּסֶה (`bak-KĒ-seh`), which occurs only here with a final *heh* and once in Proverbs 7:20 with an *aleph* (לְיוֹם הַכֵּסֶא יָבֹא בֵיתוֹ, "on the day of the *kese'* he will come home"). How a reader translates that single word determines which festival the psalm is describing. In the Talmud (*b. Rosh Hashanah* 8b; *b. Beitzah* 16a), Rabbi Abbahu connects בַּכֵּסֶה to the Hebrew root כסה, "to cover" or "conceal: אֵיזֶהוּ חַג שֶׁהַחֹדֶשׁ מִתְכַּסֶּה בּוֹ? הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר זֶה רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה, Which is the festival on which the new moon is covered? You must say that this is Rosh Hashanah." The pilgrim festivals that have fixed dates in their lunar months—Passover and Sukkot—fall in the middle of the month, when the moon is full and bright; only the first of Tishrei (Rosh Hashanah) falls on the new moon, when the crescent is still hidden in the sun's glare. On this rabbinic reading (followed by the Targum's בְּיַרְחָא דְמִתְכַּסֵי, "in the month that is covered," and reflected in the Edot HaMizrach custom of blessing every new month except Tishrei so that its moon remains "covered"), the two halves of verse 4 say the same thing twice: blow the shofar on the new moon—that is, at the hidden moon of our New Year feast.

Comparative Semitic philology, however, points in the opposite direction. In Akkadian (*kuse'u*, the "headdress" or "cap" of the moon god Sin on the fifteenth day), Phoenician, and Syriac, the noun denotes the **full moon**. If בַּכֵּסֶה means "on the full moon," then the two halves of verse 4 are not synonymous; they span the entire two-week autumn festival season of the seventh month, from the shofar blast on the new moon (בַחֹדֶשׁ, Tishrei 1) to the great pilgrimage feast of Sukkot ("our feast day," לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ, which the Bible calls simply הֶחָג, "the Feast," in 1 Kgs 8:2) on the full moon of Tishrei 15.

We do not have to choose between the two readings to appreciate what each does for the poem. On the full-moon reading, the psalm brackets the two opposite extremes of the lunar cycle—the dark new moon (בַחֹדֶשׁ) and the blazing full moon (בַּכֵּסֶה)—into a single liturgical event. Physical oceanography recognizes those same two opposite phases as twins: "spring tides," the moments when the sea rises to its highest range, occur twice each month, both at the dark new moon and at the bright full moon, because at both moments the sun, earth, and moon align in syzygy and pull in the same line. On the rabbinic "covered moon" reading, meanwhile, בַּכֵּסֶה starts a sequence of concealments that leads straight into the oracle: the moon is covered in verse 4 (בַּכֵּסֶה), the prophet hears an unknown tongue in verse 6 (שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי), and God answers from a hiding place of thunder in verse 8 (בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם).

In either case, the shofar blast of verse 4 is an auditory weapon meant to pierce the sky and change the atmosphere of the courtyard—much like the moment at dawn in J. R. R. Tolkien's *The Return of the King* when, amid the clash of arms and before the siege of Gondor is overthrown, a sound splits the dark:

> Great horns of the North wildly blowing.

> Great horns of the North wildly blowing.

In Tolkien's epic, the horns announce that the king's riders have arrived to save the city. In Psalm 81, the worshipers blow their horns expecting a royal triumph of their own—and instead summon the prosecutor of Sinai.

**Verses 5–6**

כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא; מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב.

> For it is a statute for Israel, a ruling of the God of Jacob;

עֵדוּת בִּיהוֹסֵף שָׂמוֹ, בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם; שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע.

> A testimony He set in Yehosef when He went out against the land of Egypt; a language I did not know I hear.

Verses 5 and 6a stack three legal nouns in parallel—חֹק, "statute, מִשְׁפָּט, ruling," and עֵדוּת, "testimony"—alongside three expanding names for the nation: יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Israel, יַעֲקֹב, Jacob," and יְהוֹסֵף, "Yehosef." Both the legal terms and the prepositions are slyly double-edged. In verse 5, the preposition **לְ** (*lə-*) appears twice: חֹק **לְ**יִשְׂרָאֵל, "a statute *for* Israel," and מִשְׁפָּט **לֵ**אלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "a ruling *of* [or *for*] the God of Jacob." In the Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Rosh Hashanah* 1:3), the rabbis take both prepositions the same way and deduce a startling rule of calendar law: אִם אֵינוֹ חוֹק לִיִשְׂרָאֵל, כִּבְיָכוֹל אֵינוֹ מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "If it is not a statute for Israel, then, so to speak, it is not a judgment for the God of Jacob." If the human court on earth decides to postpone the declaration of the new moon of Tishrei until tomorrow, God tells the ministering angels to take down the heavenly courtroom dais until tomorrow, because Heaven's judgment waits on Israel's calendar. Malbim, reading the two nouns side by side, draws a different contrast: for Israel, blowing the horn is a חֹק, an inscrutable decree whose deeper reason is hidden from the worshiper, whereas for the God of Jacob it is a מִשְׁפָּט, a rational judgment whose purpose He knows in full.

Verse 6 then introduces two famous puzzles before the hinge of 6c. First, the name Joseph is spelled יְהוֹסֵף (*Yehosef*), with an internal letter *heh*—the only time in the entire Hebrew Bible that the name appears in this five-letter form (a spelling also preserved in the Dead Sea Scrolls manuscript 11QPs^d). Historically, *Yehosef* may preserve an archaic northern Israelite pronunciation incorporating the divine name *Yeho-*, matching the northern prominence of the tribes of Joseph (Ephraim and Manasseh) in the Asaph psalms (compare Pss 77:16; 78:67; 80:2). For the rabbis, however, that extra letter *heh* was a badge of moral courage. In *b. Sotah* 10b, Rav Hanin bar Bizna says in the name of Rabbi Shimon Ḥasida: יוֹסֵף שֶׁקִּדֵּשׁ שֵׁם שָׁמַיִם בַּסֵּתֶר, זָכָה וְהוֹסִיפוּ לוֹ אוֹת אַחַת מִשְּׁמוֹ שֶׁל הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, דִּכְתִיב: עֵדוּת בִּיהוֹסֵף שָׂמוֹ, "Joseph, who sanctified the Name of Heaven in secret [by refusing Potiphar's wife when no one was watching], merited that one letter from the Name of the Holy One, blessed be He, was added to his name, as it is written: 'A testimony He set in *Yehosef*.'"

Second, what does בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם mean? English translations often render "when he went forth *from* the land of Egypt," as if עַל (*ʿal*, "over" or "against") meant מִן (*min*, "from"). It does not. The phrase בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם is a close echo of Genesis 41:45, וַיֵּצֵא יוֹסֵף עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "And Joseph went out *over* the land of Egypt" as Pharaoh's newly elevated viceroy (and in Genesis 47:26, וַיָּשֶׂם אֹתָהּ יוֹסֵף לְחֹק... עַל־אַדְמַת מִצְרַיִם, "Joseph set it as a statute... over the land of Egypt," uniting the words שׂים, חֹק, and Joseph with a parallel phrase for "over the land of Egypt," just as in verses 5–6). At the same time, as Radak argues (והנכון בעיני כי בצאתו נאמר על האל יתברך כלומר כשיצא על ארץ מצרים לגאלינו, "And the correct view in my eyes is that 'when He went out' is said of God, blessed be He—that is, when He went out against the land of Egypt to redeem us"), the phrase equally describes God marching out *against* Egypt on the night of the Exodus (Exod 11:4). The poet has chosen a phrase that superimposes two memories at once: Joseph rising from the dungeon to rule over Egypt, and God striding out over Egypt to free Joseph's descendants.

And then comes the three-word jolt that splits the psalm in two: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "A language I did not know I hear." The introduction showed how this first-person verb marks the Levitical prophet hearing God's voice break into the temple music. Notice how strange it is to call God's voice "a language I did not know." In *Exodus Rabbah* 5:9, Rabbi Yohanan imagines the voice at Sinai in the exact opposite way—as a voice that split into seventy tongues so that every nation on earth heard its own familiar native speech:

> אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: הָיָה הַקּוֹל יוֹצֵא וְנֶחְלָק לְשִׁבְעִים קוֹלוֹת לְשִׁבְעִים לָשׁוֹן כְּדֵי שֶׁיִּשְׁמְעוּ כָּל הָאֻמּוֹת, וְכָל אֻמָּה וְאֻמָּה שׁוֹמַעַת קוֹל בִּלְשׁוֹן הָאֻמָּה.

>

> Rabbi Yohanan said: The voice went forth and divided into seventy voices, into seventy languages, so that all the nations might hear; and each and every nation heard the voice in the language of that nation.

Why, then, does our psalm's speaker experience the divine voice as a language he *did not know*? A 1984 study in developmental linguistics by Janet F. Werker and Richard C. Tees offers an unexpected answer. Testing infants between six and twelve months of age, Werker and Tees showed that six-month-old babies growing up in English-speaking homes can effortlessly hear and distinguish consonant contrasts from languages they have never heard before, such as Hindi or Interior Salish. By ten to twelve months of age, however, the brain's "perceptual reorganization" tunes the child's ear so tightly to the familiar sounds of its own native environment that it literally loses the ability to hear the foreign contrasts. Habit deafens the ear to anything outside its own repertoire. In Psalm 81, Israel has grown so accustomed to the sound of its own temple songs and its own inner counsels (v. 13) that when the real voice of God breaks into the liturgy, it registers as an alien tongue.

Literature knows moments when an encounter with a sacred object or a private crisis suddenly flips into being addressed by an unfamiliar voice that demands a new life. One is Book 8 of Augustine's *Confessions* (8.12.29), where Augustine, weeping in a Milan garden, hears a voice from a neighboring house—he cannot tell whether it is a boy's or a girl's, nor recognize any children's game it belongs to—chanting *tolle, lege*, "Take up and read." The other is Rainer Maria Rilke's 1908 sonnet *Archaic Torso of Apollo*, which begins with what the viewer "did not know" (*Wir kannten nicht sein unerhörtes Haupt*). In Jessie Lemont's translation, it reads:

> We cannot fathom his mysterious head,

> Through the veiled eyes no flickering ray is sent:

> But from his torso gleaming light is shed

> As from a candelabrum; inward bent

> His glance there glows and lingers. Otherwise

> The round breast would not blind you with its grace,

> Nor could the soft-curved circle of the thighs

> Steal to the arc whence issues a new race.

> Nor could this stark and stunted stone display

> Vibrance beneath the shoulders' heavy bar,

> Nor shine like fur upon a beast of prey,

> Nor break forth from its lines like a great star—

> There is no spot that does not bind you fast

> And transport you back, back to a far past.

Rilke's German ends more directly: "There is no spot that does not see you. You must change your life." That is the hinge of verse 6c: the worshipers came to the temple to perform before God, and suddenly discover that the liturgy is looking back at them and speaking.

**Verse 7**

הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ; כַּפָּיו מִדּוּד תַּעֲבֹרְנָה.

> I removed his shoulder from the burden; his palms passed away from the basket.

God's first words in the oracle do not mention covenants or commandments; they begin with the ache in a laborer's collarbone and palms. In Second Temple times, as the Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Rosh Hashanah* 4:4) and Babylonian Talmud (*b. Rosh Hashanah* 30b; *b. Sukkah* 55a) record, the Levites treated this verse as the start of an independent hymn—singing from הַרְנִינוּ, "Sing joyously" (v. 2), at one service and starting right here at הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ, "I removed his shoulder from the burden," at another—marking in ancient temple performance the seam where modern readers hear the divine voice begin.

Every word in the verse is tactile. The noun סֵבֶל, "burden" (the singular counterpart of the plural סִבְלֹת, "forced burdens," of Exod 1:11 and 6:6), can denote a burden of state-imposed corvée labor; outside our verse and Nehemiah 4:11, this singular form occurs in the Hebrew Bible only at 1 Kings 11:28, where Solomon appoints Jeroboam over לְכׇל־סֵבֶל בֵּית יוֹסֵף, "all the forced labor of the house of Joseph"—the very grievance that split the northern kingdom from Jerusalem. Pairing "Yehosef" in verse 6 with סֵבֶל, "forced labor," and שִׁכְמוֹ, "his shoulder" (which shares its consonants with שְׁכֶם, *Shekhem*, the northern covenant capital where Joseph's bones were buried in Joshua 24:32), would have made any northern Israelite ear tingle. And the דּוּד, "basket" or "cauldron" (which Rashi glosses as the slave's cooking pot, and Ibn Ezra and modern lexicographers identify as the clay-hod used for hauling mud and bricks), comes straight out of the visual world of ancient Near Eastern brickmaking. In Mesopotamian royal inscriptions, such as the cylinders of Gudea of Lagash, Gudea carries a holy basket of clay or mortar on his head in a prescribed temple-building rite; at the opposite end of the social scale, in the fifteenth-century BCE Tomb of Rekhmire at Thebes (TT100), Egyptian wall paintings show Syrian and Nubian captives hauling mud in baskets to the brick-molds under the rods of overseers, while bioarchaeological studies of laborers' skeletons from Akhenaten's capital at Amarna document the crushed vertebrae and degenerated shoulder joints left by a lifetime under those baskets.

Look at the verb at the end of the verse: תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "passed away from." In the Masoretic text it is spelled with a *resh* (from עבר, "to pass over" or "pass away"), so that the slave's palms pass free of the basket. The Septuagint, however, read the nearly identical Hebrew letter *dalet* instead of *resh*—תַּעֲבֹדְנָה, from עבד, "to serve" or "be enslaved"—and translated αἱ χεῖρες αὐτοῦ ἐν τῷ κοφίνῳ ἐδούλευσαν (*hai cheires autou en tō kophinō edouleusan*), "his hands slaved in the basket." Remarkably, the root עבד, "slave/serve," never appears anywhere in the Masoretic text of Psalm 81, even where the psalm quotes the Ten Commandments (omitting Exodus 20:2's מִבֵּית עֲבָדִים, "from the house of slaves," in v. 11). By a single stroke of the pen distinguishing *resh* from *dalet*, the Hebrew text turns servitude (עבד) into passing free (עבר).

Why does God begin His lawsuit by reminding Israel of the physical feel of the brick-basket? Because until you remember what slavery felt like in your shoulders and hands, you do not know who you are talking to. In Chapter 10 of his 1845 *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*, Douglass describes the moment he finally pried the overseer Covey's fingers from his throat:

> He only can understand the deep satisfaction which I experienced, who has himself repelled by force the bloody arm of slavery. I felt as I never felt before. It was a glorious resurrection, from the tomb of slavery, to the heaven of freedom.

Douglass had to wrest his own shoulders free; in verse 7, God reminds Israel that *He* lifted the basket from their palms, leaving those hands empty for what comes next.

**Verse 8**

בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ וָאֲחַלְּצֶךָּ; אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, אֶבְחׇנְךָ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה סֶלָה.

> In distress you called and I rescued you; I answered you in the hiding place of thunder; I tested you at the waters of Meribah. Selah.

In verse 7 God spoke of Israel in the third person ("*his* shoulder... *his* palms"). Now, as the memory moves from the brickfields to the cry for help, God turns to address Israel face to face in the second person singular, binding the three clauses together with a triple rhyme on the suffix **־ךָ** (*-khā*, "you"): וָאֲחַלְּצֶ**ךָּ** (`wā-'a-ḥal-lə-TSEKH-khā`), "and I rescued you"; אֶעֶנְ**ךָ** (`'e-ʿen-KHĀ`), "I answered you"; אֶבְחׇנְ**ךָ** (`'ev-ḥon-KHĀ`), "I tested you."

The middle phrase—אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "I answered you in the hiding place of thunder"—is an evocative paradox in the Psalter. Thunder (רַעַם) is the loudest, most public phenomenon in nature; a hiding place (סֵתֶר) is the most private. How can God answer in both at once? Rashi hears a contrast between how Israel prayed and how God replied: אַתָּה קְרָאתַנִי בְּסֵתֶר, בֵּינִי וּבֵינֶךָ, וַאֲנִי עָנִיתִיךָ בְּקוֹל רַעַם; הוֹדַעְתִּי גְּבוּרוֹת וְנוֹרָאוֹת בְּפַרְהֶסְיָא, "You called Me in secret, between Me and you, and I answered you with a voice of thunder; I made known My mighty and awesome deeds in public." At the same time, the phrase describes the storm-cloud of Sinai itself (compare Ps 18:12, יָשֶׁת חֹשֶׁךְ סִתְרוֹ, "He made darkness His hiding place"): God comes near enough to answer the cry of the distressed, yet remains veiled inside the dark thunderhead so that He cannot be seen or managed like an idol. It is the supernatural counterpart to the moment near the end of Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* (Chapter 35) when Jane, in an agony of indecision and praying for a path, hears a cry cut through the night from an unseen source miles away:

> I saw nothing, but I heard a voice somewhere cry—

> "Jane! Jane! Jane!"—nothing more.

Then the third verb of the rhyme—אֶבְחׇנְךָ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה, "I tested you at the waters of Meribah"—turns the wilderness tradition upside down. In Exodus 17:7 and in Psalm 95:9 (אֲשֶׁר נִסּוּנִי אֲבוֹתֵיכֶם, בְּחָנוּנִי, "where your fathers tried Me, tested Me"), it was *Israel* who tested *God* at Massah and Meribah by demanding water from the rock. Here God uses the exact same verb (בחן, "to test" or "assay") and says: *I* tested *you* at the waters of Meribah. A closely related passage casts God as tester and contender at these twin wilderness sites: Moses' blessing of the tribe of Levi in Deuteronomy 33:8, אֲשֶׁר נִסִּיתוֹ בְּמַסָּה, תְּרִיבֵהוּ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה, "whom You tested at Massah, with whom You contended at the waters of Meribah." The Asaphite poet—himself a Levite—has adopted his own tribe's perspective on the Massah-Meribah crisis, where the confrontation at the rock was God's examination of Israel's loyalty. And right on the word מְרִיבָה, "Meribah" (literally "strife" or "contention," from the root ריב, *rîb*, a covenant lawsuit), the poet places the psalm's only סֶלָה, "Selah." The musical interlude forces the congregation to sit in silence with the memory of the rock where they failed the test—before verse 9 gives them the test all over again.

**Verse 9**

שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ; יִשְׂרָאֵל, אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי.

> Hear, My people, and I will admonish you; Israel, if you would but listen to Me!

After the pause of *Selah*, the divine voice resumes with the word that every Israelite knows as the watchword of covenant loyalty: שְׁמַע, "Hear!" (compare Deut 6:4, שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Hear, O Israel," and the twin Asaphite summons in Ps 50:7, שִׁמְעָה עַמִּי וַאֲדַבֵּרָה, יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, "Hear, My people, and I will speak; O Israel, and I will testify against you"). Notice the verb וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, "and I will admonish you" or "testify against you." It comes from the same root (עוד, "witness/testimony") as עֵדוּת, "testimony," in verse 6. In verse 6, the congregation boasted that their noisy festival was an עֵדוּת, a "testimony" ordained in Joseph; now God takes that noun and turns it into a prosecutorial verb (וְאָעִידָה, "I will testify") directed at them. In doing so, the psalm enacts the charge God gave Moses in Deuteronomy 31:19–21 when He told him to put a song in Israel's mouth לְמַעַן תִּהְיֶה־לִּי הַשִּׁירָה הַזֹּאת לְעֵד בִּבְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, "so that this song may be for Me a witness against the children of Israel."

How do you get through to a crowd that is busy making its own festival din? In 1959, the cognitive psychologist Neville Moray published an experiment on what is called the "cocktail-party effect." Subjects wore headphones playing two different spoken messages simultaneously into their left and right ears and were told to shadow the message in one ear while ignoring the other. They missed almost everything said in the unattended ear—with one consistent exception: when the speaker in the ignored channel spoke the listener's own name, subjects suddenly heard it. Calling a person's name is the one acoustic signal built to pierce unattended noise. In verse 9, God calls out both intimate names across the two halves of the line—עַמִּי, "My people," and יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Israel"—and leaves the conditional clause hanging in mid-air: יִשְׂרָאֵל, אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי, "Israel, if you would but listen to Me!"

In 1908, the American composer Charles Ives wrote a short orchestral piece, *The Unanswered Question*, that shares the exact acoustic shape of our psalm from verse 9 to the end, with the roles reversed. Over quiet, sustained strings, a solo trumpet states a short, searching phrase six times; each time, a quartet of flutes tries to answer with increasingly loud, fast, discordant chatter, until the flutes fall silent and the trumpet sounds its question one last time into the void. In Ives, humanity asks and heaven is silent. In Psalm 81, God is the solo voice pleading "Israel, if you would listen to Me!" while the human instruments make all the noise.

**Verses 10–11**

לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ קֵל זָר; וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְקֵל נֵכָר.

> There shall be no strange god in you, and you shall not bow to a foreign god.

אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ, הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם; הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ.

> I am the LORD your God, who brought you up from the land of Egypt; open your mouth wide and I will fill it.

The introduction traced how these two verses invert the order of Exodus 20:2–3 so that the Decalogue's credential ("I am the LORD your God...") leads directly into the invitation to open the mouth and be fed. Two further details in the Hebrew sharpen the edge of that invitation.

First, look at the participle in verse 11: הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "who brought you up from the land of Egypt." Both versions of the Ten Commandments in the Torah (Exod 20:2 and Deut 5:6) use a different verb: אֲשֶׁר הוֹצֵאתִיךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "who brought you *out* from the land of Egypt." Why does Psalm 81 substitute הַמַּעַלְךָ (from העלה, "to bring *up*")? Because in the northern kingdom of Israel—the "house of Joseph" invoked in verse 6—העלה was the verb of the golden calves. When Aaron fashioned the calf at Sinai (Exod 32:4), and when Jeroboam set up his twin golden calves at Bethel and Dan to rival Jerusalem's autumn festival (1 Kgs 12:28), their liturgical proclamations were nearly identical: Aaron's began אֵלֶּה אֱלֹקֶיךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל אֲשֶׁר הֶעֱלוּךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up from the land of Egypt!" while Jeroboam's began הִנֵּה אֱלֹקֶיךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Behold your gods, O Israel," followed by the exact same words. By pairing the ban on any קֵל זָר, "strange god" (v. 10), with אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "I am YHWH your God who brought you up from the land of Egypt" (v. 11), the psalm echoes and reverses the northern calf-slogan for YHWH alone (a connection the sixteenth-century commentator Alshich, in *Romemot El*, already heard hovering behind these verses).

Second, consider what it means to put הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "open your mouth wide and I will fill it," immediately after the prohibition of bowing to a foreign god. In ancient Israel, idol worship was also performed with the mouth: worshipers kissed the calves (Hos 13:2, זֹבְחֵי אָדָם עֲגָלִים יִשָּׁקוּן, "those who sacrifice men kiss calves"; compare the seven thousand in 1 Kings 19:18 whose mouths had not kissed Baal). And in *Surat al-Baqarah* (2:93), the Qur'an condenses Israel's refusal to listen at Sinai and the worship of the golden calf into a dark twin of our verse—a mouth opened not to God's food, but to drink the idol into the heart:

> And [recall] when We took your covenant and raised over you the mount, [saying], "Take what We have given you with determination and listen." They said [instead], "We hear and disobey." And their hearts absorbed [the worship of] the calf because of their disbelief.

Against mouths that kiss calves or hearts that drink them in, verse 11 asks only for an empty cavity. In lost-wax bronze casting, preparation of the mold includes coating a wax model and its attached rods with potter's clay, letting the clay dry, and heating the assembly in a furnace so that the clay fires and the wax burns out; only where the lost wax has left a completely empty negative space can the molten bronze rush in and take form. So too in ethology, altricial songbird chicks—born blind and featherless in the nest—survive by a single reflex: at the vibration of the parent's landing, they fling their beaks open as wide as the hinge will go, exposing a bright target that triggers the parent's feeding response (an image already used for divine providence in the Egyptian *Great Hymn to the Aten*, where the deity sustains the chirping chick inside the shell). Classical Jewish commentators translated that fledgling image into prayer and study: Rashi glosses הַרְחֶב פִּיךָ, "open your mouth wide," as לִשְׁאוֹל מִמֶּנִּי כָּל תַּאֲוַת לִבְּךָ, "to request of Me whatever your heart desires," while the Talmud (*b. Berakhot* 50a) applies it to asking boldly for wisdom in Torah, where no request is too wide.

In her 1958 story *Babette's Feast*, Isak Dinesen captured the comedy of religious people who are terrified to open their mouths to abundance. A tiny, austere pietist sect in a Norwegian fjord—who have spent their lives renouncing bodily pleasure and vowing not even to notice what they eat—sit down to a sumptuous French banquet secretly prepared by their housekeeper Babette, formerly chef at the Café Anglais in Paris:

> At the word of ‘food’ the guests, with their old heads bent over their folded hands, remembered how they had vowed not to utter a word about the subject, and in their hearts they reinforced the vow: they would not even give it a thought! They were sitting down to a meal, well, so had people done at the wedding of Cana. And grace has chosen to manifest itself there, in the very wine, as fully as anywhere.

> Babette’s boy filled a small glass before each of the party. They lifted it to their lips gravely, in confirmation of their resolution.

> General Loewenhielm, somewhat suspicious of his wine, took a sip of it, startled, raised the glass first to his nose and then to his eyes, and sat it down bewildered. ‘This is very strange!’ he thought. ‘Amontillado! And the finest Amontillado that I have ever tasted.’

Even Dinesen's ascetics, despite their vows, open their mouths and are transfigured by the feast. In verse 12, Israel will not even open its mouth.

**Verses 12–13**

וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי; וְיִשְׂרָאֵל, לֹא־אָבָה לִי.

> But My people did not listen to My voice, and Israel was not willing for Me.

וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם; יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם.

> So I let him go in the stubbornness of their heart; let them walk in their own counsels.

Look at the second verb in verse 12: לֹא־אָבָה לִי, "was not willing for Me," or "wanted nothing to do with Me." In Biblical Hebrew, the verb אבה, "to be willing" or "to consent," almost never appears in the affirmative (outside Isaiah 1:19, אִם־תֹּאבוּ וּשְׁמַעְתֶּם, טוּב הָאָרֶץ תֹּאכֵלוּ, "If you are willing and listen, you shall eat the good of the land," which pairs the same two verbs with eating!). Elsewhere, apart from a rhetorical question in Job 39:9, it appears with a negative particle to express a stone-faced refusal of desire. Most piercingly, in Deuteronomy 13:9, Moses uses these exact two negated verbs to command how Israel must treat a family member or close friend who tries to entice them to worship foreign gods: לֹא־תֹאבֶה לוֹ וְלֹא תִשְׁמַע אֵלָיו, "You shall not be willing for him, and you shall not listen to him." In Psalm 81:12, Israel treats YHWH the way the Torah told them to treat an idol-enticer. It is the maddening, impenetrable weapon of passive non-response that Herman Melville immortalized in *Bartleby, the Scrivener* (1853), where the clerk brings his employer's entire world to a standstill without ever raising his voice, simply by answering every request and every kindness with five quiet words:

> "I would prefer not to."

> "I would prefer not to."

Notice, too, the shift between singular and plural across verse 13: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵ**הוּ** בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּ**ם**, יֵלְכ**וּ** בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵי**הֶם**, "So I let **him** go in the stubbornness of **their** heart; let **them** walk in **their** own counsels." As Meiri remarks, Hebrew poetry often alternates between collective singular and plural when speaking of the nation, yet here the shift does poetic work. When God remembers Israel as His covenant partner, the people are a single person ("I let *him* go"); the moment they are released into their own stubbornness, they splinter into a plural crowd ("*their* heart... let *them* walk in *their* counsels"), each person following his own head.

The noun שְׁרִירוּת, "stubbornness" or "hardness" (related to Aramaic שָׁרִיר, "firm/hard," though Rashi connects it to the root שׁור, "to look," as בְּמַרְאִית לִבָּם, "in the outlook of their heart," while the Targum renders it בְּהִרְהוּרֵי לִבְּהוֹן, "in the thoughts of their heart"), is the signature diagnostic word of Deuteronomy 29:18 and the book of Jeremiah, where it occurs eight times—above all in Jeremiah 7:24: וְלֹא שָׁמְעוּ וְלֹא־הִטּוּ אֶת־אׇזְנָם, וַיֵּלְכוּ בְּמֹעֵצוֹת בִּשְׁרִרוּת לִבָּם הָרָע, "Yet they did not listen and did not incline their ear, but walked in counsels, in the stubbornness of their evil heart."

As the introduction showed, God's judgment in verse 13 is simply to take His hands off the wheel and let them walk where they insist on going—the truth C. S. Lewis formulated in *The Great Divorce* (1945), where George MacDonald explains that in the end there are only two kinds of people: those who say to God, "Thy will be done," and those to whom God says, in the end, "Thy will be done." Yet compare how other poets stage this moment. At the opening of Homer's *Odyssey* (Book 1, lines 32–43), Zeus looks down from Olympus at the ruin of Aegisthus—who murdered Agamemnon and married his wife despite an explicit warning sent through Hermes—and complains to the assembled gods with cool, self-exonerating detachment:

> "Alas, how shamelessly mortals blame the gods! For from us, they say, come evils, yet they themselves by their own reckless follies have sorrows beyond what is ordained—just as now Aegisthus, beyond what was ordained, married the wedded wife of the son of Atreus and slew him on his return, though he knew of sheer destruction, since we spoke to him beforehand, sending Hermes, the keen-sighted slayer of Argus, that he should neither slay the man nor woo his wife."

Homer's Zeus washes his hands of human folly and feels no grief. George Herbert, in the closing lines of *The Collar* (1633), imagines the opposite resolution: the rebellious speaker raves harder and harder in his willful heart until a single word from God breaks his stride:

> But as I rav'd, and grew more fierce and wild

> At every word,

> Me thoughts I heard one calling, "Child";

> And I replied, "My Lord."

Psalm 81 is more unsettling than either Homer or Herbert. Unlike Herbert's speaker, Israel never replies "My Lord." And unlike Homer's Zeus, Israel's God cannot stay coolly detached on Olympus after letting them go.

**Verses 14–16**

לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי; יִשְׂרָאֵל, בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ.

> If only My people were listening to Me, if Israel would walk in My ways!

כִּמְעַט אוֹיְבֵיהֶם אַכְנִיעַ; וְעַל צָרֵיהֶם אָשִׁיב יָדִי.

> In a moment I would subdue their enemies, and against their foes I would turn My hand.

מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳ יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ; וִיהִי עִתָּם לְעוֹלָם.

> Those who hate the LORD would cringe before Him, and their time would be forever.

The introduction examined how God's sigh in verse 14 (לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי, "If only My people were listening to Me!") shatters the judicial detachment of verse 13. That sigh belongs to a small, extraordinary group of biblical passages—Deuteronomy 5:26 (5:29 in many English Bibles) and Isaiah 48:18 (לוּא הִקְשַׁבְתָּ לְמִצְוֹתָי, "If only you had paid heed to My commandments!"), alongside Moses' parallel wish in Deuteronomy 32:29 (לוּ חָכְמוּ, "If only they were wise!")—where the Sovereign of the universe or His prophet speaks in the optative mood of unfulfilled longing. Later Jewish and Christian texts returned repeatedly to this grief. In the Gospel of Luke (19:41–42), Jesus approaches Jerusalem and weeps over it with the same broken conditional:

> As he approached Jerusalem and saw the city, he wept over it and said, "If you, even you, had only known on this day what would bring you peace—but now it is hidden from your eyes."

And in *Lamentations Rabbah* (Proem 24), the midrash pictures God weeping after the destruction of the Temple over the very judgment He has just carried out:

> הָיָה הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא בּוֹכֶה וְאוֹמֵר: אוֹי לִי מָה עָשִׂיתִי! הִשְׁרֵיתִי שְׁכִינָתִי לְמַטָּה בִּשְׁבִיל יִשְׂרָאֵל, וְעַכְשָׁיו שֶׁחָטְאוּ חָזַרְתִּי לִמְקוֹמִי הָרִאשׁוֹן.

>

> The Holy One, blessed be He, wept and said: "Woe is Me! What have I done? I caused My Presence to dwell below on earth for the sake of Israel, and now that they have sinned, I have returned to My former place!"

Notice how verses 14–16 work as a calculation of what might have been: if they were listening (v. 14), then כִּמְעַט, "in a moment" or "at once" (or, as Malbim reads it, even if they listened only "a little," כִּמְעַט), I would subdue their enemies and turn My hand back against their foes (v. 15). In 2007, the computer scientists Martin Zinkevich, Michael Johanson, Michael Bowling, and Carmelo Piccione introduced an algorithm called Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR), which became the engine behind superhuman poker-playing artificial intelligence. At each information set in a game of imperfect information, the algorithm computes "counterfactual regret"—evaluating, in terms of counterfactual utility weighted by the counterfactual probability of reaching that information set had the player tried to do so, how much better an alternative action would have fared—and uses that regret to update its own future play. Verses 14–16 are a counterfactual-regret calculation spoken aloud by God: here is the payoff—enemies subdued in an instant, haters cringing—that lay down the untaken road. The tragedy is that an algorithm can update its own choices, whereas God is voicing the regret for another player whose will He refuses to coerce.

Verse 16 contains two subtle ambiguities. First, inside God's first-person speech, the poet suddenly refers to God in the third person: מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳ יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ, "Those who hate the LORD would cringe before Him" (matching Moses' blessing in Deut 33:29, וְיִכָּחֲשׁוּ אֹיְבֶיךָ לָךְ, "and your enemies shall come cringing to you"). Radak and Meiri suggest that the pronoun לוֹ, "to him," may actually refer to Israel: those who hate YHWH would cringe before *Israel*, denying out of fear that they had ever harmed God's people during their exile. Second, whose is עִתָּם, "their time," at the end of the verse (וִיהִי עִתָּם לְעוֹלָם, "and their time would be forever")? Rashi and Malbim take "their" to refer to the nearest noun, the haters of YHWH: the time of their doom and humiliation would last forever (compare Ezek 30:3, עֵת גּוֹיִם, "the time of doom for the nations"). Radak and Meiri take "their" to refer to Israel, as in verses 13–15 ("their heart," "their counsels," "their enemies," "their foes"): Israel's season of blessing would endure forever without interruption. The jussive verb וִיהִי, "and may it be" ("and let their time be forever!"), sounds like the prophet or congregation momentarily chiming in with a wish—which may also explain why the next word in verse 17 (וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ, "And He would feed him") starts in the third person before God reclaims the microphone for the final three words.

**Verse 17**

וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה; וּמִצּוּר, דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ.

> And He would feed him from the fat of wheat, and from the rock with honey I would sate you.

The introduction showed how this closing verse reverses the tragic sequence of Deuteronomy 32:13–15—placing the banquet of finest wheat and rock-honey *after* Israel's rebellion rather than before it—and how the final word אַשְׂבִּיעֶ**ךָ**, "I would sate **you**," turns from third-person wistfulness to look straight at the singular worshiper.

Look closely at the two foods on this final table. The first, חֵלֶב חִטָּה, literally "the fat of wheat" (rendered by the Targum טוּב לְחֵם חִטִּין, "the best of wheat bread," and echoed only in Deut 32:14 and Ps 147:14, חֵלֶב חִטִּים יַשְׂבִּיעֵךְ, "He sates you with the fat of wheat"), represents the pinnacle of settled agricultural labor: plowed soil, timely autumn rains, harvested grain, and winnowed flour. The second food, וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ, "and from the rock, honey," comes from the opposite world—the unplowed, barren stone of the wilderness. In the tenth-to-ninth-century BCE apiary excavated by Amihai Mazar at Tel Rehov in the Beth Shean Valley, rows of clay cylinder hives housed managed colonies of Anatolian honeybees, showing how labor-intensive actual beekeeping was in biblical Israel. Yet in the wild—as with the giant cliff honeybees (*Apis laboriosa*) that hang massive, exposed honeycombs directly from sheer granite overhangs in the Himalayas, or wild bees nesting in Judean limestone clefts—sweetness can literally drip from naked stone.

By pairing "the fat of wheat" with "honey from the rock," the psalmist joins the best that human farming can harvest in the promised land with the miracle that only God can bring out of wilderness stone. For Ibn Ezra, the verse primarily looks back to the desert generation—the manna that was nobler than the fat of wheat, and the water from the rock that was sweeter than honey—though he also reports an alternative reading in which the honey clause addresses Israel in the future. For Rashi, the honey recalls what God gave Israel when they walked in His ways (הִשְׂבִּיעָם דְּבַשׁ כְּשֶׁהָלְכוּ בִדְרָכָיו, "He sated them with honey when they walked in His ways"), while Radak and Meiri describe the abundance God would provide (Meiri noting that וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ uses a past form in place of a future, עבר במקום עתיד). Notice how the two promises of verses 15 and 17 chime together at the end of their lines: in verse 15 God says אַכְנִ**יעַ** (`'akh-NIY-aʿ`), "I would subdue" your enemies, and in verse 17 He says אַשְׂבִּ**יעֶ**ךָ (`'as-biy-ʿE-khā`), "I would sate you." Both are first-person causative (*Hiphil*) verbs built on roots ending in the guttural letter *ʿayin* (כנע and שׂבע), linking military peace and physical fullness by sound. Even the hard, unforgiving stone of the Meribah test in verse 8 has been turned into a honeycomb—the wilderness counterpart to the exiled Duke Senior's discovery in the Forest of Arden in Shakespeare's *As You Like It* (Act 2, Scene 1):

> Sweet are the uses of adversity,

> Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous,

> Wears yet a precious jewel in his head;

> And this our life, exempt from public haunt,

> Finds tongues in trees, books in the running brooks,

> Sermons in stones, and good in everything.

Why, then, does the psalm stop right here, with a Masoretic open paragraph marker (`{פ}`) and no reply from the congregation? In the 1892 English Court of Appeal case *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co*, the judges had to decide whether a newspaper advertisement promising £100 to anyone who used the company's smoke ball and still caught influenza was a binding contract even though Mrs. Carlill had never written to notify the company that she accepted their offer. Lord Justice Bowen ruled that an offer made to the world at large—a "unilateral contract"—requires no verbal reply to become binding: "the performance of the conditions is the acceptance of the offer."

God's offer in Psalm 81 has the exact legal shape of a unilateral contract. Spoken aloud to the whole festival crowd, הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "Open your mouth wide and I will fill it" (v. 11), and וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "and from the rock with honey I would sate you" (v. 17), ask for no verbal pledge in return. Words and songs were what got in the way in the first place. The offer stays open in the silence after the last word, waiting for the listener to accept it by doing the thing itself.
