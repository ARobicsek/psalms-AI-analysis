Every year on Rosh Hashanah, at the climax of the Musaf service, the synagogue liturgy makes its case for blowing the ram's horn by quoting two verses from Psalm 81. In the *Shofarot* blessing, the congregation recites verses 4 and 5: תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר, בַּכֵּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ. כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא, מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "Blow the horn on the new moon, on the appointed time for our feast day. For it is a law for Israel, a ruling of the God of Jacob." And there the prayer book stops. It cuts off right before verse 6, where the psalm springs its trap. In the psalm itself, no sooner has the congregation blown its horn and struck its timbrels than a voice breaks in from the thunder to silence the musicians. Psalm 81 is a festival liturgy interrupted by the God it is celebrating. It takes a crowd that has gathered to fill the sanctuary with song for "our feast day," strips away their collective voice, and teaches them that what covenant worship asks of a human mouth is not to emit sound, but to listen and be fed.

The architecture of the poem enacts this interruption. For four and a half verses (vv. 2–6b), a liturgical leader marshals the assembly with five plural commands—sing, shout, lift up song, strike the drum, blow the horn—and grounds the festival in the memory of the Exodus. Then, in the final words of verse 6, an unnamed "I" cuts across the music: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "A language I did not know I hear." From verse 7 on, God's voice takes over the psalm, with only brief shifts into third-person speech about Him near the close (vv. 16–17a). The divine address moves from the physical memory of lifting Egyptian brick-baskets off Israel's shoulders and answering them in Sinai's thunder (vv. 7–8), to an urgent plea built out of the Ten Commandments (vv. 9–11). It drops into the grief of Israel's refusal and God's decision to let them go (vv. 12–13). Finally, it breaks into an aching daydream—"If only my people were listening to me!"—that ends by holding out the finest wheat and honey from the rock to a silent listener (vv. 14–17). Once God begins to speak in verse 7, the congregation never utters another word.

### From Making Noise to Opening the Mouth

The opening verses sound like any other temple hymn, and that is how they lull the hearer. The choir leader piles up instruments—תֹף, "timbrel, כִּנּוֹר נָעִים, melodious lyre, נָבֶל, harp, שׁוֹפָר, ram's horn"—and binds the worshipers together with the suffix **־נוּ** (*-nū*, "our"). God is לֵאלֹקִים עוּזֵּ**נוּ**, "God, our strength" (v. 2), and the autumn pilgrimage is לְיוֹם חַגֵּ**נוּ**, "for our feast day" (v. 4). Notice, too, how the human speakers name God. They call Him אֱלֹקִים, "God," and אֱלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "the God of Jacob" (vv. 2, 5), the title that frames the opening hymn, but they never pronounce His personal name, ה׳. It is "our" feast, and God is our patron.

Yet the congregation's own ritual summons what it cannot control. In verse 2 they are told הָרִיעוּ (`hā-RIY-ʿū`), "raise a shout," and in verse 4 they are told תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר, "Blow the horn on the new moon." The shofar is not merely a festival instrument; it is the acoustic trigger of Mount Sinai. There, as Exodus recounts, קוֹל הַשֹּׁפָר הוֹלֵךְ וְחָזֵק מְאֹד, מֹשֶׁה יְדַבֵּר וְהָאֱלֹקִים יַעֲנֶנּוּ בְקוֹל, "the sound of the horn grew louder and louder; Moses would speak, and God would answer him in thunder" (Exod 19:19). When the worshipers reenact Sinai with their horns and their shouts (`hā-RIY-ʿū`), Sinai answers back. By verse 8, God is reminding them of His רַעַם (`RA-ʿam`), "thunder," whose consonants echo their shout.

The hinge snaps in verse 6c: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "A language I did not know I hear." Ancient readers were so startled by this sudden "I" that they tried to smooth it away. The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation) translates γλῶσσαν ἣν οὐκ ἔγνω ἤκουσεν (*glōssan hēn ouk egnō ēkousen*), "a tongue which he knew not he heard," turning the line into a third-person report about Joseph hearing an unfamiliar language when he came forth out of the land of Egypt. But the Hebrew verb אֶשְׁמָע is first-person imperfect—"I hear" (or, in a past setting, "I heard"). Heard in the present, it marks the moment the Levitical prophet in the temple is overtaken by a voice that is not the choir's.

The instant that voice takes over, the grammar of possession flips. After חַגֵּנוּ, "our feast day," in verse 4, the human "we" and "our" disappear from the psalm. God never says "our," and He never calls Himself "the God of Jacob." Instead, His personal name ה׳ appears (vv. 11, 16), and He addresses the crowd three times as עַמִּי, "My people," and יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Israel," with the syllable **לִי** (*lī*, "to Me") recurring in every plea:

> שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, יִשְׂרָאֵל אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־**לִי**. (v. 9)

> וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי, וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה **לִי**. (v. 12)

> לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ **לִי**, יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ. (v. 14)

>

> "Hear, My people, and I will admonish you; Israel, if you would but listen **to Me**!"

> "But My people would not listen to My voice, Israel would not obey **Me**."

> "If only My people would listen **to Me**, if Israel would follow My paths!"

Even the causative verbs (the Hebrew *Hiphil* stem, which expresses making something happen) change hands. In verse 2, the human leader gave Israel two causative commands to make noise: הַרְנִינוּ, "make a ringing cry," and הָרִיעוּ, "raise a shout." Once the oracle begins, almost every causative action belongs to God: הֲסִירוֹתִי, "I removed" the burden (v. 7), אַכְנִיעַ, "I would subdue" enemies (v. 15), and אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "I would sate you" (v. 17). God leaves Israel only one *Hiphil* command of His own, beginning with the same *har-* sound as their opening הַרְנִינוּ (`har-NIY-nū`) and הָרִיעוּ (`hā-RIY-ʿū`): הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ (`har-ḥev-PIY-khā wa-'a-mal-'Ē-hū`), "Open your mouth wide and I will fill it" (v. 11).

Look at where that command sits. Verses 10 and 11 quote the opening of the Ten Commandments, but backwards. At Sinai (Exod 20:2–3), God first announces His liberating act—"I am YHWH your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt"—and then issues the prohibition: "You shall have no other gods before Me." Psalm 81 reverses the sequence. First comes the prohibition in verse 10: לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ קֵל זָר, וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְקֵל נֵכָר, "You shall have no foreign god in you, you shall not bow to an alien god." Then, in verse 11, comes the self-introduction: אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "I the LORD am your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt; open your mouth wide and I will fill it." By inverting the Decalogue, the poet makes God's liberation from Egypt lead not into a restriction, but into a feeding. God remembers the slave's body in verse 7: הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ, כַּפָּיו מִדּוּד תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "I relieved his shoulder of the burden, his hands were freed from the basket." Having freed their shoulders from the heavy clay-carrying baskets of Egyptian forced labor, He asks for a different bodily posture now: not lips noisy with temple songs, but the gaping beak of a fledgling bird waiting to be fed.

The seventeenth-century English poet George Herbert staged this same discovery at the close of *Love (III)*, where the anxious sinner, convinced he must earn his keep, is disarmed by his host:

> "Truth, Lord, but I have marr'd them; let my shame

> Go where it doth deserve."

> "And know you not," says Love, "who bore the blame?"

> "My dear, then I will serve."

> "You must sit down," says Love, "and taste my meat."

> So I did sit and eat.

In Herbert's poem, the guest surrenders and eats. The tragedy of Psalm 81 is that God sets the same table, and the guest refuses to sit down.

### The God Who Cannot Keep His Distance

Why would a liberated people refuse an open-mouth feast? The psalm tracks the failure through five occurrences of the root שׁמע, "hear" or "listen." The only person in the entire psalm who actually hears in the indicative mood is the prophet at the hinge of verse 6c: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "A language I did not know I hear." When Israel cried out in Egypt, God heard and answered at once: בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ וָאֲחַלְּצֶךָּ, אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "In distress you called and I rescued you; I answered you from the secret place of thunder" (v. 8). Yet when God speaks in Israel's own tongue, the verb שׁמע slides steadily away from reality. In verse 9, it is an imperative followed by a sentence that breaks off mid-thought: שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, יִשְׂרָאֵל אִם־תִּֽשְׁמַֽע־לִֽי, "Hear, My people, and I will admonish you; Israel, if you would but listen to Me—" In the Masoretic text, secondary stress marks (*metegs*) slow the reader's voice on the last two words (`’im-tish-maʿ-LIY`), leaving the "if" hanging without a "then." By verse 12, the plea has collapsed into flat past negation: וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי, וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה לִי, "But My people would not listen to My voice, Israel would not obey Me."

Notice where the psalm locates the rival to God's voice. Where the Decalogue says לֹא יִהְיֶה לְךָ, "You shall not have *for* you" other gods (Exod 20:3), verse 10 says לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ קֵל זָר, "There shall not be *in* you a strange god." In the Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Nedarim* 9:1), Rabbi Yannai seized on that preposition: כָּל הַשּׁוֹמֵעַ לְיִצְרוֹ כְּאִילּוּ עוֹבֵד עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה. מַאי טַעֲמָא? לֹא יִהְיֶה בְךְ קֵל זָר וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְקֵל נֵכָר. זָר שֶׁבְּקִרְבָּךְ אַל תַּמְלִיכֵהוּ עָלֶיךָ, "Whoever listens to his impulse is as though he worshiped idols. What is the reason? 'In yourself there shall be no strange god, and you shall not bow to an alien god'—the stranger within you, do not make him king over you." Verse 13 confirms Rabbi Yannai's reading by naming the culprit inside them: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם, יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם, "So I let them go after their willful heart, that they might follow their own devices." The foreign god *in* them (בְךָ) is the stubbornness of their own heart (לִבָּם), which prefers its own plans to opening its mouth in dependence.

God's punishment in verse 13 is contained in a single verb: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ, "So I sent him away," or "I let them go." The verb שׁלח in the intensive *Piel* stem is the watchword of the Exodus—שַׁלַּח אֶת־עַמִּי, "Let My people go" (Exod 5:1)—and it is also the legal term for divorce (Deut 24:1; Jer 3:8). Here God turns the verb of liberation into the verb of abandonment. Radak states the chill of the line plainly: סלקתי שמירתי מעליהם ושמתים הפקר, "I removed My protection from upon them and left them ownerless." Divine wrath here is not a thunderbolt; it is God stepping back and letting human beings have their own way.

In the twentieth century, the psychologist Rudolf Dreikurs built an approach to parenting around this exact move. He advised parents to stop reminding a defiant child and instead allow "natural consequences" to do the teaching—provided, Dreikurs insisted, that the parent remains calm and detached while the child learns the lesson. In Psalm 81, God tries that detachment in verse 13, and cannot hold it for a single verse. In verse 14, the judge's composure breaks: לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי, יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ, "If only My people would listen to Me, if Israel would follow My paths!" The particle לוּ (*lū*, "if only") introduces an unfulfilled wish, what the Talmud in *b. Avodah Zarah* 5a calls לְשׁוֹן תַּחֲנוּנִים, "the language of supplication," when spoken by God. Yet instead of following לוּ with a past-tense verb ("If only my people *had* listened"), the poet writes the present participle שֹׁמֵעַ, "listening." As Rashi hears it, the participle keeps the door open in the present: עֲדַיִן אִם הָיוּ רוֹצִין לָשׁוּב לִי וְלִשְׁמֹעַ אֵלַי, "Still, even now, if they desired to return to Me and to hearken to Me!" Where Israel trudged away in the simple *Qal* stem in verse 13 (יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם, "they walk in their own devices"), God dreams of their stride in the intensive *Piel* stem: בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ, "in My paths they would walk."

### Honey After Rebellion, and the Unanswered "You"

Verses 15–17 lay out the blessings God is holding in His hands with no one to receive them. כִּמְעַט אוֹיְבֵיהֶם אַכְנִיעַ, וְעַל צָרֵיהֶם אָשִׁיב יָדִי, "Then would I subdue their enemies at once, strike their foes again and again" (v. 15). The human hands (כַּפָּיו) freed from the brick-basket in verse 7 would not need to fight; God's own hand (יָדִי) would turn the distress (בַּצָּרָה, v. 8) back upon their distressers (צָרֵיהֶם, v. 15). In verse 16, מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳ יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ, "Those who hate the LORD would cower before Him." As Ibn Ezra notes, the verb יְכַחֲשׁוּ means to feign obedience out of terror: מרוב פחדם יכחשו שלא מרדו בך, "out of their great fear they would lie and say they never rebelled against You." Foreign enemies would at least offer YHWH lip-service, while His own people refused to listen at all. Even the sound of לוֹ (`lō`, "to Him") echoes God's sigh לוּ (`lū`, "if only") two verses above.

Then comes the final verse (v. 17): וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה, וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "He fed them the finest wheat; I sated you with honey from the rock." Every phrase here comes from Moses' farewell poem in Deuteronomy 32, with which Psalm 81 is in quiet conversation throughout. The psalm's first word of praise, הַרְנִינוּ, "Sing joyously!" (v. 2), occurs elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible only in Psalm 32:11 and at the close of the Song of Moses: הַרְנִינוּ גוֹיִם עַמּוֹ, "Sing joyously, O nations, of His people!" (Deut 32:43). And in Deuteronomy 32:13–14, Moses describes how God fed Israel from the land's abundance: וַיֵּנִקֵהוּ דְבַשׁ מִסֶּלַע וְשֶׁמֶן מֵחַלְמִישׁ צוּר... עִם־חֵלֶב כִּלְיוֹת חִטָּה וְדַם־עֵנָב תִּשְׁתֶּה־חָמֶר, "He suckled him with honey from the crag, and oil from the flinty rock... with the kidney-fat of wheat, and the blood of the grape you drank as foaming wine."

But compare the order of events in the two poems. In Deuteronomy (both in 31:20 and in 32:12–17), the sequence is tragic: first God guides Israel with no foreign god (קֵל נֵכָר, Deut 32:12) beside Him and feeds them with wheat and honey from the rock (32:13–14); then וַיִּשְׁמַן יְשֻׁרוּן וַיִּבְעָט, "Jeshurun grew fat and kicked" (Deut 32:15), turning to strange gods (Deut 32:16–17); and finally God pronounces judgment. Satiety comes *before* rebellion and causes it. Psalm 81 runs that film in reverse. Here the foreign god (קֵל נֵכָר, v. 10), the refusal to listen (v. 12), and the stubborn heart (v. 13) have *already* happened. Only after the rebellion does the poem arrive at the finest wheat and honey from the rock, stopping mid-verse in Deuteronomy 32:14 right before the heady wine and right before Jeshurun kicks. Even the test at the waters of Meribah in verse 8 (אֶבְחׇנְךָ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה סֶלָה, "I tested you at the waters of Meribah. Selah") is redeemed in verse 17: the צוּר, "rock," of wilderness strife becomes a rock dripping with דְּבַשׁ, "honey." What Deuteronomy remembered as the fullness that spoiled Israel, Psalm 81 holds out as the grace still waiting after Israel has failed.

That explains the sudden grammatical swerve on the last word of the poem. Since verse 12, God has been speaking *about* Israel in the third person, as someone who has left the room: "My people would not listen... I let *him* go... I would subdue *their* enemies... and He would feed *him* (וַיַּאֲכִילֵ**הוּ**) from the finest wheat." Both the Septuagint and the Roman Catholic Introit for Corpus Christi (*Cibavit eos ex adipe frumenti*) keep all of verse 17 in the third-person past tense: "He fed them... and sated them." Modern editors regularly propose emending the Hebrew so that the two halves of the verse match. To "correct" that mismatch is to ruin the poem's ending. In the first half of verse 17, God is still speaking wistfully about Israel in the third person; then, in the final three words—וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶ**ךָ**, "and from the rock with honey I would sate **you**"—He turns and looks straight at the worshiper standing in the courtyard.

The loud plural "we" who began the psalm shouting about "our strength" and "our feast day" has been whittled down to a single, silent "you" (**־ךָ**). The psalm records no congregational response, no closing hymn, no promise to do better. It cannot, because the entire poem has shown that Israel's songs are too easily used to drown God out. The offer of verse 11—הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "Open your mouth wide and I will fill it"—and the rock-honey of verse 17 are left hanging in the festival air, where the only way to accept them is to stop singing and listen.

#### Full psalm

Psalm 81 is the Song of the Day for Thursday at the close of weekday morning prayer (Shacharit) across Nusach Ashkenaz, Nusach Sefard (the Hasidic rite), Chabad, and Edot HaMizrach, following Mishnah *Tamid* 7:4: בַּחֲמִישִׁי הָיוּ אוֹמְרִים, הַרְנִינוּ לֵאלֹקִים עוּזֵּנוּ, הָרִיעוּ לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "On the fifth day they would say: 'Sing joyously to God our strength; raise a shout for the God of Jacob'" (v. 2). The Talmud (*b. Rosh Hashanah* 31a) explains that Thursday was chosen because on the fifth day of creation God made the birds and fish to praise His name—a rationale that hears only the jubilant opening of the psalm and ignores the prophetic rebuke that occupies its remaining eleven verses. In the Edot HaMizrach machzor for Rosh Hashanah, the full psalm is also recited before the evening prayer on the eve of the festival (between the liturgical poem *Achot Ketannah* and the kabbalistic meditation הֲרֵינִי מוּכָן, *Leshem Yichud*) and, in some communities, again at Mincha, letting the festival's shofar verse (v. 4) anchor the entire chapter in the New Year liturgy.

#### Key verses

Verses 4 and 5 are the liturgical signature of Rosh Hashanah across all rites. In the Musaf Amidah of both days, they stand among the verses from the Writings in the *Shofarot* section (Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard recite vv. 4–5; Edot HaMizrach recites v. 4), set directly after Psalm 98:6: וְנֶאֱמַר, בַּחֲצוֹצְרוֹת וְקוֹל שׁוֹפָר הָרִיעוּ לִפְנֵי הַמֶּלֶךְ ה׳. וְנֶאֱמַר, תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר, בַּכֶּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ. כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא, מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "And it is said: 'With trumpets and the sound of the shofar shout before the King, the LORD.' And it is said: 'Blow the shofar at the new moon, at the covered moon for our feast day; for it is a statute for Israel, a judgment of the God of Jacob.'" Psalm 81 itself never uses the word מֶלֶךְ, "king," so pairing it with Psalm 98:6 supplies the royal coronation theme that Rosh Hashanah requires. The same two verses follow *Hashkiveinu* before the evening Amidah on Rosh Hashanah (Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard), stand in the daytime Kiddush for the festival (Nusach Sefard), and are recited before the sounding of the shofar (Edot HaMizrach) and throughout the festival piyyutim and selichot.

Verse 5 is also woven directly into the prose opening of the *Zikhronot* section of the Rosh Hashanah Musaf Amidah in all three rites: זֶה הַיּוֹם תְּחִלַּת מַעֲשֶׂיךָ, זִכָּרוֹן לְיוֹם רִאשׁוֹן. כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא, מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב. וְעַל הַמְּדִינוֹת בּוֹ יֵאָמֵר אֵי זוֹ לַחֶרֶב וְאֵי זוֹ לַשָּׁלוֹם, "This day is the beginning of Your works, a remembrance of the first day. 'For it is a statute for Israel, a judgment of the God of Jacob.' And concerning the countries it is pronounced on it which for the sword and which for peace." What in the psalm's plain sense was a liturgical ordinance (מִשְׁפָּט, "ruling") for keeping the feast becomes, in the prayer book, the cosmic courtroom of Judgment Day.

Finally, v. 11 appears daily and on Shabbat across all rites in the anthology of verses following *Hodu* in *Pesukei Dezimra*: קוּמָה עֶזְרָתָה לָּנוּ וּפְדֵנוּ לְמַעַן חַסְדֶּךָ. אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "'Arise, help us, and redeem us for Your kindness' sake.' 'I am the LORD your God who brought you up from the land of Egypt; open your mouth wide and I will fill it.'" Placed immediately after the plea of Psalm 44:27, God's admonition in our psalm is repurposed as a reassuring divine answer to the morning petitioner's prayer.
