**Verse 1**

לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־הַגִּתִּית, לְאָסָף.

> For the leader; upon the gittith. Of Asaph.

Only three psalms in the Psalter bear the heading עַל־הַגִּתִּית, "upon the gittith": Psalms 8, 81, and 84. Ancient and medieval readers split over whether the word names a place of origin or a season of the agricultural year. Rashi (כְּלִי שִׁיר הַבָּא מִגַּת, "a musical instrument that comes from Gath") and the Aramaic Targum (עַל כִּנָרָא דְאָתְיָא מִן גַת, "upon the lyre that comes from Gath") derive it from the Philistine city of Gath, where David lived as a mercenary and from which Obed-edom the Gittite, in whose house the Ark rested for three months (2 Samuel 6:10–11), came up to serve as a Levitical musician. The Septuagint, however, reads גִּתִּית from גַּת, "winepress," and translates ὑπὲρ τῶν ληνῶν (*hyper tōn lēnōn*), "for the winepresses" (followed by Midrash Tehillim 84:1, which links all three Gittith psalms to the eschatological winepress of Isaiah 63:6, וְאָבוּס עַמִּים בְּאַפִּי, "I trampled peoples in My anger"). That vintage reading fits an autumn pilgrimage psalm that ends with wheat and honey (v. 17), and a related image appears in Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos*, where the press serves as a figure of the Church: grapes or olives hang freely, but when put into the press and crushed, they do not perish in this world, but flow into God's storehouses. Read alongside its two sister Gittith psalms, Psalm 81 shares an unexpected bodily motif with Psalm 8: there, God establishes strength מִפִּי עוֹלְלִים וְיֹנְקִים, "from the mouth of infants and nursing babes" (Psalm 8:3); here, God asks grown worshippers to recover that nursing posture: הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "open wide your mouth and I will fill it" (v. 11).

The other two words of the heading quietly prepare the shock of verse 6c. Where the Masoretic Text vocalizes לַמְנַצֵּחַ as a Piel participle, "for the choirmaster," the Septuagint reads the root נצח in its noun sense of "perpetuity" or "end" and renders Εἰς τὸ τέλος (*Eis to telos*), "unto the end"—a phrase that gives the psalm's unresolved closing verse an eschatological cast. And the attribution לְאָסָף, "of Asaph," places the poem in the guild of Levitical musicians whom 1 Chronicles 25:1–2 describes as הַנִּבְּאִים בְּכִנֹּרוֹת, "those who prophesied with lyres." In an Asaphite psalm (compare Psalms 50, 78, and 82), the choir leader is also a cultic prophet; when the lyre stops in verse 6 to let an unfamiliar voice speak, the singer is fulfilling his guild's double office.

**Verses 2–3**

הַרְנִינוּ לֵאלֹקִים עוּזֵּנוּ; הָרִיעוּ לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב.

שְׂאוּ־זִמְרָה וּתְנוּ־תֹף; כִּנּוֹר נָעִים עִם־נָבֶל.

> Sing joyously to God, our strength; raise a shout to the God of Jacob.

> Lift up a melody and sound the timbrel, the sweet lyre with the harp.

The introduction traced how these opening calls borrow the timbrel, song, and strength of the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15:2, 20) and stake the congregation's claim with the suffix **נוּ**־ (*-nū*, "our") in עוּזֵּ**נוּ**, "our strength." Look closer at the verbs in verse 3 and the syntax is strangely compressed. Neither noun is paired with its standard verb: Hebrew normally says "lift up a voice" (נָשָׂא קוֹל), not שְׂאוּ־זִמְרָה, "lift up a melody," and "take a timbrel" (לָקַח תֹּף), not וּתְנוּ־תֹף, "give [sound to] the timbrel." Then the second half of the verse—כִּנּוֹר נָעִים עִם־נָבֶל, "the sweet lyre with the harp"—has no verb at all; as Ibn Ezra notes (ומלת תנו – תשרת בעבור אחרת, "the word 'give' serves for the other clause as well"), the single verb תְּנוּ has to do double duty for percussion and strings alike. In the Talmud (*b. Arakhin* 11a), a *tanna* seizes on the odd phrase שְׂאוּ־זִמְרָה ("lift/carry a melody") to prove that the Levites' song in the Temple was mandated by the Torah, citing Numbers 7:9, where the Kohathites receive no wagons because בַּכָּתֵף יִשָּׂאוּ, "upon the shoulder they shall lift: אֵין יִשָּׂאוּ אֶלָּא לְשׁוֹן שִׁירָה וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר שְׂאוּ זִמְרָה וּתְנוּ תֹף, 'They shall lift' is nothing other than an expression of song, and so it says: 'Lift up a melody and sound the timbrel.'" Without intending to, the midrash puts its finger on a thread inside Psalm 81 itself: the verb "lift" in verse 3 anticipates the load lifted off Israel's shoulder in verse 7 (הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ, "I removed his shoulder from the burden").

Why does the poet pair boisterous instruments—the shouting of הַרְנִינוּ and the loud frame-drum of the תֹּף—with the quiet, intimate כִּנּוֹר נָעִים, "sweet lyre" (recalling David's title in 2 Samuel 23:1, נְעִים זְמִרוֹת יִשְׂרָאֵל, "the sweet singer of Israel")? Moshe Alshich (*Romemot El*) notices that verse 2 addresses two different spiritual ranks under two different divine names: those who are genuinely righteous can face אֱלֹקִים, "God" (the attribute of strict justice), and sing out at full volume (הַרְנִינוּ) over the loud timbrel (הַתֹּף קוֹלוֹ גָבוֹהַּ, "the timbrel whose voice is high"); those who have no merit of their own and can claim only their ancestry in "the God of Jacob" (לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב) dare not sing loudly, so they are told הָרִיעוּ וְהִכָּנְעוּ, "sound the broken alarm and humble yourselves," and accompany themselves on הַכִּנּוֹר שֶׁקּוֹלוֹ נָמוּךְ, "the lyre whose voice is low."

That ambiguity in הָרִיעוּ—is it a victory acclamation or a broken cry of alarm?—shaped the afterlife of these verses across both Jewish and Christian history. In the Talmud (*b. Rosh Hashanah* 33b–34a), the sages define the Torah's יוֹם תְּרוּעָה, "day of shouting" (the noun from הָרִיעוּ), through the Aramaic Targum's יוֹם יְבָבָא, "day of wailing," connecting it to the mother of Sisera peering through her window and sobbing (וַתְּיַבֵּב, Judges 5:28); when the rabbis could not decide whether *yevava* meant long groans (*shevarim*) or short, staccato sobs (*teru'ah*), Rabbi Abbahu in Caesarea enacted that the shofar should sound both. In actual synagogue practice, the festival "shout" of verse 2 became the sound of a mother weeping—the very turn from jubilation to grief that the psalm itself makes between verses 2 and 14. In the sixteenth century, these same two verses stood at the center of opposite Christian polemics: in 1557, John Calvin pointed to the timbrel, lyre, and harp of verse 3 in his *Commentary on the Psalms* to ban musical instruments from Reformed worship as *paedagogia puerilis*, "childish pedagogies" suited only to the Old Testament's infancy, while in Elizabethan England William Byrd set verses 2–5 for six voices in *Sing joyfully unto God our strength*, and in May 1941, in Nolzay, Francis Poulenc set the Latin of verses 2–5 (*Exultate Deo*, FP 109) as a burst of liturgical defiance.

**Verse 4**

תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר; בַּכֵּסֶה, לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ.

> Blow the shofar on the new moon, on the full moon for our feast day.

Everything in the calendar puzzle of Psalm 81 hangs on the rare word בַּכֵּסֶה. Its only biblical companion is Proverbs 7:20, where the adulterous wife assures her lover that her husband has gone on a long journey with a bag of money and לְיוֹם הַכֵּסֶא יָבֹא בֵיתוֹ, "will come home only on the day of the *kese*" (quoted and glossed as an appointed time in *b. Sanhedrin* 96b, as it is by Rashi and Ibn Ezra on the psalm). Ancient translators were visibly unsure how to render it: the Septuagint guessed ἐν εὐσήμῳ ἡμέρᾳ (*en eusēmō hēmera*), "on a well-marked day," while the Syriac Peshitta read *b-kenseh*, "at the assembly." Modern comparative philology connects כֵּסֶה to Akkadian *kusē'u agū* ("headdress of the moon god at the time of the full moon") and to Syriac and Phoenician *ks'*, "full moon." On that reading, the two halves of verse 4 are not synonymous; they bracket the entire two-week autumn festival season of Tishri, from the crescent new moon (בַּחֹדֶשׁ) on the first day of the month to the full moon (בַּכֵּסֶה) on the fifteenth, when the pilgrimage feast of Sukkot—called in 1 Kings 8:2 simply הֶחָג, "the Feast," here חַגֵּנוּ, "our feast"—begins.

In physical oceanography, those two points of the lunar cycle—the dark new moon and the bright full moon—are the two moments each month when sun, earth, and moon line up in what astronomers call syzygy. Because the gravitational pulls of sun and moon reinforce each other along a single axis at both extremes, the oceans experience their highest-ranging "spring tides" twice a month, at the new moon and at the full moon. Verse 4 stations the blast of the shofar at the two lunar alignments when the pull on the earth's waters is at its maximum.

Rabbinic tradition, however, noticed that the Masoretic Text spells בַּכֵּסֶה here with a final *heh* (ה) rather than the *aleph* (א) of Proverbs 7:20 (a unique spelling flagged by Minchat Shai: המסורת עליו לית וכתיב ה"א, "the Masorah marks it as unique and written with *heh*"), and read it from the Hebrew verb כסה, "to cover" or "conceal." In *b. Beitzah* 16a and *b. Rosh Hashanah* 8b, Rabbi Abbahu asks: אֵיזֶהוּ חַג שֶׁהַחֹדֶשׁ מִתְכַּסֶּה בּוֹ הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר זֶה רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה, "Which is the festival on which the new moon is concealed? You must say that this is Rosh Hashanah"—the only biblical festival that falls at the start of the month, when the moon is dark. Read this way, verse 4 initiates a three-step sequence of hiddenness that runs across the middle of the psalm: a covered moon (בַּכֵּסֶה, v. 4), an unfamiliar tongue (שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי, v. 6c), and a God who answers from "the hiding-place of thunder" (בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, v. 8). Malbim builds his entire commentary on verses 6–8 around this hiddenness, and in Edot HaMizrach synagogues the rabbinic reading is still enacted on the Shabbat before Rosh Hashanah: every other month of the year is announced and blessed in advance, חוּץ מֵרֹאשׁ חֹדֶשׁ תִּשְׁרֵי... דִּכְתִיב בַּכֵּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ, "except for the New Moon of Tishri... as it is written: 'at the covered moon for our feast-day.'"

Notice one conspicuous absence in these opening verses: Psalm 81 has all the musical apparatus of an enthronement psalm—sharing five distinctive roots (זמרה, שופר, רוע, רנן, קול) with Psalm 98—yet the word מֶלֶךְ, "king," never appears in it. Its shofar is not a coronation fanfare; it is the horn of Sinai. Later Jewish tradition supplied the missing king in two ways. Commentators such as the *Meshekh Chokhmah* read בַּכֵּסֶה through its homophone כִּסֵּא, "throne," as the day God ascends the throne of judgment. And in the *Shofarot* section of the Rosh Hashanah Musaf Amidah, the liturgy sets Psalm 98:6 ("With trumpets and the sound of the shofar, shout before the King, the LORD") directly before Psalm 81:4, while introducing the entire scriptural sequence with the ancient liturgical poem *Atah Nigleita* (from the Amoraic *Tekiata de-Vei Rav*), which explicitly identifies the synagogue's shofar with the thunder and horn of Sinai:

> אַתָּה נִגְלֵיתָ בַּעֲנַן כְּבוֹדֶךָ עַל עַם קָדְשְׁךָ, לְדַבֵּר עִמָּם,

> מִן הַשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּם קוֹלֶךָ, וְנִגְלֵיתָ עֲלֵיהֶם בְּעַרְפִלֵּי טֹהַר.

> גַּם כָּל הָעוֹלָם כֻּלּוֹ חָל מִפָּנֶיךָ, וּבְרִיּוֹת בְּרֵאשִׁית חָרְדוּ מִמֶּךָּ,

> בְּהִגָּלוֹתְךָ מַלְכֵּנוּ עַל הַר סִינַי, לְלַמֵּד לְעַמְּךָ תּוֹרָה וּמִצְווֹת.

> וַתַּשְׁמִיעֵם אֶת הוֹד קוֹלֶךָ, וְדַבְּרוֹת קָדְשְׁךָ מִלַּהֲבוֹת אֵשׁ,

> בְּקוֹלוֹת וּבְרָקִים עֲלֵיהֶם נִגְלֵיתָ, וּבְקוֹל שׁוֹפָר עֲלֵיהֶם הוֹפָעְתָּ.

>

> You were revealed in the cloud of Your glory upon Your holy people, to speak with them;

> from the heavens You made them hear Your voice, and were revealed to them in thick clouds of purity.

> The whole world trembled before You, and the creatures of creation quaked at You,

> when You were revealed, our King, upon Mount Sinai, to teach Your people Torah and commandments.

> You made them hear the majesty of Your voice, and Your holy words out of flames of fire;

> in thunder and lightning You were revealed to them, and with the sound of the shofar You shone forth upon them.

**Verses 5–6**

כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא; מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב.

עֵדוּת בִּיהוֹסֵף שָׂמוֹ, בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם; שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע.

> For it is a statute for Israel, a ruling of the God of Jacob.

> He placed it as a testimony in Jehoseph, when He went out over the land of Egypt; a speech I did not know I hear.

Three legal nouns march across verses 5–6a—חֹק, "statute, מִשְׁפָּט, ruling," and עֵדוּת, "testimony" (the Deuteronomic triad of Deuteronomy 4:45, הָעֵדֹת וְהַחֻקִּים וְהַמִּשְׁפָּטִים אֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר מֹשֶׁה אֶל־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּצֵאתָם מִמִּצְרָיִם, "the testimonies, the statutes, and the rulings that Moses spoke to the Israelites when they went out from Egypt")—paired with three ancestral names: יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Israel, אֱלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, the God of Jacob" (closing v. 5 with the exact phrase that closed v. 2), and בִּיהוֹסֵף, "in Jehoseph." Both Meiri and Malbim use the pairing of חֹק and מִשְׁפָּט to explain the two sides of a divine command: in human eyes, blowing the horn on New Year's Day is a חֹק, an inscrutable decree whose reason is hidden from the congregation; in the eyes of the God of Jacob, it is a מִשְׁפָּט, a rational judgment whose inner logic He knows. The Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Rosh Hashanah* 1:3), however, reads the preposition לְ ("for/to") in the two halves of verse 5 with breathtaking legal audacity: אִם אֵינוֹ חוֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל כִּבְיָכוֹל אֵינוֹ מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "If it is not a statute for Israel, then—so to speak—it is not a judgment for the God of Jacob." Because the calendar depends on the human court (*Beit Din*) declaring the new moon, if the earthly judges decide to postpone Rosh Hashanah to the next day, God commands the angels to take down the heavenly tribunal: הַעֲבִירוּ בִימָה לְמָחָר יַעַבְרוּ סֻנֵיגוֹרִין יַעַבְרוּ קַטֵּיגוֹרִין שֶׁנִּמְלְכוּ בָנַיי לְעָבְרָהּ לְמָחָר, "Remove the dais until tomorrow; let the defending angels depart, let the prosecuting angels depart, for My children have decided to postpone it until tomorrow."

Why does verse 6 shift from Jacob/Israel to "Jehoseph" (יְהוֹסֵף)—spelled here, and nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible (though confirmed by the Dead Sea Scroll 11QPs^d), with an extra *heh* that embeds the divine syllable יְהוֹ־ (*Yeho-*) inside Joseph's name? And why does it say בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "when he went out *over* [or *against*] the land of Egypt," using עַל, "over," rather than מִן, "from"? Because the poet is superimposing two stories at once. On the national level, as Radak argues (כשיצא על ארץ מצרים לגאלינו, "when He went forth against the land of Egypt to redeem us"), "Joseph" is a name for all Israel (compare Psalms 77:16; 80:2), and God is the one who went out over Egypt on the night of the Exodus (Exodus 11:4). On the biographical level, however, בְּצֵאתוֹ עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם is a close verbal echo of Genesis 41:45, וַיֵּצֵא יוֹסֵף עַל־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "and Joseph went out over the land of Egypt"—the day Joseph was brought out of Pharaoh's dungeon to tour Egypt as vizier (and went on in Genesis 47:26 to set a חֹק, "statute," over the land of Egypt). From the juxtaposition of the festival and Joseph in Psalm 81:4–6 the Talmud (*b. Rosh Hashanah* 11a–b) derived that Joseph was freed from prison on Rosh Hashanah, and in *b. Sotah* 10b Rav Chanin bar Bizna, citing Rabbi Shimon Hasida, explained the extra letter *heh* in יְהוֹסֵף: יוֹסֵף שֶׁקִּדֵּשׁ שֵׁם שָׁמַיִם בַּסֵּתֶר זָכָה וְהוֹסִיפוּ לוֹ אוֹת אַחַת מִשְּׁמוֹ שֶׁל הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא, "Joseph, who sanctified the Name of Heaven in secret [by refusing Potiphar's wife], merited to have one letter from the Name of the Holy One, blessed be He, added to his name." Notice that word בַּסֵּתֶר, "in secret": Joseph's fidelity in secret is answered two verses later when God answers his descendants בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "in the secret place of thunder" (v. 8).

The introduction showed how the four words of verse 6c—שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "a speech I did not know I hear"—act as the hinge of the entire psalm, where the prophet-singer hears God's voice breaking across the festival choir. Other readers heard different voices in that line. Meiri offers a striking rationalist reading: when God overturned nature in Egypt, every philosophical observer had to confess, מַה שֶּׁלֹּא נוֹדַע לִי בְּעִיּוּן שְׁמַעְתִּיו וְקִיַּמְתִּיו וְהֶאֱמַנְתִּיו מִצַּד הַקַּבָּלָה, "what I could not know through rational inquiry, I heard, upheld, and believed by received tradition." *Exodus Rabbah* 5:9 imagines the Sinai voice in the opposite way: אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן הָיָה הַקּוֹל יוֹצֵא וְנֶחְלָק לְשִׁבְעִים קוֹלוֹת לְשִׁבְעִים לָשׁוֹן, "Rabbi Yochanan said: The voice went forth and divided into seventy voices, into seventy languages," and later in the same passage the midrash adds: הַזְּקֵנִים לְפִי כֹחָן הַבַּחוּרִים לְפִי כֹחָן וְהַקְּטַנִּים לְפִי כֹחָן וְהַיּוֹנְקִים לְפִי כֹחָן, "the elders according to their strength, the young men according to their strength, the children according to their strength, and the nursing infants according to their strength." Where the midrash makes the divine voice effortlessly familiar to every ear, Psalm 81 insists on the jolt of its unfamiliarity.

Two parallels—one from fourth-century autobiography, one from twentieth-century developmental linguistics—illuminate why that jolt matters. In Book 8 of his *Confessions* (8.12.29), Augustine sits weeping in a garden in Milan, torn between his old life and conversion, when a voice breaks in:

> So was I speaking and weeping in the most bitter contrition of my heart, when, lo! I heard from a neighbouring house a voice, as of boy or girl, I know not, chanting, and oft repeating, "Take up and read; Take up and read." Immediately my countenance changed, and I began to think most intently whether children were accustomed in any kind of game to sing such a phrase; nor could I remember ever having heard the like anywhere. Checking the torrent of my tears, I rose up, interpreting it in no other way than as a divine command to me to open the book and read the first chapter on which my eyes should fall.

Augustine's turning point depends on the fact that the chant is *not* a familiar tune from a game he knows; its strangeness is what tells him it comes from outside himself, and unlike the Israel of verse 12, he opens and obeys at once. Why, then, does Israel not hear when God speaks in verses 9–11? In 1984, the psychologists Janet F. Werker and Richard C. Tees published a study in cross-language speech perception showing that six-to-eight-month-old infants raised in English-speaking homes can effortlessly discriminate consonant contrasts from languages they have never heard—such as retroflex versus dental stops in Hindi, or glottalized velar versus uvular ejectives in Thompson (Interior Salish)—just as well as native adult speakers of those languages. By ten to twelve months of age, however, infants' auditory cortex has reorganized around the familiar phonetic categories of their own household, and they become functionally deaf to the foreign contrasts they could hear four months earlier. Verse 6c ("a speech I did not know I hear") and verse 12 ("My people would not listen to My voice") trace that perceptual narrowing in the spiritual life: a community whose ears are tuned exclusively to its own familiar noise loses the capacity to register the voice of another.

**Verse 7**

הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ; כַּפָּיו מִדּוּד תַּעֲבֹרְנָה.

> I removed his shoulder from the burden; his palms passed away from the basket.

While the Septuagint delays God's first-person voice for another verse by translating הֲסִירוֹתִי in the third person—Ἀπέστησεν (*Apestēsen*), "He removed"—the Hebrew opens the divine oracle immediately with "I removed." The introduction noted the terms סֵבֶל, "burden," and דּוּד, "basket" (which Rashi takes as a slave's cooking cauldron, קְדֵרָה, citing 1 Samuel 2:14, while Ibn Ezra pictures the hands ceasing to make clay and bricks and carry them in the vessel), as well as the one-letter split between MT תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "passed away," and the Septuagint's ἐδούλευσαν (*edouleusan*, reading תַּעֲבֹדְנָה, "served"). Notice that the same ר/ד (*resh*/*dalet*) variant between עבר, "pass," and עבד, "enslave," occurs in the Joseph story to which verses 5–6 just alluded: at Genesis 47:21, where Joseph reorganizes the Egyptians under Pharaoh's statute, the Masoretic Text reads הֶעֱבִיר אֹתוֹ לֶעָרִים, "he moved them to the cities," while the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Septuagint read הֶעֱבִיד אֹתוֹ לַעֲבָדִים, "he enslaved them as slaves." Where Joseph in Egypt administered a statute of servitude, God's statute in "Jehoseph" (v. 6) is a release from servitude.

Placed immediately after בִּיהוֹסֵף, "in Jehoseph," the word שִׁכְמוֹ, "his shoulder," carries a geographical pun as well: שְׁכֶם, *Shekhem*, the northern covenant capital that Jacob gave to Joseph with a play on the same word (שְׁכֶם אַחַד עַל־אַחֶיךָ, "one mountain-shoulder [or: Shechem] above your brothers," Genesis 48:22), where Joseph's bones were buried after the Exodus (Joshua 24:32), and where Joshua gathered the tribes to put away foreign gods (אֱלֹקֵי הַנֵּכָר, Joshua 24:20, 23; compare לְקֵל נֵכָר in v. 10). And as Zephaniah 3:9 shows—אֶהְפֹּךְ אֶל־עַמִּים שָׂפָה בְרוּרָה... לְעָבְדוֹ שְׁכֶם אֶחָד, "I will change the peoples to a pure speech... to serve Him with one shoulder"—"speech" (שָׂפָה, v. 6c) and "shoulder" (שֶׁכֶם, v. 7) belong together as an idiom for changing masters.

The physical toll of that shoulder-load is no mere metaphor. Bioarchaeological analysis by Jerome Rose, Barry Kemp, and their colleagues of the skeletons excavated from the South Tombs Cemetery at Amarna—the fourteenth-century BCE Egyptian capital built by conscripted laborers under Akhenaten—found high rates of vertebral compression fractures, Schmorl's nodes (where spinal discs herniate into the bone under heavy axial loads), and severe degenerative joint disease in the shoulders and hands. When verse 7 speaks of the shoulder relieved from the סֵבֶל and the palms freed from the דּוּד, it is speaking the language of crushed vertebrae and calloused hands—hands that God frees here so that, in verse 15, His own hand (יָדִי) can do the fighting for them.

**Verse 8**

בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ וָאֲחַלְּצֶךָּ; אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם; אֶבְחׇנְךָ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה, סֶלָה.

> In distress you called and I rescued you; I answered you in the secret place of thunder; I tested you at the waters of Meribah. Selah.

In verse 7, God spoke *about* Israel in the third person ("his shoulder," "his palms"); in verse 8, the memory of Israel's pain (בַּצָּרָה, "in distress") makes God turn and address Israel face-to-face as "you." Three first-person verbs ending in the second-person singular suffix **ךָ**־ (*-khā*, "you") hammer out a triple rhyme across the three clauses: וָאֲחַלְּצֶ**ךָּ** (`wā-'a-ḥal-lə-TSEKH-khā`, "and I rescued you"), אֶעֶנְ**ךָ** (`'e-ʿen-KHĀ`, "I answered you"), אֶבְחׇנְ**ךָ** (`'ev-ḥon-KHĀ`, "I tested you"). The progression of the three verbs is the whole wilderness story in miniature: rescue at the sea, revelation in the thunder of Sinai, and testing in the desert.

What does אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "I answered you in the secret place of thunder," mean? On the plain sense, סֵתֶר רַעַם is the dark storm-cloud at Sinai where God's presence was both concealed and heard (compare Psalm 18:12, יָשֶׁת חֹשֶׁךְ סִתְרוֹ, "He made darkness His hiding-place"). Rashi and Radak, however, offer an exquisite midrashic reading by redistributing the words between the caller and the answerer: אַתָּה קְרָאתַנִי בְּסֵתֶר בֵּינִי וּבֵינֶךָ וַאֲנִי עָנִיתִיךָ בְּקוֹל רַעַם הוֹדַעְתִּי גְּבוּרוֹת וְנוֹרָאוֹת בְּפַרְהֶסְיָא, "You called to Me in secret, between Me and you, and I answered you with a voice of thunder—I made known My mighty and awesome deeds in public." A whispered, private cry in the brick-fields of Egypt was answered with a public cosmic roar. Literature knows the reverse of that moment as well—when a voice from a hidden, impossible space answers a solitary cry of distress with a single name, as in Chapter 35 of Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre*, when Jane, on the verge of yielding to a loveless marriage, hears Rochester's voice across the moors: "I saw nothing, but I heard a voice somewhere cry—'Jane! Jane! Jane!'—nothing more."

Then comes the twist of the third clause: אֶבְחׇנְךָ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה, "I tested you at the waters of Meribah." In Exodus 17:7 and in Psalm 95:9 (נִסּוּנִי אֲבוֹתֵיכֶם בְּחָנוּנִי, "your fathers tried Me, they tested Me"), it is *Israel* who tests God at Massah and Meribah by demanding water from the rock; here, using the same verb בחן, *God* says that He tested Israel (just as אֶעֶנְךָ, "I answered you," also carries the shadow of its homonym ענה II, "afflict" or "humble," paired with testing in Deuteronomy 8:2, לְמַעַן עַנֹּתְךָ לְנַסֹּתְךָ, "in order to humble you, to test you"). Where did our poet find the idea that God was the tester at Meribah? In Moses' blessing of the Levites in Deuteronomy 33:8: וּלְלֵוִי אָמַר תֻּמֶּיךָ וְאוּרֶיךָ לְאִישׁ חֲסִידֶךָ אֲשֶׁר נִסִּיתוֹ בְּמַסָּה תְּרִיבֵהוּ עַל־מֵי מְרִיבָה, "And of Levi he said: Let Your Thummim and Urim be with Your faithful one, whom You tested at Massah, with whom You contended at the waters of Meribah." The Levitical singers of the guild of Asaph read the nation's crisis at the rock through their own tribe's founding test. And right after מֵי מְרִיבָה falls the psalm's only סֶלָה, "Selah": a musical pause that lets the name מְרִיבָה—from רִיב, "contention" or "covenant lawsuit"—echo in the silence before God opens His case in the next verse, and prepares for verse 17, where the rock of Meribah will return dripping with honey.

**Verses 9–10**

שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ; יִשְׂרָאֵל, אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי.

לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ קֵל זָר; וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְקֵל נֵכָר.

> Hear, My people, and I will admonish you; O Israel, if you would but listen to Me!

> There shall be no strange god in you, and you shall not bow down to a foreign god.

Verse 9 is the twin of another Asaphite covenant lawsuit, Psalm 50:7: שִׁמְעָה עַמִּי וַאֲדַבֵּרָה יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, "Hear, My people, and I will speak; O Israel, and I will testify against you" (so close that the Septuagint actually splices καὶ λαλήσω σοι, *kai lalēsō soi*, "and I will speak to you," into our verse to harmonize the two). By saying וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, "and I will admonish [or: call witness against] you," God turns the noun עֵדוּת, "testimony," of verse 6 on its head: the festival statute that Israel gathered to celebrate is now the legal warning invoked against them. Meiri hears a quiet progression between the two appearances of שׁמע in verse 9: the imperative שְׁמַע עַמִּי, "Hear, My people," asks for שְׁמִיעַת הָאֹזֶן, "the hearing of the ear," while the broken conditional יִשְׂרָאֵל אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי, "O Israel, if you would listen to Me," asks for קַבָּלַת דְּבָרִים, "the inward acceptance of the words."

In the Hebrew, that conditional clause אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי breaks off without a "then..." clause, leaving the wish hanging in mid-air before the prohibition of verse 10 begins. The Septuagint, however, refuses the pause and reads verses 9c and 10 as a single conditional sentence: ἐὰν ἀκούσῃς μου, οὐκ ἔσται ἐν σοὶ θεὸς πρόσφατος (*ean akousēs mou, ouk estai en soi theos prosphatos*), "If you listen to Me, there *will* not be in you a newly arrived god." On the Greek reading, verse 10 is not a second command piled on top of "listen"; it is the promised result of listening—hear God's voice, and no counterfeit god will have room inside you. Notice also the Greek translator's striking word for זָר, "strange": πρόσφατος (*prosphatos*), "newly arrived" or "freshly made," borrowed straight from the Song of Moses, Deuteronomy 32:17, חֲדָשִׁים מִקָּרֹב בָּאוּ, "new gods that came recently." And the singular phrase קֵל נֵכָר, "a foreign god" (as opposed to the usual plural אֱלֹקֵי נֵכָר), occurs in the Hebrew Bible only here, in Malachi 2:11, and in Deuteronomy 32:12 (וְאֵין עִמּוֹ קֵל נֵכָר, "and there was no foreign god with Him"), tightening the thread between our psalm and Moses' Song.

A seventh-century retelling of the Sinai covenant in the *Qur'an* (Surat al-Baqarah 2:93) brings together the very elements that verses 9–11 hold in tension—the command to hear, the idol inside the body (בְךָ, "in you"), and the open mouth:

> And [recall] when We took your covenant and raised over you the mount, [saying], "Take what We have given you with determination and listen." They said [instead], "We hear and disobey." And their hearts absorbed [the worship of] the calf because of their disbelief. Say, "How wretched is that which your faith enjoins upon you, if you should be believers."

Where Psalm 81 warns against letting a strange god lodge "in you" (v. 10) and immediately invites Israel to open wide its mouth to be filled by the LORD (v. 11), the Qur'anic verse fuses the refusal to listen with a dark inversion of the open mouth: a people whose hearts "drink in" the golden calf.

**Verse 11**

אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ, הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם; הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ.

> I am the LORD your God, who brought you up from the land of Egypt; open wide your mouth and I will fill it.

The introduction showed how verse 11 reverses the order of Exodus 20:2–3 so that the First Commandment climaxes not in a prohibition, but in a feeding—and how the single causative imperative הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ (`har-ḥev-PIY-khā`, "widen your mouth") answers the choir's opening causative imperatives הַרְנִינוּ (`har-NIY-nū`) and הָרִיעוּ (`hā-RIY-ʿū`, v. 2). In the natural world, the image is that of altricial nestlings—newly hatched songbirds that are blind and too weak to stand, whose entire repertoire of survival consists in stretching their beaks open as wide as their skulls allow, exposing a brightly colored interior that triggers the parent bird to drop food into the gap.

Rabbinic readers heard in הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ an invitation to shameless audacity in both prayer and study. In the Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Ta'anit* 3:6), Rebbi Shimon ben Lakish says: אִם סִדַּרְתָּה תְּפִילָּה לֹא תְּהֵא מֵיצַר פִּיךָ אֶלָּא הַרְחֶב פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "If you have marshaled your prayer, do not keep your mouth narrow [מֵיצַר, playing on צָרָה, 'distress/narrowness,' in v. 8]; rather, 'open wide your mouth and I will fill it.'" In the Babylonian Talmud (*b. Berakhot* 50a), when Abaye asks whether a person is really permitted to demand the full measure of God's bounty, citing our verse, the Gemara replies: הַהוּא בְּדִבְרֵי תוֹרָה כְּתִיב, "That verse is written with regard to words of Torah" (a reading built into the Aramaic Targum: אַפְתֵּי פוּמָךְ בְּפִתְגָמֵי אוֹרַיְתָא וַאֲמַלֵי יָתָהּ מִכָּל טָבְתָא, "Open wide your mouth with the words of the Torah, and I will fill it with every good thing"). Alshich (*Romemot El*) goes even further, reading the line through the midrash (*Shir HaShirim Rabbah* 1:2) in which each of the Ten Commandments at Sinai approached each Israelite in person, asked whether he accepted it, and—whenever the Israelite opened his mouth to say "Yes"—kissed him on the mouth, filling his breath with holiness.

Two analogies from distant fields—one from metallurgy, one from the history of contract law—capture the mechanics of this promise. In lost-wax bronze casting (*cire perdue*), the sculptor shapes a model in wax, encases it in clay, and bakes the mold in a kiln so that all the wax melts and drains out through the channels; the entire preparation for receiving the molten bronze consists in creating a completely empty negative space inside the clay, because wherever wax remains, bronze cannot go. And in the 1892 English Court of Appeal decision *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co*, Lord Justice Lindley and Lord Justice Bowen explained how a "unilateral contract"—an advertisement offering £100 to anyone who used the company's smoke ball as directed and still caught influenza—becomes binding without the offeree ever writing or speaking a word of acceptance: "In point of law this advertisement is an offer to pay 100l. to anybody who will perform these conditions" (Lindley LJ), and "performance of the condition is a sufficient acceptance without notification" (Bowen LJ). Verse 11 has the exact legal shape of a unilateral offer: God requires no negotiated reply, no liturgical oath, and no payment—only the act of opening the mouth. That is why, even after Israel's refusal in verse 12, the offer can remain live in verse 17 without a single word spoken by the congregation.

**Verses 12–13**

וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי; וְיִשְׂרָאֵל, לֹא־אָבָה לִי.

וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם; יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם.

> But My people did not listen to My voice, and Israel was not willing for Me.

> So I sent him away in the stubbornness of their heart; let them walk in their own counsels.

Malbim (*Beur Hamilot*) explains the pairing of the two nouns and two verbs in verse 12 with surgical precision: עַמִּי, "My people," names Israel's covenantal status as subjects of a king, under which they are obligated to obey His voice (לִשְׁמֹעַ לְקוֹלִי); יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Israel," names their inherent spiritual nobility, from which they ought to desire God (לֹא־אָבָה לִי, "was not willing for Me") out of free inward consent. Both fail, and the second failure—לֹא־אָבָה, the quiet refusal of the will—is what brings the covenant to a halt. In Herman Melville's 1853 story *Bartleby, the Scrivener*, a Wall Street law copyist paralyzes his employer's office without ever raising his voice or breaking a rule, simply by meeting every request with the words, "I would prefer not to." Verse 12b (וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה לִי) is the biblical ancestor of Bartleby's sentence: the impenetrable wall of a will that simply prefers not to.

Look at the pronouns in verse 13 and you can watch the nation fracture in real time: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵ**הוּ** בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּ**ם** יֵלְכ**וּ** בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵי**הֶם**, "So I sent **him** away in the stubbornness of **their** heart; let **them** walk in **their** own counsels." The object suffix on the verb is singular (**הוּ**־, "him"), treating Israel as a single corporate person (and forming a three-step chain across the oracle with the same `-hū` suffix: וַאֲמַלְאֵ**הוּ**, "I will fill it," v. 11 → וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵ**הוּ**, "I sent him away," v. 13 → וַיַּאֲכִילֵ**הוּ**, "He fed him," v. 17). Yet the instant God lets "him" go, the pronouns splinter into the plural: *their* heart, *they* walk, *their* counsels. United under one voice, Israel is singular; abandoned to self-direction, they dissolve into a crowd of competing private agendas.

The introduction traced how וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ, "I sent him away," turns the Exodus verb of liberation (שַׁלַּח אֶת־עַמִּי, "Let My people go") and the legal verb of divorce (Deuteronomy 24:1) into judgment by abandonment. To feel how distinctively Psalm 81 handles that abandonment, set it alongside two comparisons—one from Greek epic, one from modern child psychology. At the opening of Book 1 of Homer's *Odyssey* (lines 32–43), Zeus looks down from Olympus at the ruin of Aegisthus and absolves heaven of all responsibility:

> Mortals! They are always blaming the gods and saying that evil comes from us, when they themselves, by their own reckless folly, have sorrows beyond what is ordained. Even so now Aegisthus, beyond what was ordained, took for himself the wedded wife of the son of Atreus, and slew him on his return, though he knew of sheer destruction, since we ourselves had told him beforehand, sending Hermes, the keen-sighted slayer of Argus, that he should neither slay the man nor woo his wife.

And in *Children: The Challenge* (1964), the Adlerian psychologist Rudolf Dreikurs advised parents whose child refuses to eat at the family table to stop pleading and let natural consequences do the work: "If she refuses, the parents should maintain a friendly attitude, abstain from verbal reminders altogether, remove the unfinished food from the table when everyone is finished, and allow Alice to find out what happens." Homer's Zeus stays serenely self-vindicated on Olympus; Dreikurs's parent stays coolly detached; the God of Psalm 81 announces in verse 13 that He has taken the plate away and let Israel go—and then cannot keep up the detachment for even one verse.

**Verses 14–15**

לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי; יִשְׂרָאֵל, בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ.

כִּמְעַט אוֹיְבֵיהֶם אַכְנִיעַ; וְעַל צָרֵיהֶם אָשִׁיב יָדִי.

> If only My people were listening to Me, if Israel would walk in My ways!

> In a moment I would subdue their enemies, and against their foes I would turn My hand.

Between verse 13 and verse 14 there is no "but" or "yet"—only the raw outbreak of לוּ, "If only!" Followed by the present participle שֹׁמֵעַ, "listening," the line refuses to consign Israel's hearing to a closed past; as Rashi paraphrases, עֲדַיִן אִם הָיוּ רוֹצִין לָשׁוּב לִי וְלִשְׁמֹעַ אֵלַי, "Still now, if they desired to return to Me and to hearken to Me!" Most commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak, Meiri, and the Septuagint's ἐν τῷ μηδενί, *en tō mēdeni*, "in nothing / in an instant") take the opening word of verse 15, כִּמְעַט, as measuring how fast God would act: "In a moment I would subdue their enemies." Malbim, however, hears כִּמְעַט pulling backward to measure how little obedience God is asking for: תֵּיכֶף שֶׁיַּתְחִילוּ לִשְׁמֹעַ מְעַט לְדִבְרֵי ה' אָז אוֹיְבֵיהֶם אַכְנִיעַ, "The instant they would begin to listen *even a little* to the words of the LORD, I would subdue their enemies!"

Notice how verse 15 closes two circuits opened earlier in the oracle. In verse 7, God freed Israel's כַּפָּיו, "his palms," from the slave-basket; here, Israel does not even need to raise those freed hands in war, because אָשִׁיב יָדִי, "I would turn My hand," against their foes—a phrase Rashi reads with keen poignancy: אָשִׁיב מַכָּתִי מֵעֲלֵיכֶם לָתֵת אוֹתָהּ עֲלֵיהֶם, "I would turn My blow *back from upon you* to lay it upon them." And in verse 8, Israel called out בַּצָּרָה, "in distress"; here, God turns His hand against צָרֵיהֶם, "their distressers/foes" (a root link preserved by the Septuagint's ἐν θλίψει, *en thlipsei*, in v. 8 and τοὺς θλίβοντας αὐτούς, *tous thlibontas autous*, in v. 15).

This portrait of a God who grieves over His own judgment runs deep in rabbinic midrash. In *Lamentations Rabbah* (Proem 24), after the destruction of the Temple, God weeps over the banishment He Himself decreed:

> הָיָה הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא בּוֹכֶה וְאוֹמֵר אוֹי לִי מָה עָשִׂיתִי הִשְׁרֵיתִי שְׁכִינָתִי לְמַטָּה בִּשְׁבִיל יִשְׂרָאֵל וְעַכְשָׁיו שֶׁחָטְאוּ חָזַרְתִּי לִמְקוֹמִי הָרִאשׁוֹן חַס וְשָׁלוֹם שֶׁהָיִיתִי שְׂחוֹק לַגּוֹיִם וְלַעַג לַבְּרִיּוֹת.

>

> The Holy One, blessed be He, wept and said: "Woe is Me! What have I done? I caused My Shechinah to dwell below on earth for the sake of Israel; and now that they have sinned, I have returned to My former habitation. Heaven forefend that I become a laughter to the nations and a byword to human beings!"

Compare the same grammatical structure—a broken "if only" spoken over a city that refused to listen—in the *Gospel of Luke* (19:41–42), echoing Isaiah 48:18 (לוּא הִקְשַׁבְתָּ לְמִצְוֺתָי, "If only you had heeded My commandments!") and our verse: "As he approached Jerusalem and saw the city, he wept over it and said, 'If you, even you, had only known on this day what would bring you peace—but now it is hidden from your eyes.'" Notice what separates the two passages: Luke's sentence slams the door shut at the end ("but now it is hidden from your eyes"), whereas Psalm 81 leaves its לוּ open, rolling straight on into the wheat and honey of verse 17.

In modern game theory and machine learning, an algorithm developed by Martin Zinkevich and his colleagues in 2007—Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR), the mathematical engine behind superhuman poker-playing AIs—learns how to play games of incomplete information by calculating at every decision node its "counterfactual regret": the exact numerical difference between what actually happened and the payoff that *would* have been achieved had a different action been chosen at that node. Verses 14–16 are a counterfactual-regret calculation spoken aloud: had Israel chosen the branch marked שֹׁמֵעַ לִי ("listening to Me"), the payoff would have been instant victory over every enemy. The difference is that an algorithm computes counterfactual regret in order to alter its own play on the next hand, whereas God is computing the lost payoff of a move that belongs to another player—a player whose will He refuses to override.

**Verse 16**

מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳ יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ; וִיהִי עִתָּם לְעוֹלָם.

> Those who hate the LORD would cringe before Him, and their time would be forever.

Every word in verse 16 carries a double edge. Why does the speaker suddenly say מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳, "those who hate the LORD," naming God in the third person inside God's own first-person speech? On one level, "those who hate the LORD" is a fixed covenant title (compare the Second Commandment's לְשֹׂנְאַי, "to those who hate Me," in Exodus 20:5, and Deuteronomy 32:41, וְלִמְשַׂנְאַי אֲשַׁלֵּם, "and I will repay those who hate Me"): Israel's political enemies (`אוֹיְבֵיהֶם`, `צָרֵיהֶם` in v. 15) are unmasked here as haters of the LORD Himself. On another level, the third-person shift allows לוֹ (`LŌ`, "to Him" or "to him") to point in two directions at once. Rashi takes לוֹ as referring to the LORD, meaning that His enemies would lie to Him (playing by sound on verse 14's לוּ, `LŪ`, "if only"); Malbim likewise refers it to God, reading the clause of enemies who deny His existence and providence. Radak and Meiri, however, take לוֹ as referring to Israel: מֵרֹב פַּחְדָּם בִּרְאוֹתָם הַכְנָעַת עַצְמָם וְהִתְרוֹמְמוּת יִשְׂרָאֵל יְכַחֲשׁוּ וְיִכְפְּרוּ שֶׁלֹּא הֵרֵעוּ לָהֶם, "out of their great fear at seeing their own subjugation and Israel's exaltation, they will lie to Israel and deny that they ever harmed them." And Ibn Ezra notes a second meaning of the root כחש alongside "cringe/lie" (as in Deuteronomy 33:29 and Psalm 18:45): "grow lean" or "waste away," as in Psalm 109:24, וּבְשָׂרִי כָּחַשׁ מִשָּׁמֶן, "and my flesh has grown lean for lack of fatness." Read with that overtone, verses 16 and 17 form a sharp physical contrast: the haters of the LORD waste away from fatness (יְכַחֲשׁוּ), while Israel is fed מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה, "from the fat of wheat."

The second half of the verse—וִיהִי עִתָּם לְעוֹלָם, "and their time would be forever"—splits the commentators down the middle over whose "time" (עִתָּם) is meant. Rashi (פּוּרְעָנוּתָם לְעוֹלָם, "their retribution would be forever"), Ibn Ezra (כִּי בָא יוֹמוֹ וּלְעוֹלָם לֹא יָקוּמוּ, "for their day has come and they shall never rise again"), and Malbim take the pronoun "their" to refer to the nearest noun, the haters of the LORD: their "time" is their hour of doom (compare Jeremiah 27:7, עַד בֹּא־עֵת אַרְצוֹ, "until his land's time comes"). Radak and Meiri, on the other hand, read עִתָּם as Israel's enduring season of blessing—as Radak puts it, עֵת וּזְמַן בִּרְכָתָם שֶׁהֻחַל בְּטוֹב יִהְיֶה לְעוֹלָם בְּלִי הֶפְסֵק, "the time and season of their blessing, once begun in goodness, would endure forever without interruption"—and the Aramaic Targum similarly renders it וִיהֵי תּוּקְפֵיהוֹן לַעֲלָם, "and their strength shall be forever," continuing the third-person plural suffix **ם**־ (*-ām*, "their") that has referred to Israel throughout verses 13–15 (לִבָּם, מוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם, אוֹיְבֵיהֶם, צָרֵיהֶם). The ambiguity lets the two readings work as two sides of a single coin: the permanence of the enemies' defeat *is* the permanence of Israel's peace.

**Verse 17**

וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה; וּמִצּוּר, דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ.

> And He fed him from the fat of wheat; and from the rock, with honey I would sate you.

The final verse of the psalm has troubled grammarians for two thousand years. Its first half—וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה, "And He fed him from the fat of wheat"—uses a past-tense narrative verb (וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ) with a third-person subject ("He") and a third-person singular object ("him"), continuing the third-person reference to "the LORD" in verse 16; its second half—וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "and from the rock, with honey I would sate you"—suddenly switches back to a first-person imperfect verb ("I will/would sate") and a second-person singular object ("you"). Notice also the subtle sound echo binding the two halves of the promise in verses 15 and 17: the first half ended with אַכְנִ**יעַ** (`'akh-NIY-aʿ`, "I would subdue," v. 15a), and the psalm ends with אַשְׂבִּ**יעֶ**ךָ (`'as-biy-ʿE-khā`, "I would sate you," v. 17b), two first-person causative verbs built on roots ending in the guttural *ayin* (ע).

Ancient translators and modern scholars alike tried to iron out the grammatical jolt: the Septuagint flattened the final word into a third-person singular past verb with a plural object, ἐχόρτασεν αὐτούς (*echortasen autous*), "He satisfied them," while the Syriac Peshitta and many modern commentators emend the first word to וָאַאֲכִילֵהוּ, "and I would feed him." Ibn Ezra, however, defends the Masoretic text on both counts and shows what is lost when you smooth it out. First, he reads the provision as past, taking the preposition מִן ("from") comparatively—in the wilderness, God fed Israel manna finer "than the fat of wheat" and sated them with water from the rock sweeter "than honey"—and then reports another reading on which אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "I will sate you," addresses Israel about the future. Second, Ibn Ezra points out that switching mid-verse from speaking *about* Israel in the third person to speaking *to* Israel in the second person is a convention of language, citing the exact verse from the Song of Moses that our line is echoing, Deuteronomy 32:15: וַיִּשְׁמַן יְשֻׁרוּן וַיִּבְעָט שָׁמַנְתָּ עָבִיתָ כָּשִׂיתָ, "So Jeshurun grew fat and kicked—you grew fat, thick, and sleek!"

Look at the material contrast inside וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ, "and from the rock, honey." Between 2005 and 2007, excavations led by Amihai Mazar at Tel Rehov in the Beth Shean Valley uncovered a large tenth-to-ninth-century BCE industrial apiary—rows of unfired clay cylinders stacked three tiers high, holding the remains of Anatolian honeybees—demonstrating that in biblical Israel, cultivated honey was a labor-intensive commodity produced in mud-clay vessels made from the same material as the bricklayer's דּוּד, "basket/pot," of verse 7. Yet in the limestone cliffs of the Levant—and most spectacularly in the Himalayan foothills, where the giant cliff honeybee (*Apis laboriosa*) builds massive, open honeycombs suspended directly from barren granite overhangs—wild bees nest in the clefts of sheer stone, so that the rock itself appears to ooze honey. To hands that He freed from the clay brick-basket in verse 7, and to a people whom He tested at the dry flint rock (צוּר, Exodus 17:6) of Meribah in verse 8, God promises honey that requires no clay cylinders and no forced labor: honey straight from the Rock.

In 1908, the American composer Charles Ives wrote a six-minute orchestral piece titled *The Unanswered Question*. Offstage strings play slow, almost inaudible chords that never pause; against them, a solo trumpet states a short, non-tonal phrase seven times, and after the first six statements a quartet of flutes rushes in to answer, growing louder, faster, and more discordant with each attempt, until the flutes give up and the trumpet sounds its question one last time into the silence. Psalm 81 is built on the reverse of Ives's score. Here the human instruments—timbrel, lyre, harp, and shofar—make all their noise in the opening bars and fall silent at verse 6, while God's voice carries the rest of the piece alone, ending on a single word addressed to the listener—אַשְׂבִּיעֶ**ךָ**, "I would sate **you**"—with no answer written into the text.
