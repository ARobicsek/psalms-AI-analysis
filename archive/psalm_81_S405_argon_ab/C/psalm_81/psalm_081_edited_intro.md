When the synagogue and the church excerpt Psalm 81 at the height of a festival, the way each liturgy clips it tells you where the poem's tensions lie. On Rosh Hashanah, in the *Shofarot* section of the Musaf Amidah, the liturgy quotes verses 4 and 5—תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר בַּכֵּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "Blow the horn on the new moon, on the full moon for our feast day. For it is a law for Israel, a ruling of the God of Jacob"—and stops dead before verse 6. In the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church, the liturgy of Corpus Christi goes to the opposite end of the psalm, lifting verse 17 out of its context to serve as its festive Introit (*Cibavit eos*): וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "He fed him the finest wheat; I sated you with honey from the rock," turning an aching, unfulfilled offer into an accomplished sacramental fact. One tradition keeps the opening fanfare and cuts off the voice that answers it; the other keeps the final banquet and cuts off the grief that precedes it. Between those two sheared edges lies the poem itself.

Psalm 81 is a festival liturgy engineered to interrupt itself. Its seventeen verses fall into two unequal halves joined by a four-word hinge in the middle of verse 6. For the first four and a half verses (vv. 2–6b), a temple choir summons the congregation to fill the sanctuary with noise—singing, shouting, beating the timbrel, plucking lyre and harp, and blowing the ram's horn to celebrate the Exodus. Then, in the last clause of verse 6, an unnamed "I" freezes the music: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "I hear a language that I knew not." For the remaining eleven verses (vv. 7–17), the congregation never utters another word. God takes over the poem in the first person, recalling how He lifted the burden from Israel's shoulders and freed their hands from the basket (vv. 7–8), pleading with His people to listen and open their mouths to be fed (vv. 9–11), mourning their refusal and His own decision to let them go their willful way (vv. 12–13), and finally breaking down into a counterfactual cry—לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי, "If only My people would listen to Me" (v. 14)—that dangles the promise of victory and honey from the rock before a silent listener (vv. 15–17). The psalm is not against music; it is about the religious temptation to use music to drown out the one we are singing to. It stages a worship service in which the congregation's own shofar blast accidentally summons the voice of Sinai, and that voice asks for the one thing a noisy festival is least prepared to give: an ear that listens and an empty mouth waiting to be filled.

#### How the Shofar Summons the Thunder

The opening verses are built to sound as if Israel owns the party. Five plural imperatives tumble over one another in three verses—הַרְנִינוּ, "sing joyously, הָרִיעוּ, raise a shout" (v. 2), שְׂאוּ, "take up, וּתְנוּ, sound" (v. 3), תִּקְעוּ, "blow" (v. 4)—while the choir gathers the instruments of Israel's oldest liberation song. Calling God עוּזֵּנוּ, "our strength" (v. 2), raising a זִמְרָה, "song," and striking a תֹּף, "timbrel" (v. 3), replays the vocabulary of Moses and Miriam at the Sea of Reeds: עׇזִּי וְזִמְרָת יָ‑הּ, "The LORD is my strength and might" (Exodus 15:2), and וַתִּקַּח מִרְיָם הַנְּבִיאָה אֲחוֹת אַהֲרֹן אֶת־הַתֹּף, "Miriam the prophetess, Aaron's sister, took a timbrel" (Exodus 15:20). Even the grammar of possession belongs to the crowd. The first-person plural suffix **נוּ**־ (*-nū*, "our") rings out in עוּזֵּ**נוּ** (`ʿūz-ZĒ-nū`, "our strength," v. 2) and חַגֵּ**נוּ** (`ḥag-GĒ-nū`, "our feast day," v. 4), two inflected forms that occur nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. Whether בַּכֵּסֶה, "on the full moon" (v. 4), refers to the middle of the autumn month of Tishri at Sukkot—matching the Akkadian *kusē'u*, the "crowned" full moon, and the superscription's עַל־הַגִּתִּית, "on the gittith" (v. 1), which the Septuagint renders ὑπὲρ τῶν ληνῶν (*hyper tōn lēnōn*), "for the winepresses"—or whether, as Rabbi Abbahu argues in the Talmud (*b. Beitzah* 16a; cf. *b. Rosh Hashanah* 8a–b), it means the dark, "covered" moon of Rosh Hashanah on the first of Tishri (אֵיזֶהוּ חַג שֶׁהַחֹדֶשׁ מִתְכַּסֶּה בּוֹ הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר זֶה רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה, "On which festival is the new moon concealed? You must say that it is Rosh Hashanah"), the debate turns on the two lunar extremes of the autumn festival season, when the tides pull hardest.

Yet the crowd's instruments carry a trap inside them. The percussion and strings of verse 3 give way in verse 4 to the שׁוֹפָר, "horn"—and the shofar is not a parlor instrument. In Israel's memory, its blast is the acoustic signature of Mount Sinai, where וְקוֹל שֹׁפָר חָזָק מְאֹד, "a very loud blast of the horn" (Exodus 19:16), accompanied the mountain's smoke and thunder, and מֹשֶׁה יְדַבֵּר וְהָאֱלֹקִים יַעֲנֶנּוּ בְקוֹל, "Moses would speak and God would answer him in thunder" (Exodus 19:19). The psalm locks the human festival shout and the divine storm-voice together by sound: the choir's הָרִיעוּ (`hā-RIY-ʿū`, "raise a shout," v. 2) is answered in verse 8 by God's רַעַם (`RA-ʿam`, "thunder"), a word that already holds עַם (`ʿam`, "people") inside its consonants. Malbim notices the strangeness of combining temple song with the shofar at all: וְהַשּׁוֹפָר סִימַן חֲרָדָה תָּמִיד, "the shofar is always a sign of trembling," blown in times of drought or siege when the Levites' lyres fell silent. By blowing the horn at the feast, the worshippers think they are keeping a statute they control; instead, they trigger a theophany.

The turn happens in the four words of verse 6c: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "I heard a language that I knew not." Classical commentators felt the jolt of that sudden first-person verb אֶשְׁמָע, "I hear," and tried to domesticize it: the Septuagint flattened it into the third person, ἤκουσεν (*ēkousen*), "he heard," making it a historical note about hearing an unknown language when coming forth from the land of Egypt, while a rabbinic reading in *b. Sotah* 36b took it of Joseph when he left prison, where the angel Gabriel adds a letter from God's name to turn יוֹסֵף, "Joseph," into יְהוֹסֵף, "Jehoseph," so he can understand seventy languages. Look at the seam in the poem, however, and the first-person singular is the voice of the Levitical prophet-singer, stunned as a voice from beyond the liturgy breaks across the choir's song. In the Second Temple, the Levites themselves recognized this rupture in performance: according to the Jerusalem Talmud (*y. Rosh Hashanah* 4:4; cf. *b. Rosh Hashanah* 30b), when Rosh Hashanah fell on a Thursday—the weekday to which Mishnah *Tamid* 7:4 assigns Psalm 81—they sang the opening hymn הַרְנִינוּ, "Sing joyously" (v. 2), at one service and began the other straight at verse 7, הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ, "I relieved his shoulder of the burden." It is the poetic equivalent of Rainer Maria Rilke's *Archaic Torso of Apollo*, where the museum-goer stands admiring an ancient stone fragment whose head is missing, until the direction of the gaze reverses:

> We cannot fathom his mysterious head,

> Through the veiled eyes no flickering ray is sent:

> But from his torso gleaming light is shed

> As from a candelabrum; inward bent

> His glance there glows and lingers.

> ...

> Nor could this stark and stunted stone display

> Vibrance beneath the shoulders heavy bar,

> Nor shine like fur upon a beast of prey,

> Nor break forth from its lines like a great star—

> There is no spot that does not bind you fast

> And transport you back, back to a far past.

Rilke's final sentence—*denn da ist keine Stelle, die dich nicht sieht. Du mußt dein Leben ändern*, "for there is no place that does not see you. You must change your life"—is the exact motion of verses 6 to 9. You come to celebrate an ancient object of worship, and the object speaks back in the second person singular.

Once God speaks, the distribution of pronouns and divine names in the psalm changes completely. In the human hymn of verses 2–6b, the worshippers say "our" twice (עוּזֵּנוּ, v. 2; חַגֵּנוּ, v. 4), call the deity אֱלֹקִים, "God," and אֱלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "the God of Jacob" (vv. 2, 5), and never utter the personal name ה׳, "the LORD." The moment the oracle begins, "our" and "Jacob" disappear from the poem forever. God claims the first-person possessive—עַמִּ**י**, "My people" (vv. 9, 12, 14), לְקוֹלִ**י**, "My voice" (v. 12), בִּדְרָכַ**י**, "My paths" (v. 14), יָדִ**י**, "My hand" (v. 15)—introduces Himself by His proper name ה׳ only inside His own speech (vv. 11, 16), and punctuates three key verses with the identical word לִי (`LIY`, "to Me"):

- שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ יִשְׂרָאֵל אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־**לִי** — "Hear, My people, and I will admonish you; Israel, if you would but listen **to Me**!" (v. 9)

- וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה **לִי** — "But My people would not listen to My voice, Israel would not obey **Me**." (v. 12)

- לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ **לִי** יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ — "If only My people would listen **to Me**, if Israel would follow My paths," (v. 14)

Track the five occurrences of the root שׁמע, "hear," across the psalm and a bitter asymmetry emerges. In verse 9, hearing is an imperative (שְׁמַע) and an unfinished condition (אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי)—a sentence that trails off without an "if... then" conclusion, slowed down in the Masoretic text by three successive vertical accent marks (תִּֽשְׁמַֽע־לִֽי) that force the reader to linger on every word. In verse 12, hearing is flatly negated (וְלֹא־שָׁמַע). In verse 14, it is an unreal wish (לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי). The only affirmative act of hearing voiced in the entire psalm is the prophet's אֶשְׁמָע, "I hear," in verse 6c—and he hears a language he does *not* know, whereas Israel refuses to hear a God speaking to them in the plainest Hebrew of the Sinai covenant. Meanwhile, God's own record of listening is spotless: בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ וָאֲחַלְּצֶךָּ אֶעֶנְךָ בְּסֵתֶר רַעַם, "In distress you called and I rescued you; I answered you from the secret place of thunder" (v. 8). In 1959, the psychologist Neville Moray demonstrated in dichotic-listening experiments what is known as the "cocktail-party effect": people shadowing one stream of speech in one ear are deaf to almost everything played into the other ear, until someone speaks their own name. In verse 9, God tries the one signal that should cut through the festival din—He calls His people by both of their names, עַמִּי, "My people," and יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Israel"—and still verse 12 reports: וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי, "But My people would not listen to My voice."

#### The Freed Shoulder and the Open Mouth

When God reminds Israel of the Exodus in verse 7, He does not speak of cosmic plagues or parted seas; He speaks of what slavery felt like in the collarbone and the palms of the hands: הֲסִירוֹתִי מִסֵּבֶל שִׁכְמוֹ כַּפָּיו מִדּוּד תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "I relieved his shoulder of the burden, his hands were freed from the basket." The noun סֵבֶל means a load or enforced burden and can denote state corvée labor; outside Nehemiah, it appears elsewhere in the Bible only in 1 Kings 11:28, where Solomon appoints Jeroboam over לְכׇל־סֵבֶל בֵּית יוֹסֵף, "all the forced labor of the house of Joseph"—the very tribe Psalm 81 has just named in verse 6 (בִּיהוֹסֵף, "in Jehoseph"). The דּוּד, "basket" (or clay cauldron), is the brick-carrier's hod, familiar from Mesopotamian royal inscriptions where the *tupšikku* basket was the badge of conscripted labor, and visible on the walls of the fifteenth-century BCE Tomb of Rekhmire at Thebes, where Syrian and Nubian captives haul mud-bricks in baskets slung from shoulder-yokes under the rods of Egyptian overseers.

In the Masoretic Text of verse 7, Israel's palms תַּעֲבֹרְנָה, "passed away," from the basket; the Septuagint, reading a *dalet* (ד) for the *resh* (ר), translates ἐδούλευσαν (*edouleusan*), "served" (תַּעֲבֹדְנָה). That single-letter difference between עבר, "pass free," and עבד, "serve as a slave," points to a striking absence in the Hebrew text of Psalm 81: the root עבד, "serve" or "slave," never occurs in the psalm at all. Even when verse 11 quotes the opening of the Ten Commandments, it drops the Decalogue's phrase מִבֵּית עֲבָדִים, "out of the house of bondage" (Exodus 20:2).

Why? Because in this psalm, God does not free Israel's hands from Pharaoh's brick-basket in order to put a new load on their shoulders. Look at what happens to the Ten Commandments in verses 10–11:

- לֹא־יִהְיֶה בְךָ קֵל זָר וְלֹא תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה לְקֵל נֵכָר — "You shall have no foreign god, you shall not bow to an alien god." (v. 10)

- אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ — "I the LORD am your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt; open your mouth wide and I will fill it." (v. 11)

In Exodus 20:2–3, God first announces "I the LORD am your God" and then issues the prohibition "You shall have no other gods." Psalm 81 reverses the two verses. By putting the prohibition against foreign gods first (v. 10) and the self-introduction "I the LORD am your God" second (v. 11)—using the northern participle הַמַּעַלְךָ, "who brought you up," which reclaims for the LORD the golden-calf slogan אֵלֶּה אֱלֹקֶיךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל אֲשֶׁר הֶעֱלוּךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם, "These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up from the land of Egypt" (Exodus 32:4; compare 1 Kings 12:28, which begins הִנֵּה, "Behold")—the psalm makes the Decalogue climax not in a restriction, but in a feeding: הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "open your mouth wide and I will fill it."

Follow the causative (Hiphil) imperatives across the poem and you can watch the entire theology of worship turn inside out. At the start of the psalm, the choir gives Israel two causative imperatives that demand vocal output: הַרְנִינוּ (`har-NIY-nū`, "make a joyous cry") and הָרִיעוּ (`hā-RIY-ʿū`, "make a shout," v. 2). In the middle of the psalm, God gives Israel only one causative imperative, starting with the same *har-* sound: הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ (`har-ḥev-PIY-khā`, "widen your mouth," v. 11). Every other causative verb in the poem belongs to God: הֲסִירוֹתִי, "I relieved" (v. 7), וְאָעִידָה, "I will admonish" (v. 9), הַמַּעַלְךָ, "who brought you up" (v. 11), אַכְנִיעַ, "I would subdue," and אָשִׁיב, "I would turn back" (v. 15), וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ, "He fed them," and אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "I would sate you" (v. 17). The mouth that began by projecting song in verses 2–3, and that cried out in pain in Egypt in verse 8 (בַּצָּרָה קָרָאתָ, "in distress you called"), is asked in verse 11 to adopt the posture of a nestling gaping in the nest—or, as Rashi glosses it, לִשְׁאוֹל מִמֶּנִּי כָּל תַּאֲוַת לִבְּךָ, "to request of Me whatever your heart desires." Sinai, in Psalm 81, is not a new brick-yard; it is a table where the hardest thing required of the guest is to stop talking and let himself be fed, the same surrender George Herbert staged at the end of *Love (III)*:

> "Truth, Lord, but I have marr'd them; let my shame

> Go where it doth deserve."

> "And know you not," says Love, "who bore the blame?"

> "My dear, then I will serve."

> "You must sit down," says Love, "and taste my meat."

> So I did sit and eat.

#### The Divorce of "Letting Go" and the God Who Cannot Keep It Up

Herbert's speaker sits and eats; Israel in verse 12 does not. וְלֹא־שָׁמַע עַמִּי לְקוֹלִי וְיִשְׂרָאֵל לֹא־אָבָה לִי, "But My people would not listen to My voice, Israel would not obey Me." The verb אָבָה, "be willing" or "consent," goes deeper than the ear; it names the desire of the will, and by pairing לֹא־שָׁמַע with לֹא־אָבָה, the psalm shows Israel treating God the exact way Deuteronomy 13:9 commands Israel to treat an idolatrous seducer: לֹא־תֹאבֶה לוֹ וְלֹא תִשְׁמַע אֵלָיו, "do not assent or give heed to him." Why would a liberated people prefer their own devices to an open-mouth banquet? Notice a subtle change the psalm made in verse 10: where the Ten Commandments say לֹא־יִהְיֶה לְךָ, "You shall have no other gods *for* you" (Exodus 20:3), verse 10 says לֹא־יִהְיֶה **בְךָ** קֵל זָר, "There shall be no strange god **in you**." When verse 13 names the rival god to which Israel bowed, no Canaanite Baal or Asherah appears—only the interior of their own chest: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ בִּשְׁרִירוּת לִבָּם יֵלְכוּ בְּמוֹעֲצוֹתֵיהֶם, "So I let them go after their willful heart that they might follow their own devices." When Rabbi Abin remarked in the Talmud (*b. Shabbat* 105b; compare Rabbi Yannai's related teaching in *y. Nedarim* 9:1), אֵיזֶהוּ קֵל זָר שֶׁיֵּשׁ בְּגוּפוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם הֱוֵי אוֹמֵר זֶה יֵצֶר הָרָע, "Which strange god is in a person's body? Say: that is the evil inclination," he was not indulging in homiletic fancy; he was tracing the psalm's own wire between the foreign god "in you" (בְךָ, v. 10) and the stubbornness of "their heart" (לִבָּם, v. 13).

God's punishment in verse 13 is contained in a single devastating word: וָאֲשַׁלְּחֵהוּ, "So I let them go" (literally, "I sent him away"). The Piel stem of שׁלח, *shillaḥ*, is the great verb of the Exodus—שַׁלַּח אֶת־עַמִּי, "Let My people go" (Exodus 5:1)—and it is also the legal verb for divorcing a spouse (וְשִׁלְּחָהּ מִבֵּיתוֹ, "and he sends her out of his house," Deuteronomy 24:1; Jeremiah 3:8). In Egypt, Pharaoh refused to let Israel go; in the wilderness, God punishes Israel by letting them go. As Radak explains the line, סִלַּקְתִּי שְׁמִירָתִי מֵעֲלֵיהֶם וְשַׂמְתִּים הֶפְקֵר, "I removed My protection from upon them and left them ownerless." Wrath here is not a lightning bolt from heaven; it is the terrifying grant of autonomy, severing the tether so that a people who insisted on their own counsels must now live inside them.

In Jeremiah 11:4–8—the only other passage in the Bible that spells בִּשְׁרִירוּת, "in the stubbornness," full with a *yod*, and a passage that shares several motifs with Psalm 81's oracle, though in a different order ("on the day I brought them out from the land of Egypt... Listen to My voice... a land flowing with milk and honey... I solemnly warned your fathers... yet they did not listen... but walked each in the stubbornness of their evil heart")—the sentence ends with judicial execution: וָאָבִיא עֲלֵיהֶם אֶת־כׇּל־דִּבְרֵי הַבְּרִית־הַזֹּאת, "therefore I brought upon them all the words [of curse] of this covenant" (Jeremiah 11:8). In Psalm 81, God pronounces sentence in verse 13—and then cannot hold the pose for a single verse.

Verse 14 breaks out without a conjunction: לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי יִשְׂרָאֵל בִּדְרָכַי יְהַלֵּכוּ, "If only My people would listen to Me, if Israel would follow My paths!" Instead of the past-tense verb that normally follows לוּ, "if only," to mark a dead possibility ("if only they *had* listened"), the poet puts a present participle, שֹׁמֵעַ, "listening": *If only My people were listening to Me right now!* Where verse 13 had them trudging in the simple Qal stem (יֵלְכוּ, "they walk") in their own counsels, verse 14 imagines them striding in the intensive Piel stem (יְהַלֵּכוּ, "they walk about freely") in God's ways. The Talmud (*b. Avodah Zarah* 5a) hears in this verse the sound of a sovereign reduced to begging: אֵין אִם אֶלָּא לְשׁוֹן תַּחֲנוּנִים וְכֵן הוּא אוֹמֵר לוּ עַמִּי שׁוֹמֵעַ לִי, "The word 'if' is nothing other than an expression of supplication, and similarly it states: 'If only My people would listen to Me.'" Even Israel's enemies, verse 16 adds with a twist of the knife, would at least *pretend* to listen if God flexed His hand: מְשַׂנְאֵי ה׳ יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ, "Those who hate the LORD would cringe before Him" (literally, "would feign obedience to Him," playing on לוֹ, `LŌ`, "to Him," against verse 14's לוּ, `LŪ`, "if only"). God's haters would offer Him the outward submission that His own beloved people refuse.

And then comes the final verse, where the psalm's relationship to the end of Deuteronomy comes into focus. Already the psalm's opening word הַרְנִינוּ, "Sing joyously" (v. 2), is a form found also in Psalm 32:11 and in the final verse of the Song of Moses (הַרְנִינוּ גוֹיִם עַמּוֹ, "O nations, acclaim His people," Deuteronomy 32:43), and verse 16's יְכַחֲשׁוּ־לוֹ echoes the final verse of the Blessing of Moses (וְיִכָּחֲשׁוּ אֹיְבֶיךָ לָךְ, "And your enemies shall come cringing to you," Deuteronomy 33:29). In Deuteronomy 31:19–21, just before Moses recites his Song, God commands him: שִׂימָהּ בְּפִיהֶם לְמַעַן תִּהְיֶה־לִּי הַשִּׁירָה הַזֹּאת לְעֵד בִּבְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, "put it in their mouths, in order that this song may be My witness against the people of Israel," predicting that they will eat, be sated (וְאָכַל וְשָׂבַע, 31:20), and turn to foreign gods. In the Song of Moses itself, God feeds Israel דְּבַשׁ מִסֶּלַע, "honey from the crag," and חֵלֶב כִּלְיוֹת חִטָּה, "fat of kidney-wheat" (Deuteronomy 32:13–14a), followed immediately in the next half-verse by וְדַם־עֵנָב תִּשְׁתֶּה־חָמֶר וַיִּשְׁמַן יְשֻׁרוּן וַיִּבְעָט, "and blood of the grape you drank as wine—so Jeshurun grew fat and kicked" (Deuteronomy 32:14b–15).

Psalm 81 is that "song as witness" (עֵדוּת, v. 6; וְאָעִידָה, v. 9), sung in the season of winepresses, but it reverses Deuteronomy's sequence. In Deuteronomy, the feeding comes before the rebellion and provokes it; in Psalm 81, the rebellion has already happened (vv. 12–13), and God holds out the wheat and the honey *afterward*, stopping at the exact word before the wine and the kicking. The dry צוּר, "rock," of Meribah where God tested a thirsty people in verse 8 becomes in verse 17 a rock dripping with wild honey. And in the last five words of the poem, the grammar makes one final swerve:

- וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה — "He fed them [literally, 'him'] the finest wheat;" (v. 17a)

- וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶ**ךָ** — "I sated **you** [or: 'I would sate **you**'] with honey from the rock." (v. 17b)

Following verse 16's third-person reference to "the LORD," verse 17a begins in the third person ("He fed him"), as if a narrator were summarizing what might have been. Then, on the last word of the psalm, God pushes the narrator aside and speaks directly to a single listener in the second-person masculine singular: אַשְׂבִּיעֶ**ךָ** (`'as-biy-ʿE-khā`), "I would sate **you**." The noisy festival crowd that began in verse 2 shouting in the plural about "our strength" and "our feast" has been pared down to a solitary "you," standing in the sudden quiet after the shofar blast. No congregational response is recorded. The offer of verse 11—open your mouth wide and I will fill it—is still on the table, and the poem ends in silence, waiting to see what the listener will do with his mouth.

#### Full psalm

Mishnah *Tamid* 7:4 assigns Psalm 81 to the Levites in the Temple as the Song of the Day for Thursday: בַּחֲמִישִׁי הָיוּ אוֹמְרִים הַרְנִינוּ לֵאלֹקִים עוּזֵּנוּ הָרִיעוּ לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "On the fifth day they would say: 'Sing joyfully to God our strength, shout to the God of Jacob'" (v. 2), a choice the Talmud (*b. Rosh Hashanah* 31a) connects to the fifth day of creation, when God made the birds and fish to praise His name. Following this Temple usage, the full psalm is recited at the close of weekday Shacharit on Thursdays across Nusach Ashkenaz, Nusach Sefard, Chabad, and Edot HaMizrach (introduced by הַיּוֹם יוֹם חֲמִישִׁי בַּשַּׁבָּת שֶׁבּוֹ הָיוּ הַלְוִיִּם אוֹמְרִים בְּבֵית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ, "Today is the fifth day of the week, on which the Levites would say in the Temple"), and is reprinted in the Rosh Chodesh, Rosh Hashanah, and Yom Kippur orders for when those days fall on a Thursday. Because v. 4 commands the blowing of the shofar at the new moon, the Edot HaMizrach machzor also appoints the entire psalm on the eve of Rosh Hashanah before Arvit (between the piyyut *Achot Ketannah* and the meditation *Leshem Yichud*) and, under the rubric יֵשׁ נוֹהֲגִים לוֹמַר, "some are accustomed to say," at Mincha on Rosh Hashanah.

#### Key verses

Verses 4–5 are the signature scriptural prooftext for Rosh Hashanah. In the *Shofarot* section of the Rosh Hashanah Musaf Amidah (in Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard, with v. 4 in Edot HaMizrach), they stand among the ten verses of the shofar, immediately after Psalm 98:6: וְנֶאֱמַר בַּחֲצוֹצְרוֹת וְקוֹל שׁוֹפָר הָרִיעוּ לִפְנֵי הַמֶּלֶךְ ה׳ וְנֶאֱמַר תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר בַּכֶּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "And it is said: 'With trumpets and the sound of the shofar shout before the King, the LORD.' And it is said: 'Blow the shofar at the new moon, at the covered moon for our feast-day; for it is a statute for Israel, a judgment of the God of Jacob.'" Verses 4–5 also recur after *Hashkiveinu* in the Ma'ariv service of Rosh Hashanah (Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard), in the daytime Kiddush for Rosh Hashanah (Nusach Sefard), before the sounding of the shofar (Edot HaMizrach), and across the selichot and piyyutim of the Days of Awe. Verse 5 alone is woven directly into the prose opening of the *Zikhronot* blessing of the Rosh Hashanah Musaf Amidah in all three rites, reading מִשְׁפָּט, "ruling," not merely as a festival regulation but as the cosmic day of judgment: זֶה הַיּוֹם תְּחִלַּת מַעֲשֶׂיךָ זִכָּרוֹן לְיוֹם רִאשׁוֹן כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב וְעַל הַמְּדִינוֹת בּוֹ יֵאָמֵר אֵי זוֹ לַחֶרֶב וְאֵי זוֹ לַשָּׁלוֹם, "This day is the beginning of Your works, a remembrance of the first day. 'For it is a statute for Israel, a judgment of the God of Jacob.' And concerning the countries it is pronounced on it which for the sword and which for peace."

In daily morning prayer, v. 11 appears in the anthology of verses following 1 Chronicles 16 in *Hodu* (Pesukei Dezimra) across Nusach Ashkenaz, Nusach Sefard, Chabad, and Edot HaMizrach. Placed immediately after the plea of Psalm 44:27—קוּמָה עֶזְרָתָה לָּנוּ וּפְדֵנוּ לְמַעַן חַסְדֶּךָ אָנֹכִי ה׳ אֱלֹקֶיךָ הַמַּעַלְךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם הַרְחֶב־פִּיךָ וַאֲמַלְאֵהוּ, "Arise, help us, and redeem us for Your kindness' sake. 'I am the LORD your God who brought you up from the land of Egypt; open your mouth wide and I will fill it'"—the liturgy turns God's rebuke in the psalm into a direct, comforting answer to the petitioner's cry for help. Elsewhere, v. 3 (כִּנּוֹר נָעִים עִם נָבֶל, "the melodious lyre with the harp") is invoked in the Simchat Torah hakafot (Nusach Sefard) in a prayer for the restoration of Levitical music, and v. 9 (שְׁמַע עַמִּי וְאָעִידָה בָּךְ, "Hear, My people, and I will admonish you") enters the Yom Kippur night selichot (Edot HaMizrach) in a litany of verses built on שְׁמַע, "hear."
