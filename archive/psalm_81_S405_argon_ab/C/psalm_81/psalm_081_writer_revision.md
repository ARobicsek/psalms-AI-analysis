### P1 (flagged)

Both the synagogue and the church found Psalm 81 too volatile to recite whole at the height of a festival, and the way each tradition clipped it tells you where the danger lies. On Rosh Hashanah, in the *Shofarot* section of the Musaf Amidah, the liturgy quotes verses 4 and 5—תִּקְעוּ בַחֹדֶשׁ שׁוֹפָר בַּכֵּסֶה לְיוֹם חַגֵּנוּ כִּי חֹק לְיִשְׂרָאֵל הוּא מִשְׁפָּט לֵאלֹקֵי יַעֲקֹב, "Blow the horn on the new moon, on the full moon for our feast day. For it is a law for Israel, a ruling of the God of Jacob"—and stops dead before verse 6. In the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church, the liturgy of Corpus Christi goes to the opposite end of the psalm, lifting verse 17 out of its context to serve as its festive Introit (*Cibavit eos*): וַיַּאֲכִילֵהוּ מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה וּמִצּוּר דְּבַשׁ אַשְׂבִּיעֶךָ, "He fed them the finest wheat; I sated you with honey from the rock," turning an aching, unfulfilled offer into an accomplished sacramental fact. One tradition keeps the opening fanfare and cuts off the voice that answers it; the other keeps the final banquet and cuts off the grief that precedes it. Between those two sheared edges lies the poem itself.

### P2 (flagged)

**Before:**

Psalm 81 is a festival liturgy engineered to interrupt itself. Its seventeen verses fall into two unequal halves joined by a three-word hinge in the middle of verse 6. For the first four and a half verses (vv. 2–6b), a temple choir summons the congregation to fill the sanctuary with noise—singing, shouting, beating the timbrel, plucking lyre and harp, and blowing the ram's horn to celebrate the Exodus. Then, in the last clause of verse 6, an unnamed "I" freezes the music: שְׂפַת לֹא־יָדַעְתִּי אֶשְׁמָע, "I heard a language that I knew not." For the remaining eleven verses (vv. 7–17), the congregation never utters another word. God takes over the poem in the first person, recalling how He lifted the slave-basket from Israel's shoulders (vv. 7–8), pleading with His people to listen and open their mouths to be fed (vv. 9–11), mourning their refusal and His own decision to let them go their willful way (vv. 12–13), and finally breaking down into a counterfactual cry—לוּ עַמִּי שֹׁמֵעַֽ לִי, "If only My people would listen to Me" (v. 14)—that dangles the promise of victory and honey from the rock before a silent listener (vv. 15–17). The psalm is not against music; it is about the religious temptation to use music to drown out the one we are singing to. It stages a worship service in which the congregation's own shofar blast accidentally summons the voice of Sinai, and that voice asks for the one thing a noisy festival is least prepared to give: an ear that listens and an empty mouth waiting to be filled.

### P11 (flagged)

**Before:**

Track the five occurrences of the root שׁמע, "hear," across the psalm and a bitter asymmetry emerges. In verse 9, hearing is an imperative (שְׁמַע) and an unfinished condition (אִם־תִּשְׁמַע־לִי)—a sentence that trails off without an "if... then" conclusion, slowed down in the Masoretic text by three successive vertical accent marks (תִּֽשְׁמַֽע־לִֽי) that force the reader to linger on every word. Read with that overtone, verses 16 and 17 form a sharp physical contrast: the haters of the LORD waste away from fatness (יְכַחֲשׁוּ), while Israel is fed מֵחֵלֶב חִטָּה, "from the fat of wheat."
