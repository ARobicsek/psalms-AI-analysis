# Commentary on Psalm 76
---
## Introduction
Psalm 76 has been sung on the eve of real battles, and by any reckoning it is a victory song: broken bows, collapsed cavalry, kings bringing tribute. Read it slowly, though, and something is missing. Nobody in it fights. No army of Judah takes the field. No king is named. No enemy dead are counted. No sword is drawn except to be snapped.
The stranger absence is this: no one in the poem ever says "we." There is no "us," no "our," no "I." Set it beside its closest sibling and the silence becomes audible. Psalm 46 has a refrain: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת עִמָּנוּ מִשְׂגָּב־לָנוּ אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "YHWH of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our stronghold" (46:8, 12). Psalm 76 uses the same title, אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, "God of Jacob" (v. 7), and attaches it to no one.
Both absences are the point. Psalm 76 is not about a battle won but about war undone. It makes that claim less by stating it than by how it hands out its verbs. Every act of force belongs to God. The human beings, allies and enemies alike, are left with three things they can do: fail, fall silent, or give thanks.
The poem moves in four short steps after its heading (v. 1):
- **Verses 2–4** locate God. He is known in Judah, housed in Salem and Zion, and "there" He breaks arrows, shield, sword, and finally war itself. A *Selah* closes the scene. (Selah is a musical or liturgical marker whose exact sense is lost; it seems to signal a pause.)
- **Verses 5–7** turn to address God directly. He is the radiant one on the mountains of prey. Before Him the stout-hearted slump into sleep and cannot find their hands, and horse and chariot go down at a single rebuke.
- **Verses 8–10** move from battlefield to courtroom. A verdict is heard from heaven, a frightened earth falls still, and a Judge rises "to save all the lowly of the earth." A second *Selah* follows.
- **Verses 11–13** form a coda in a new tense. Human fury will praise; worshipers will vow and pay; the surrounding peoples will bring gifts; God prunes the spirit of princes.
The geography widens at every stage: Judah, Zion, heaven, the whole earth, its kings. And the verbs slide from what God did to what God does.
## Who Holds the Verbs
List the poem's actions and who performs them, and the argument writes itself.
God's verbs are all acts of force. He shatters (שִׁבַּר, v. 4). He makes judgment heard (הִשְׁמַעְתָּ, v. 9). He rises (קוּם) and saves (לְהוֹשִׁיעַ, v. 10). He girds (תַּחְגֹּר, v. 11). He prunes (יִבְצֹר, v. 13).
The enemy's verbs belong to another species:
- they "were stripped" (אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ);
- they "slumbered their sleep" (נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם);
- they "did not find" (לֹא מָצְאוּ) their own hands (v. 6);
- horse and chariot "sank into deep sleep" (נִרְדָּם, v. 7);
- the earth "feared and fell still" (v. 9).
The one verb of resistance, "stand," appears only inside a question that expects no answer: וּמִי־יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ, "who can stand before You?" (v. 8). In the first ten verses, the only thing a human being does to anything is fail to find his own hands.
After the verdict, human verbs return, but in a different key. Fury "will acknowledge You" (תּוֹדֶךָּ, v. 11). Worshipers are told to vow and to pay. The peoples round about "bring tribute" (v. 12). Human beings get their agency back only as worship.
The poem's most famous list makes the same point in miniature: שָׁמָּה שִׁבַּר רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת מָגֵן וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה, "There He shattered the flaming shafts of the bow, shield and sword and war" (v. 4). Three things you can hold, then one you cannot. It may be the only war poem whose principal casualty is a noun.
The poet did not invent this list. He borrowed it from Hosea's promise of a covenant with the beasts: וְקֶשֶׁת וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה אֶשְׁבּוֹר מִן־הָאָרֶץ וְהִשְׁכַּבְתִּים לָבֶטַח, "bow and sword and war I will break from the land, and I will make them lie down in safety" (Hos 2:20). The verb is the same, and so is the climb from weapon to abstraction. In both passages, sleep follows.
But the sleepers differ. In Hosea it is Israel who lies down, safe at last. In Psalm 76 the sleepers are the invaders. Their slumber in v. 6 deepens in v. 7 into the heavy, God-sent unconsciousness the Bible calls תַּרְדֵּמָה, *tardemah*. In Hosea, peace means Israel can sleep. Here, peace means the aggressor does not wake.
Where does all this happen? In a lair: וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ בְצִיּוֹן, "His covert was in Salem, His den in Zion" (v. 3). These are words for a lion's hiding place, as in Jeremiah: עָזַב כַּכְּפִיר סֻכּוֹ, "like a young lion He has left His covert" (Jer 25:38). The only predator in Jerusalem is God. Beside the lair stands the city's archaic name, Salem, which sounds like "peace."
## The Song of the Sea, Run Backwards
Why "chariot and horse" in v. 7? Because the poet is reworking the oldest victory song in Israel's memory, the one that begins סוּס וְרֹכְבוֹ רָמָה בַיָּם, "horse and rider He has hurled into the sea" (Exod 15:1).
The Song at the Sea and Psalm 76 share more than the horse:
- the root behind "majestic" (נֶאְדָּר, Exod 15:6, 11; אַדִּיר, Ps 76:5);
- the word "awesome" (נוֹרָא תְהִלֹּת, "awesome in praises," Exod 15:11; נוֹרָא, Ps 76:8, 13);
- a line about God's name that ends on the word "name."
The order, however, is reversed. Exodus moves from victory, to nations frozen "still as stone" (יִדְּמוּ כָּאָבֶן, 15:16), to the sanctuary as destination: מָכוֹן לְשִׁבְתְּךָ... מִקְּדָשׁ, "the place for Your dwelling… the sanctuary" (15:17). Psalm 76 begins in the sanctuary, then gives the victory, then the stilled earth. The Song at the Sea ends where this psalm starts.
The sharpest revision is a single word. Exodus names God יְהוָה אִישׁ מִלְחָמָה יְהוָה שְׁמוֹ, "YHWH is a man of war; YHWH is His name" (15:3). Psalm 76 opens with the name, גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ, "great is His name." Two verses later He breaks מִלְחָמָה, "war." The man of war has become the breaker of war.
Some communities recite Psalm 76 on the seventh day of Passover, the day the Song at the Sea is read in synagogue. That custom rests on more than a shared horse.
The reversal also changes time. Through v. 10 the poem speaks in completed past actions: He shattered, You made heard, God rose. The coda switches to the ongoing and the future: fury will acknowledge, they will bring, He prunes. A single deliverance has become a rule.
This is why the commentators never agreed on which war the psalm describes. Some read it as Sennacherib's army, found dead at dawn outside Jerusalem in 701 BCE (Rashi, Malbim). Others read it as the final war of Gog (Radak). The poem's verbs give each reading exactly half its footing.
## A Deliverance Without a Possessive
That brings us back to the missing "we." The beneficiaries of the verdict are named in v. 10, and they are not Israel: לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ, "to save all the lowly of the earth."
The commentators know a gap in a sentence when they see one, and they fill it. Rashi writes לְהוֹשִׁיעַ חִזְקִיָּהוּ וְסִיעָתוֹ, "to save Hezekiah and his company." Radak writes להושיע את ישראל מהם שהם ענוי ארץ, "to save Israel from them, for they are the lowly of the earth."
Both readings are reasonable; the psalm was sung in Jerusalem by Jerusalemites. But the poem itself refuses the narrowing, and not in one universalist verse only. It refuses it everywhere. The only people ever addressed directly are the plural "you" of v. 12, and they are addressed only to be told to pay their vows. Judah is where God is known. It is not where the saved are listed.
Read from underneath, the poem is simpler than any of this. Most people who have ever lived had no army and no say in the wars that crossed their fields. They heard of armies the way one hears of floods. This psalm is addressed to them. It says the sentence has already been read, from higher up than any throne, and that it was read in their favor.
The Judge does not ask the powerful to become gentle. He predicts something stranger: כִּי־חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ, "human fury will acknowledge You" (v. 11). Rage, the thing that drives history in every other war poem, is given one last job in this one: to say thank you.

#### Full psalm
Psalm 76 is recited in full as the psalm of the day on the **first day of Sukkot**. This is the custom in many Mizrachi and Sephardic (Edot HaMizrach) communities and in the custom of the Vilna Gaon. The obvious hook is v. 3, וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ, "His *sukkah* was in Salem," but the choice rests on more than one word:
- The festival's prophetic readings dramatize the psalm's plot. The haftarah for the first day is Zechariah 14, where the survivors of the nations who attacked Jerusalem go up each year לָחֹג אֶת־חַג הַסֻּכּוֹת, "to keep the festival of Sukkot" (Zech 14:16).
- The haftarah for Shabbat Ḥol ha-Moʿed is Ezekiel 38–39. There God breaks Gog's bow and arrows "from your hand" and Israel burns the abandoned shields (Ezek 39:3, 9), the same bow, hand, and shield that vv. 4–6 of the psalm dispose of.
- The seventy bulls of Sukkot, offered traditionally for the seventy nations, answer the psalm's closing picture of tribute from "all around Him" (v. 12).
The pairing follows the plain sense of the psalm and extends it.
Some Ashkenazi and Hasidic communities also recite the psalm on the **seventh day of Passover**, when the Song at the Sea is read. The link is v. 7, נִרְדָּם וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס, "chariot and horse sank into deep sleep." As the essay argues, the whole psalm is built from the Song's vocabulary.
#### Key verses
**Verse 2.** In Nusach Sefard (the Hasidic rite), נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ is woven into the Simchat Torah *hakafot*, the circuits with the Torah scrolls, just before the fifth circuit. It is surrounded by pleas that God וְתִבְנֶה בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ, "rebuild the Temple," and restore Levitical song. Dancing with the Torah, the congregation becomes the "Israel" in which the name is great.
The same rite opens an alphabetic *selichah* for the first Monday of the pre–Rosh Hashanah penitential season with אֱלֹהִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל נוֹדָעְתָּ, "God, great in Israel, You have made Yourself known." Two things change. The psalm's report ("He is known") becomes direct address ("You are known"). And the renown becomes the backdrop for confession: the poem goes on to say that God's kindness came לֹא בְצִדְקוֹתֵינוּ, "not by our righteous deeds." This is a novel use: the psalm's triumph becomes the measure of Israel's unworthiness.
**Verse 8.** In the Edot HaMizrach Yom Kippur evening service, Abraham ibn Ezra's piyyut *Lecha Eli Teshukati* borrows the psalm's question: וּמִי יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ וּמִי יִהְיֶה תְמוּרָתִי, "and who can stand before You, and who will take my place?" The line leads straight into the alphabetical confession, וְאָשַׁמְתִּי וְאָרַבְתִּי, "I have trespassed, I have lain in wait." In the psalm the answer to "who can stand?" is *no enemy*. In the piyyut it is *not I*.
**Verse 13.** In the Ashkenazi Rosh Hashanah morning service, the *yotzer* hymn *Melekh Azur Gevurah* places the phrase between images from Isaiah 63: מֶֽלֶךְ חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים. בְּדָרְכוֹ בוֹגְדִים. יִבְצֹר רֽוּחַ נְגִידִים, "the King with crimsoned garments, treading down the treacherous, pruning the spirit of princes."
A Shabbat table song (*zemer*) in the Nusach Sefard siddur makes the same fusion even more explicit: בְּבוֹאוֹ מֵאֱדוֹם חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים, זֶֽבַח לוֹ בְּבָצְרָה... בְּכֹחוֹ הַגָּדוֹל יִבְצוֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים, "when He comes from Edom with crimsoned garments, a sacrifice for Him in Bozrah… with His great strength He will prune the spirit of princes." Both poets heard the name Bozrah (בָּצְרָה) inside יִבְצֹר; see the commentary on v. 13.
#### Practical Kabbalah
The *Shimush Tehillim*, the manual of practical uses for the psalms attributed to Rav Hai Gaon, prescribes Psalm 76 לְהִנָּצֵל מֵאֵשׁ וּמִמַּיִם, "to be rescued from fire and from water." Later compilers also recommend it to travelers facing bandits.
The logic is not hard to reconstruct. Fire comes from v. 4: רִשְׁפֵי are flames or fire-bolts, and the God who breaks them disarms the blaze. Water is best traced not to the stilled earth of v. 9 but to v. 7's גְּעָרָה, "rebuke." Almost everywhere else, that word names God's rebuke of the sea. The exact form מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ appears in only one other place, Ps 18:16, where it lays bare the channels of the waters.
There may also be a quieter prompt. The only other occurrence of the form רִשְׁפֵי is Song 8:6, and the very next verse says מַיִם רַבִּים לֹא יוּכְלוּ לְכַבּוֹת, "many waters cannot quench" it. The travelers' use needs no such subtlety: vv. 6–7 describe armed men who "slumber their sleep" and "cannot find their hands."

## Verse-by-Verse Commentary
**Verse 1**
לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת; מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף, שִׁיר.
> For the conductor, with strings. A psalm of Asaph; a song.
Asaph was one of David's Levite singers (1 Chr 16:5). His name heads twelve psalms: 50 and 73–83. Most of them belong to what scholars call the Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42–83), a block in which an editor seems to have preferred the generic אֱלֹהִים, "God," over the personal name YHWH. Psalm 76 follows the pattern almost perfectly, with one exception (v. 12), and that exception tells us something; see there. The instruction בִּנְגִינֹת, "with strings," appears in several other headings (Pss 4, 6, 54, 55, 67).
The Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation made by Alexandrian Jews in the last centuries BCE, adds a phrase to the heading: ᾠδὴ πρὸς τὸν Ἀσσύριον, "an ode concerning the Assyrian." This is the earliest surviving decision about which war the psalm describes. It points to Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem in 701 BCE, which ended when וַיַּשְׁכִּימוּ בַבֹּקֶר וְהִנֵּה כֻלָּם פְּגָרִים מֵתִים, "they rose early in the morning, and behold, they were all dead corpses" (2 Kgs 19:35). The translator had more to go on than that one verse; see the notes on vv. 4 and 6.
Malbim dates the psalm to Hezekiah's reign. That is less odd than it sounds, since "Asaph" can name a guild: the "sons of Asaph" appear among Hezekiah's Levites (2 Chr 29:13). Meiri, taking Asaph to be David's contemporary, has David foresee the event and praise God "as if it had already come about."
**Verse 2**
נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה אֱלֹהִים; בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל גָּדוֹל שְׁמוֹ.
> God has made Himself known in Judah; in Israel His name is great.
Nobody in this verse discovers God; God does the disclosing. The form נוֹדָע means "has become known" or "has made Himself known," not "they knew Him." The parallel lines look like a simple restatement, but Malbim, in his notes on individual words (*Beur Hamilot*), reads them as a step down. To be "known" is knowledge of God Himself, and that belongs to Judah, where the divine Presence dwells. To have a great "name" is reputation, and that is what reaches the rest of Israel. On his reading, the two halves of the verse map a center and a periphery.
Judah comes first. Meiri says this is out of respect for the royal house. Most modern readers connect it to the Asaphite guild's northern roots: after the northern kingdom fell in 722, "Israel" survives here as a name joined to Judah's sanctuary.
The pairing of "known" (from ידע) and "great" (from גדל) is stock language for the nations' assault on Zion. The sister Zion song, Psalm 48, begins גָּדוֹל יְהוָה, "great is YHWH" (48:2), and continues נוֹדַע לְמִשְׂגָּב, "He has made Himself known as a stronghold" (48:4). Ezekiel closes his oracle against Gog with the same two roots: וְהִתְגַּדִּלְתִּי וְהִתְקַדִּשְׁתִּי וְנוֹדַעְתִּי לְעֵינֵי גּוֹיִם רַבִּים, "I will magnify Myself and sanctify Myself and make Myself known in the eyes of many nations" (Ezek 38:23). When Radak reads this psalm as the war of Gog, then, he is following a real verbal trail, not indulging a whim. He glosses our verse as the moment God's vengeance is seen "upon the nations gathered against Jerusalem."
This opening line has had the fullest afterlife of any in the psalm. It sets the tone for Charpentier's grand motet *Notus in Judaea Deus*. In the Hasidic rite it is also embedded in the Simchat Torah *hakafot* and turned into the first line of a penitential acrostic; see the liturgical section.
**Verse 3**
וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סוּכּוֹ, וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ בְצִיּוֹן.
> His covert was in Salem, and His den in Zion.
*Salem* is Jerusalem's oldest name, the city of Melchizedek: וּמַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק מֶלֶךְ שָׁלֵם, "and Melchizedek, king of Salem" (Gen 14:18). The Greek translator did not render it as a place name at all: ἐν εἰρήνῃ, "in peace." This is less a mistake than a decision to say the pun out loud. God's den stands "in Peace," the weapons are broken there in the next verse, and in v. 12 the worshipers are told to שַׁלְּמוּ, "pay," their vows. The same three consonants, ש-ל-ם, frame the poem.
Why this name and not "Jerusalem"? The Chida (*Chomat Anakh*) quotes an earlier work that answers directly: ירושלם הוא יראה שלם וסילק היראה ונשאר שלם, "Jerusalem is *Yir'eh-Shalem*; the *Yir'eh* has been taken away, and *Shalem* remains."
The step behind this is a midrash that Radak quotes on this same verse. Shem (whom the rabbis identify with Melchizedek) called the place *Shalem*. Abraham called it *Yir'eh*, from ה׳ יִרְאֶה, "the LORD will see" (Gen 22:14). God, unwilling to slight either, fused the two into *Yerushalayim*. The psalm uses only Shem's half.
The other half has not vanished. It is scattered through the poem as its key word, the root ירא, "fear": נוֹרָא (v. 8), יָרְאָה (v. 9), מוֹרָא (v. 12), נוֹרָא (v. 13). At v. 9 the exact consonants י-ר-א-ה reappear, though there they mean "feared," not Abraham's "will see." Whether the poet intended this cannot be proven. But the effect is that the city's full name is spelled out across the poem: *Shalem* at the start, fear at the end.
The masoretic notes, recorded by *Minchat Shai*, add a remarkable detail. (The Masoretes were the scribes who fixed the Bible's text and annotated its spellings.) Their note lists three places where a word pronounced *sukko* occurs:
- Jer 25:38, עָזַב כַּכְּפִיר סֻכּוֹ, "like a young lion He has left His covert";
- our verse, spelled with a *vav*;
- Lam 2:6, וַיַּחְמֹס כַּגַּן שֻׂכּוֹ, "He has stripped His booth like a garden," spelled with a *sin*.
The note exists to track spelling. In doing so, it files our word beside a lion's lair and beside the booth God Himself tore down.
So is סוּכּוֹ a festival booth or a lion's covert? The parallel term tips the balance. מְעוֹנָה is a dwelling, but its marked uses are animal dens: הֲיִתֵּן כְּפִיר קוֹלוֹ מִמְּעֹנָתוֹ, "does a young lion give voice from his den?" (Amos 3:4). Malbim's word-notes add that the מָעוֹן is often "the den of fearsome beasts of prey." Verse 5's "mountains of prey" then closes the case. The booth still hovers in the background, and the Sukkot liturgy was right to hear it. But the primary picture is a predator at home.
The picture sharpens morally against Ps 10:9, where the wicked יֶאֱרֹב... כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה... לַחֲטוֹף עָנִי, "lurks like a lion in his covert, to seize the poor." Every other lion in a covert hunts the helpless. This is the one lair whose lion hunts the hunters; v. 10 names its beneficiaries.
(Radak, committed to a future war, has to translate this verse's opening וַיְהִי, "and it was," as ואז יהיה, "and then it will be." See v. 13 for why.)
Ted Hughes, in "The Jaguar" (from his first collection, *The Hawk in the Rain*, 1957), watches a big cat in a zoo, and the poem turns on the discovery that the cage does not contain it:
> He spins from the bars, but there's no cage to him
> More than to the visionary his cell:
> His stride is wildernesses of freedom
The cage in Hughes is humiliating, and it fails. The psalm's lair is chosen, and it does not diminish its occupant. Both poets refuse to let a fixed address domesticate a wild power. Hughes's animal carries the wilderness into the enclosure. The psalmist's God makes the city itself into the lair, and what He breaks there reaches the ends of the earth.
**Verse 4**
שָׁמָּה שִׁבַּר רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת, מָגֵן וְחֶרֶב וּמִלְחָמָה. סֶלָה.
> There He shattered the flaming shafts of the bow, shield and sword and war. Selah.
The emphatic "there" (שָׁמָּה) answers a prophecy. Isaiah's oracle to Sennacherib promised לֹא־יוֹרֶה שָׁם חֵץ וְלֹא־יְקַדְּמֶנָּה מָגֵן, "he shall not shoot an arrow *there*, nor come before it with a *shield*" (Isa 37:33 // 2 Kgs 19:32). The psalm uses the same three pieces, "there," arrow, and shield, and turns the negative into a positive: there He broke the arrows. This lexical match, more than the overnight deaths of 2 Kings 19:35, may explain why the tradition was so sure the psalm is about Assyria.
The word also belongs to a chain of sounds running through the poem: `**SH**ĀM-māh`, "there," after `shə-**M**Ō`, "His name" (v. 2), and before `mi**sh**-**sh**ā-**M**A-yim hi**sh**-**M**Aʿ-tā`, "from heaven You made heard" (v. 9). Name, place, heaven, and hearing are tied together by one pair of consonants.
What are רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת? Rashi rejects the obvious gloss "sparks," and he argues from the text. First, sparks have nothing to do with a bow. Second, the פ here has no dot (it is pronounced *f*), while in רִשְׁפֵּי אֵשׁ, "flames of fire" (Song 8:6), it has one (*p*). (*Minchat Shai* confirms from the masoretic notes that our פ is undotted.) Rashi instead ties the word to Deut 32:24 and Job 5:7, which the Aramaic translation renders "fowl," and concludes:
וּבְנֵי רֶשֶׁף בְּנֵי עֲפִיפָה הֵם שֵׁדִים יַגְבִּיהוּ לָעוּף, "the 'sons of Resheph' are flying things — they are demons, who soar upward."
So רִשְׁפֵי־קָשֶׁת are "the arrows the bow sends flying."
Rashi never saw a Ugaritic tablet, yet his grammar brought him to a demon. The tablets, found in 1929, name *Ršp*, Resheph, a Canaanite god of plague and archery, known in Phoenician as "Resheph of the arrow." Habakkuk still remembers him walking behind the divine warrior: וְיֵצֵא רֶשֶׁף לְרַגְלָיו, "and Resheph goes forth at His feet" (Hab 3:5). In our verse a god's arrows are snapped and left as scrap among the shields. If the plague traditions of 701 lie behind the psalm, the demotion is pointed: the power that brings pestilence is itself broken ordnance.
The Alshich (*Romemot El*) reads the verse against every assumption of a Zion song. Writing in sixteenth-century Safed, long after the Temple had fallen, he takes "there" to mean Zion itself as the target. God, he says, השליט את האף וחמה על ציון להתיך בה כחם, "set anger and wrath loose upon Zion, to melt away their force against it." This is the theology of Lamentations: כִּלָּה יְהוָה אֶת־חֲמָתוֹ... וַיַּצֶּת־אֵשׁ בְּצִיּוֹן, "the LORD spent His fury… and kindled a fire in Zion" (Lam 4:11). And the list's "shield"? כאשר המגן סובל הכאות שמכים את בעליו כך ציון סבלה מכות בעד ישראל, "as a shield takes the blows aimed at its owner, so Zion took blows on Israel's behalf."
On this reading the psalm of Zion's inviolability becomes a psalm of Zion's burning, and the shield in the list is the city. It is a reading taken at a real cost, and it shows what a reader after 70 CE could not stop hearing in the word "there."
The *Selah* falls right after the abstract noun "war." On how that word returns in v. 9, see there.
**Verse 5**
נָאוֹר אַתָּה, אַדִּיר מֵהַרְרֵי־טָרֶף.
> Resplendent are You, majestic, from the mountains of prey.
נָאוֹר, "made light, resplendent," is an odd form, and it sits one reshuffle away from the poem's key word: `nā-'ŌR` against `NŌ-rā'`, the same three letters, נ-א-ר against נ-ר-א. Many scholars emend it to נוֹרָא, "awesome." The Alshich reports that the Aramaic Targum already renders it that way, דְּחִיל, "feared." The Hebrew has "light," the Targum has "fear," and the poem has room for both.
Malbim shows why neither needs to replace the other. Sennacherib's army fell at night, so he pictures God going out from Zion like a lion hunting in the dark, and he explains the title "resplendent" accordingly: שלא יחשך לך בלילה בלכתך אל מחנה האויב, "for it does not grow dark for You by night as You go to the enemy camp." The light is what lets the hunter see. To those caught in the camp, the same light is the terror. Keeping נָאוֹר loses nothing of נוֹרָא; it tells us the terror arrived lit.
The same night-logic drives the Passover hymn *Vayehi Ba-Ḥatzi ha-Laylah*, attributed to Yannai, the Byzantine-era Palestinian poet. In Ashkenazi homes it is sung near the end of the Seder. Every line ends on the word לַיְלָה, "night," as it lists God's victories that came while the enemy slept, Sennacherib among them:
> הִפְחַדְתָּ אֲרַמִּי בְּאֶמֶשׁ לַיְלָה
> ...
> יָעַץ מְחָרֵף לְנוֹפֵף אִוּוּי
> הוֹבַשְׁתָּ פְגָרָיו בַּלַּיְלָה
>
> "You terrified the Aramean in the dark of night…
> The blasphemer plotted to shake his fist at the Desired [city];
> You withered his corpses in the night."
Yannai's repeated rhyme makes night the stage on which power reverses. The psalm puts the same idea into one word of light: the light is not a dawn that ends the night but the thing that works in it.
The second half of the verse is a textual crux. The Greek reads ἀπὸ ὀρέων αἰωνίων, "from everlasting mountains." That suggests the translator's Hebrew had הַרְרֵי־עַד, the phrase of Hab 3:6, וַיִּתְפֹּצְצוּ הַרְרֵי־עַד, "the everlasting mountains were shattered."
A count of the form מֵהַרְרֵי helps decide the question. It occurs three times in the Bible. One is Num 23:7, מֵהַרְרֵי־קֶדֶם, "from the mountains of the east," so the "ancient mountains" type is real. Another is Song 4:8: מִמְּעֹנוֹת אֲרָיוֹת מֵהַרְרֵי נְמֵרִים, "from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards." That is exactly the pairing of den (מְעוֹנָה) and predators' mountains that vv. 3–5 of our psalm make. The traditional Hebrew text's "mountains of prey" is therefore not a scribal accident. It is a known collocation, and it keeps the lion in view: the hunter comes down from the high places where torn prey is dragged.
**Verse 6**
אֶשְׁתּוֹלְלוּ אַבִּירֵי לֵב, נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם; וְלֹא־מָצְאוּ כָל־אַנְשֵׁי־חַיִל יְדֵיהֶם.
> The stout-hearted were stripped; they slumbered their sleep; and all the men of valor could not find their hands.
The first word is spelled like no other. Where Hebrew would normally write **הִ**שְׁתּוֹלְלוּ, the psalm writes **אֶ**שְׁתּוֹלְלוּ, with an *aleph* in place of the *he*, an Aramaic habit. Rashi compares אֶתְחַבַּר, "joined himself" (2 Chr 20:35). The form turns the verb back on its subject: these men did something to themselves.
What did they do? Radak derives the word from שָׁלָל, "plunder": the plunderers became plunder. Rashi derives it from שׁוֹלָל, "senseless" (Job 12:17): they went mad "like fools who err." I take the plunder reading, with the madness heard underneath. The army that came to strip Jerusalem is itself stripped, the reversal Isaiah curses in advance: הוֹי שׁוֹדֵד וְאַתָּה לֹא שָׁדוּד, "woe to you, despoiler, who were never despoiled" (Isa 33:1).
The sound sets the enemy against God. God in v. 5 is `'ad-**D**IYR`, "majestic." These men are `'ab-**B**IY-rēy`, "mighty." One letter separates the words and the power gap between them is total.
The phrase נָמוּ שְׁנָתָם, "they slumbered their sleep," occurs in this form in only one other place: נָמוּ רֹעֶיךָ מֶלֶךְ אַשּׁוּר יִשְׁכְּנוּ אַדִּירֶיךָ, "your shepherds slumber, O king of Assyria; your nobles lie still" (Nah 3:18). Nahum, the Bible's anti-Assyrian poem, uses the psalm's vocabulary thickly elsewhere too: God גּוֹעֵר בַּיָּם, "rebukes the sea" (1:4, compare v. 7); he mocks אַיֵּה מְעוֹן אֲרָיוֹת, "where is the lions' den?" (2:12, compare v. 3). In Nahum the Assyrian nobles are called אַדִּירִים. The psalm takes that word and gives it to God (v. 5), leaving the enemy only the slumber.
We cannot tell which text borrowed from which. The overlap still gives the Assyrian reading a footing independent of the Book of Kings. It also sharpens a contrast with another psalm: הִנֵּה לֹא־יָנוּם וְלֹא יִישָׁן שׁוֹמֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל, "behold, the Guardian of Israel neither slumbers nor sleeps" (Ps 121:4). The contest is decided by who stays awake. The sleep, moreover, is the kind no one wakes from: וְיָשְׁנוּ שְׁנַת־עוֹלָם וְלֹא יָקִיצוּ, "they shall sleep an everlasting sleep and not wake" (Jer 51:39).
The last clause has no parallel in the Bible. The warriors do not fail to *use* their hands; they fail to *find* them. The hand, the body's means of acting on the world, has gone missing from its owner.
The nearest scene in Western art is the climax of Mozart's *Don Giovanni* (1787, libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte). The libertine has invited to supper the stone statue of the man he killed. The statue comes and asks for his hand:
> COMMENDATORE: Dammi la mano in pegno!
> DON GIOVANNI: Eccola! ... Ohimè!
> Che gelo è questo mai!
>
> "COMMENDATORE: Give me your hand as a pledge!
> DON GIOVANNI: Here it is! … Alas!
> What ice is this!"
The hand is where Da Ponte's bravado fails, and the failure is supernatural and physical at once: the grip freezes before the soul gives way. Yet Don Giovanni keeps his voice. He goes down shouting "No, no, ch'io non mi pento," "No, no, I do not repent." The psalm's warriors are not granted even that. In the whole of Psalm 76 no enemy speaks a single word, and that silence fits a poem in which the enemy's only verbs are forms of losing.
**Verse 7**
מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ, אֱלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב, נִרְדָּם וְרֶכֶב וָסוּס.
> At Your rebuke, God of Jacob, both chariot and horse sank into deep sleep.
Verse 6 gave the effect; only now does the poem name the cause, and the cause is one word. גְּעָרָה, "rebuke" or "blast," is the Bible's word for what God does to the sea: וַיִּגְעַר בְּיַם־סוּף וַיֶּחֱרָב, "He rebuked the Sea of Reeds and it dried up" (Ps 106:9); הֵן בְּגַעֲרָתִי אַחֲרִיב יָם, "behold, by My rebuke I dry up the sea" (Isa 50:2). The exact form מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ occurs in only one other place, at the moment the channels of the waters are laid bare: מִגַּעֲרָתְךָ יְהוָה מִנִּשְׁמַת רוּחַ אַפֶּךָ, "at Your rebuke, O LORD, at the blast of the breath of Your nostrils" (Ps 18:16). The weapon that emptied the sea is here aimed at cavalry. On why "chariot and horse," see the essay.
Malbim's word-notes catch the oddity of the scene. He first grades the verbs of sleep: תְּנוּמָה, light drowsing (v. 6's נָמוּ); then שֵׁנָה, sleep; then תַּרְדֵּמָה, the deep stupor of נִרְדָּם. Then he observes that this deepest sleep comes ע"י גערת אלהי יעקב, הפך מגערת אנשים שמעיר מן השינה, "through the rebuke of the God of Jacob — the reverse of a human rebuke, which wakes a person from sleep." A shout is the one sound we expect to rouse a sleeper, and here God's shout puts men to sleep. (Malbim adds, in his main commentary, that horses are normally wakeful at night, so their falling asleep is its own portent.)
This deep sleep has a history. On the night David crept into Saul's camp and lifted the king's spear from beside his head, no one stirred, כִּי תַּרְדֵּמַת יְהוָה נָפְלָה עֲלֵיהֶם, "for a deep sleep from the LORD had fallen upon them" (1 Sam 26:12). That is the pattern here: a God-sent stupor during which weapons are taken away without resistance.
Ibn Ezra notes that "God of Jacob" answers the "Israel" of v. 2. A further reason for the title in this particular verse can be offered, though not proven. In Jacob's blessing of Joseph, Joseph's *bow* stays firm and "the arms of his *hands*" are strengthened מִידֵי אֲבִיר יַעֲקֹב, "by the hands of the Mighty One of Jacob" (Gen 49:24). Psalm 76 has just reversed each of these terms for the enemy: the bow broken (v. 4), the אַבִּירֵי unable to find their hands (v. 6). The very next words give "Jacob" back to its proper owner.
The overlap is in the consonants only; the Masoretes vocalize the divine אֲבִיר differently from the human אַבִּיר. Still, the Asaph guild had roots in the Joseph tribes, and for such a guild the echo would not have been hard to hear.
**Verse 8**
אַתָּה — נוֹרָא אַתָּה! וּמִי־יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ מֵאָז אַפֶּךָ?
> You — awesome are You! And who can stand before You once Your anger is roused?
The pronoun אַתָּה, "You," stands on both sides of נוֹרָא, "awesome," wrapping the adjective the way a frame surrounds a picture. Ibn Ezra reads the doubling as exclusive: "You alone are awesome." The rhetorical question that follows expects the answer "no one." The verb, "stand before," is the posture of a party in court or at a royal audience, and it prepares for the courtroom of vv. 9–10.
The last two words, מֵאָז אַפֶּךָ, read literally "from then, Your nostril" (anger in Hebrew is a flaring nose). Most take them as Rashi does: "from the moment You are angry." Malbim takes a real risk and reads "Your anger" as a proper name, Assyria, which Isaiah calls הוֹי אַשּׁוּר שֵׁבֶט אַפִּי, "Woe, Assyria, rod of My anger" (Isa 10:5):
אשור שהיה שבט אפו של ה'... מאז ששלח ה' את אפו על הארץ, לא היה ממלכה שתעמוד לפניו, "Assyria, which was the rod of the LORD's anger… from the time the LORD sent His anger out over the earth, no kingdom could stand before it."
On this reading, "who can stand?" is a sober summary of the eighth century: empire after empire fell to the instrument of God's anger. Verse 9 then brings the reversal, when the heavenly court turns on the instrument itself. Malbim's reading ties vv. 8 and 9 into one hinge and explains why a fearful earth would fall still: its fear had a name, and the verdict removed it.
Abraham ibn Ezra, better known to us as a grammarian than as a poet, took this very question into the Yom Kippur liturgy. In his piyyut *Lecha Eli Teshukati*, recited in Edot HaMizrach communities after Kol Nidrei, the worshiper confesses: וּמִי יַעֲמֹד לְפָנֶיךָ וּמִי יִהְיֶה תְמוּרָתִי, "and who can stand before You, and who will take my place?" The psalm's question expects the answer *no invader*. The piyyut's expects *not I*, and it immediately begins the alphabetical confession. The words stay the same; the people who cannot stand have changed.
**Verse 9**
מִשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמַעְתָּ דִּין; אֶרֶץ יָרְאָה וְשָׁקָטָה,
> From heaven You made judgment heard; the earth feared, and fell still,
The scene moves from the lair in Zion to heaven, and from weapons to a verdict. God does not fight; He makes a ruling heard. The consonants of "heaven" and "made heard" chime (`mi**sh**-**sh**ā-**M**A-yim hi**sh**-**M**Aʿ-tā`), as if the sky's name and the act of proclaiming were cut from the same material.
The Talmud found a puzzle in the second half and solved it with Sinai. Torah Temimah gathers the passages (b. Shabbat 88a; b. Avodah Zarah 3a). King Hezekiah asks: אם יראה למה שקטה ואם שקטה למה יראה, "if it feared, why was it still? And if it was still, why did it fear?" Fear and calm seem to cancel each other. He answers: בתחילה יראה ולבסוף שקטה, "first it feared, and in the end it was still."
What was the earth afraid of? Resh Lakish supplies the answer from an irregularity in Genesis. Of all the days of creation, only the sixth has the definite article: יוֹם הַשִּׁשִּׁי, "*the* sixth day" (Gen 1:31). He reads it as pointing to a particular sixth, the sixth of Sivan, the day of the giving of the Torah. On that reading, God made the world's existence conditional: if Israel accepted the Torah, well and good; if not, creation would return to chaos. The earth trembled until Sinai and calmed when Israel said yes.
Why Sinai, of all possible events? The diction explains it. Moses describes Sinai in these words: מִן־הַשָּׁמַיִם הִשְׁמִיעֲךָ אֶת־קֹלוֹ... וְעַל־הָאָרֶץ, "from heaven He made you hear His voice… and on earth" (Deut 4:36). The verb for "make heard" is the same, the "from heaven" is the same, and earth stands opposite heaven in both. The rabbis heard Sinai's vocabulary in the psalm and read the earth's fear as fear for its own existence.
The second verb carries a formula. When Israel's conquest was complete, the book of Joshua records וְהָאָרֶץ שָׁקְטָה מִמִּלְחָמָה, "and the land had rest from war" (Josh 14:15). The last word of that formula, מִלְחָמָה, "war," is what God broke in v. 4, just before the *Selah*. Its opening words return here: אֶרֶץ... שָׁקָטָה. The psalm splits the formula across its two panels, and the break changes its meaning. The rest now follows a war that God ended, not Joshua. And אֶרֶץ may mean "the land" or "the earth."
The commentators divide on exactly this point. Rashi hears Eretz Israel, finally calm after fearing Sennacherib. Radak hears "the lands of the nations," quieted from further war against Israel. The widening in the next verse settles it for me: the whole earth.
**Verse 10**
בְּקוּם־לַמִּשְׁפָּט אֱלֹהִים, לְהוֹשִׁיעַ כָּל־עַנְוֵי־אֶרֶץ. סֶלָה.
> when God rose up for judgment, to save all the lowly of the earth. Selah.
The essay dealt with the scope of "all the lowly of the earth" and with the commentators who narrowed it. What remains is the direction of the verdict. A judge rises to pronounce sentence, and one expects punishment. This Judge rises "to save."
Malbim draws out what that purpose implies for the verse before:
כי המשפט האלהי לא יבא לתת תוקף להעריץ העושק וחומס, רק להושיע את הענוים הנדכאים תחת ידו, "for divine judgment never comes to give force to the tyrant who oppresses and robs, but only to save the lowly crushed beneath his hand."
That, he says, is why the earth, hearing that God had risen to judge, שקטה מיראתה, "was stilled from its fear." The silence of v. 9 therefore has two faces. It is terror to the tyrant and relief to everyone else, because the earth has heard which side the court is on.
The whole cluster of vv. 2–13 answers, point by point, one of the Psalter's great complaints about injustice, the acrostic that runs across Psalms 9–10:
- God is נוֹדַע... מִשְׁפָּט עָשָׂה, "known… by the judgment He executed" (9:17; compare v. 2 and v. 10 here);
- He is begged קוּמָה יְהוָה, "Arise, O LORD" (9:20; compare בְּקוּם);
- He is asked שִׁיתָה יְהוָה מוֹרָה לָהֶם, "put terror into them" (9:21; compare v. 12's מוֹרָא);
- He is praised as יֹשֵׁב צִיּוֹן, "dweller in Zion" (9:12);
- the wicked lurks כְּאַרְיֵה בְסֻכֹּה, "like a lion in his covert," to seize the poor (10:9);
- the prayer ends with the desire of the עֲנָוִים, "the lowly," being heard, בַּל־יוֹסִיף עוֹד לַעֲרֹץ אֱנוֹשׁ מִן־הָאָרֶץ, "so that mortals of the earth may terrify no more" (10:17–18).
Malbim, glossing our v. 13, quotes that last line directly. The two psalms belong to different collections, and I would not claim that one was written in answer to the other. But read side by side, Psalm 76 gives the lament's plea as already done.
The phrase itself has prophetic company. Zephaniah summons בַּקְּשׁוּ אֶת־יְהוָה כָּל־עַנְוֵי הָאָרֶץ, "seek the LORD, all you lowly of the earth" (Zeph 2:3). Isaiah's ideal king judges בְּמִישׁוֹר לְעַנְוֵי־אָרֶץ, "with equity for the lowly of the earth" (Isa 11:4).
Tukaram, the seventeenth-century saint-poet of Maharashtra, wrote in the devotional *abhang* form for the Varkari pilgrims of the god Vitthal. He tied holiness to the same test:
> जे का रंजले गांजले ।
> त्यासी म्हणे जो आपुले ॥
> तोचि साधु ओळखावा ।
> देव तेथेचि जाणावा ॥
>
> "Those who are tormented and oppressed —
> whoever calls them his own,
> know him to be the true saint;
> know that God is right there."
Tukaram's lines depend on "whoever… know him": one recognizes the divine by where it takes its stand. The psalm makes the same argument about God's anger. The terror of vv. 5–9 is identified by its purpose, and the purpose is the lowly. The difference is where the tenderness sits. Tukaram finds God in the one who embraces the sufferer. The psalmist finds Him in a Judge who stands up. One is a saint's embrace, the other a court's ruling, and both are measured by the oppressed.
**Verse 11**
כִּי־חֲמַת אָדָם תּוֹדֶךָּ; שְׁאֵרִית חֵמֹת תַּחְגֹּר.
> For human fury will acknowledge You; with the last of the furies You gird Yourself.
This is the psalm's hardest verse. The first line is a paradox that Rashi makes concrete with Nebuchadnezzar. The king's rage heated the furnace for Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah; when they walked out, the same king said בְּרִיךְ אֱלָהֲהוֹן, "blessed be their God" (Dan 3:28). The fury itself turned into praise.
The second line is the puzzle. Rashi offers two readings. The first takes תַּחְגֹּר from later rabbinic Hebrew, where the verb means to catch or hold back. The laws of the slaughtering knife speak of a nick in the blade deep enough כְּדֵי שֶׁתַּחְגֹּר בּוֹ הַצִּפֹּרֶן, "that the fingernail catches on it." So here: the remainder of the wicked are checked and held back from showing their wrath. Rashi adds that he heard that Rabbi Eleazar the Gaon cited our verse as proof of that very legal usage. A psalm of cosmic war ends up clarifying a butcher's inspection of his blade. Rashi's second reading is the literal one: girding as with a belt. "The girding of all wrath is reserved for You," since human fury has proved to be nothing.
Others hold on to the belt and ask what it does. Ibn Ezra has God "tie up" the leftover wrath and keep it in His possession, "to release it as He wishes." Malbim's word-notes turn the sentence around and make human fury the subject: it is human wrath that restrains the remainder of God's.
The Greek translator heard a different verb altogether: ἑορτάσει σοι, "shall keep festival to You." That means reading תָּחֹג, "celebrate," for תַּחְגֹּר, "gird," and the remnant of fury becomes a festival crowd. The reading looks less like guesswork beside Zechariah's vision of the survivors of the siege: וְהָיָה כָּל־הַנּוֹתָר מִכָּל־הַגּוֹיִם הַבָּאִים עַל־יְרוּשָׁלָ͏ִם וְעָלוּ... לָחֹג אֶת־חַג הַסֻּכּוֹת, "and all who are left of all the nations that came against Jerusalem shall go up… to keep the festival of Sukkot" (Zech 14:16). Whether the translator had Zechariah in mind cannot be known; Zechariah's word for "left" is not our שְׁאֵרִית. But the synagogue made the connection for him by reading Zechariah 14 on the same first day of Sukkot on which this psalm is sung.
I keep the Hebrew belt. It fits the royal self-clothing of עֹז הִתְאַזָּר, "He has girded Himself with strength" (Ps 93:1). It also completes the story of the verbs. Human rage, the poem's last remaining human energy, is either converted into praise or worn by God as a trophy. Either way it is no longer anyone else's weapon.
Emendations that read "Edom" for אָדָם or the city Hamath for חֲמַת are clever and unnecessary. The Edom idea, however, points toward the text the liturgical poets heard beneath the coda; see v. 13.
**Verse 12**
נִדְרוּ וְשַׁלְּמוּ לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם; כָּל־סְבִיבָיו יֹבִילוּ שַׁי לַמּוֹרָא.
> Vow, and pay, to YHWH your God; let all around Him bring tribute to the Awesome One.
Here, and only here, the personal name YHWH appears. The likeliest explanation is that the vow formula was too fixed for the Elohistic editor to touch, the same phrase found in the law כִּי־תִדֹּר נֶדֶר לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ, "when you vow a vow to YHWH your God" (Deut 23:22). The effect is that the name arrives at the moment of obligation, together with the psalm's only direct "you," plural and commanded. The worshipers' verb שַׁלְּמוּ, "pay," returns to the שָׁלֵם, "Salem," of v. 3: peace is answered with payment. Asaph's other great psalm on sacrifice asks the same thing: וְשַׁלֵּם לְעֶלְיוֹן נְדָרֶיךָ, "and pay your vows to the Most High" (Ps 50:14).
Who are כָּל־סְבִיבָיו, "all around Him"? Radak reasons from geography: God dwells in the land, so the nations around the land are "around Him." Rashi points to Isaiah's Egyptian cities that swear allegiance to the LORD of Hosts (Isa 19:18). The closest parallel in wording comes from the heavenly court: אֵל נַעֲרָץ בְּסוֹד־קְדֹשִׁים רַבָּה וְנוֹרָא עַל־כָּל־סְבִיבָיו, "a God dreaded in the council of the holy ones, great and awesome above all around Him" (Ps 89:8). Psalm 76 uses the same pair, "all around Him" and "awesome," and sets them beside "the kings of the earth." Perhaps the poet has moved council language down to earth, so that the tribute-bearing kings take the place the angels hold in Psalm 89.
The word שַׁי, "tribute," occurs only three times, and each time it is brought to God: יוּבַל־שַׁי לַיהוָה צְבָאוֹת, "tribute shall be brought to the LORD of Hosts" (Isa 18:7); לְךָ יוֹבִילוּ מְלָכִים שָׁי, "kings shall bring You tribute" (Ps 68:30).
Malbim turns the divine title לַמּוֹרָא into a political argument. His word-notes point out that nouns formed with a prefixed מ often name a place (מִקְדָּשׁ, sanctuary; מוֹשָׁב, seat), so מוֹרָא is "the place and source of fear." The argument follows:
וה' הוא המורא, ר"ל המקור של היראה, שמשם קבל אשור את היראה... וא"כ למה תובילו שי לאשור, הובילו שי אל מקור שמשם בא היראה, "the LORD is the *Mora*, the source of fear, from whom Assyria received the fear it cast… so why bring tribute to Assyria? Bring tribute to the source from which the fear came."
Tribute had been flowing to the empire. The psalm redirects it to the source of the empire's power, and Nineveh loses its income along with its army.
**Verse 13**
יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים; נוֹרָא לְמַלְכֵי־אָרֶץ.
> He prunes the spirit of princes; He is awesome to the kings of the earth.
Rashi takes יִבְצֹר as "lessen," from לֹא־יִבָּצֵר מֵהֶם, "nothing will be withheld from them" (Gen 11:6): God diminishes the princes' arrogance. Ibn Ezra hears the harvest: the verb is the vintager's, as in כִּי תִבְצֹר כַּרְמְךָ, "when you gather the grapes of your vineyard" (Deut 24:21). I hear both, with the vineyard in front. God's own breath knocked out armies in v. 7. Now the princes' breath, their רוּחַ (spirit, pride, life), is snipped off like a cluster. It is an agricultural ending to a war poem, and there is no effort in it.
The liturgical poets heard more. Two independent compositions place יִבְצֹר רוּחַ נְגִידִים beside Isaiah 63's warrior מִבָּצְרָה... חֲמוּץ בְּגָדִים, "crimsoned in garments from Bozrah" (Isa 63:1): the Ashkenazi Rosh Hashanah *yotzer* and a Hasidic Shabbat *zemer* that names both Edom and Bozrah (quoted in the liturgical section).
They were hearing a real cluster of shared language. Isaiah 63 shares with our coda:
- לְהוֹשִׁיעַ, "to save" (63:1; compare v. 10);
- repeated חֵמָה and אַף, "fury" and "anger" (63:3, 5, 6; compare vv. 8, 11);
- clothing (compare the girding of v. 11);
- a winepress (compare the vintage here);
- Edom, whose name sits one vowel from אָדָם in v. 11;
- above all, a victory with no human ally: וְאַבִּיט וְאֵין עֹזֵר, "I looked, and there was no one to help" (63:5).
That last line could stand as a motto for Psalm 76. The poets' pairing of the two texts is reading, not decoration.
Radak's comment on this final verse is an admission:
כבר במכלול בשרש חגר פירשתי זה המזמור על מחנה אשור כשבא על ירושלים, אבל עתה נראה לי לטעם המזמור אשר למעלה ממנו לפרש אותו על גוג מגוג, "In the *Mikhlol*, under the root חגר, I already explained this psalm as concerning the Assyrian camp when it came against Jerusalem; but now, given the sense of the psalm that precedes it, it seems to me it should be explained of Gog and Magog."
The great grammarian changed his mind in print and left the earlier view in his own grammar book, where a reader looking up "gird" would still find Sennacherib. His reason was sequence. Psalm 75 promises a coming judgment, so Psalm 76, he reasoned, must describe it. His new reading cost him the forced future tense of v. 3's "and it was." The poem's own tenses explain his dilemma. The past-tense story of vv. 3–10 supports the Assyrian reading; the future-tense coda supports Gog. Meiri, facing the same fork, lets the reciter choose: the speaker, he says, may interpret it as he wishes.
The poem's frame now closes. It opened with נוֹדָע בִּיהוּדָה, "known in Judah," and ends with נוֹרָא לְמַלְכֵי־אָרֶץ, "awesome to the kings of the earth." The first word of the body and the second-to-last word of the poem differ by a single letter, ד against ר, and the distance between them is the whole psalm's widening from one tribe to the world. The last word is אָרֶץ, "earth," its third appearance in five verses.
One documented scene shows what happens when this poem enters a human mouth. On 1 June 1679, an outlawed field-meeting of Scottish Covenanters near Loudoun Hill saw government dragoons under John Graham of Claverhouse riding toward them. Covenanter tradition remembers that before engaging, they sang Psalm 76 in the Scottish metrical version:
> In Judah's land God is well known,
> his name's in Israel great:
> In Salem is his tabernacle,
> in Sion is his seat.
Then they charged and routed the dragoons. For these men, persecuted by a king who claimed authority over their church, the psalm's promise that God "prunes the spirit of princes" was a political creed. Yet the scene holds a quiet irony that a close reading brings out. They sang a poem in which no human being fights, and then they fought. Every community that has sung Psalm 76 has done what Rashi and Radak did on paper: supplied the missing "we." The psalm itself never does. It leaves the victory with its only Actor and gives everyone else, the Covenanters included, only the verbs of thanks.
