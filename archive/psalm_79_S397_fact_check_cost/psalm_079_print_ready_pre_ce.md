# Commentary on Psalm 79
---
## Introduction
Among the eighty-odd etchings in Goya's *Disasters of War* is plate 18. Two survivors stand over a heap of stripped bodies, and the title underneath is *Enterrar y callar*, "Bury them and keep quiet." It sums up what is usually left to the survivors of a massacre. Psalm 79 was written by people who could do neither. They could not bury their dead, and they would not keep quiet. The whole poem grows out of one short line at the end of its third verse: וְאֵין קוֹבֵר, "and there is none to bury."
The plot is quickly told. Nations have entered God's land, defiled the temple, and made Jerusalem into heaps of rubble. The bodies of the faithful lie out for the birds and the wild animals, and their blood has been "poured out like water all around Jerusalem." The neighbors laugh (vv. 1–4). Then the speaker turns on God with a question: "How long, O Lord, will You be angry — forever?" (v. 5). He asks God to pour that anger on the nations instead (vv. 6–7). He asks God not to hold "former iniquities" against them, to help them and atone for their sins "for Your name's sake" (vv. 8–9). He asks God to avenge the spilled blood before the eyes of the nations, to hear the groan of the prisoners, and to pay the neighbors back sevenfold (vv. 10–12). The poem ends with a vow: "And we, Your people, the flock of Your pasture, will thank You forever; from generation to generation we will recount Your praise" (v. 13). Two frames hold this together. The neighbors' contempt appears in verse 4 and is returned to them in verse 12. God's anger "forever" (לָנֶצַח) in verse 5 is answered by praise "forever" (לְעוֹלָם) in verse 13.
That summary leaves out the poem's most unsettling feature, which only shows when you ask who does things in it.
## A God with no past
Count the verbs. The nations have all of them. They "came" (בָּאוּ), "defiled," "made" Jerusalem into ruins, "gave" the corpses as food, "poured out" the blood, "devoured" Jacob, "laid waste" his pasture, and "reproached" God. Every completed action in the psalm belongs to them. The "we" of the poem has two past-tense verbs, and neither is an action. "We have become a reproach" (הָיִינוּ, v. 4) and "we have been brought very low" (דַּלּוֹנוּ, v. 8) describe things done to them. God is never the subject of a past-tense verb anywhere in the poem. God is given a present anger ("how long will You be angry") and a long run of imperatives: pour, help, rescue, atone, spare, repay. The community does not even confess with a verb. There is no "we sinned," only the nouns "iniquities" and "our sins."
This matters because the neighboring laments of the same collection of Asaph psalms do the opposite. Psalm 74, this psalm's twin, stops in the middle of its catastrophe to recite what God once did: "You split the sea… You crushed the heads of Leviathan." Psalm 80 tells how God brought a vine out of Egypt. Psalm 44 opens with "our fathers have told us" of God's deeds in the old days. Psalm 79 has no such memory. In its story God has done nothing that can be narrated. The only history of God it allows itself comes in compressed epithets and borrowed phrases: "God of our salvation" (v. 9), and כְּגֹדֶל זְרוֹעֲךָ, "by the greatness of Your arm" (v. 11), lifted from the Song of the Sea, where that arm froze the nations like stone. A hearer who follows the poem as a scene sees enemies everywhere and God nowhere in it. God appears only on the other end of the speaker's sentences.
The poet made this happen deliberately, and the proof is a borrowing. Verse 4 is usually called a verbatim quotation of Psalm 44:14, but it is not quite one. Psalm 44 says תְּשִׂימֵנוּ חֶרְפָּה לִשְׁכֵנֵינוּ, "**You** make us a reproach to our neighbors." Psalm 79 says הָיִינוּ חֶרְפָּה, "we have **become** a reproach," with no one doing it. Two verses earlier, Psalm 44 had God "give us like sheep for food" (תִּתְּנֵנוּ כְּצֹאן מַאֲכָל). Psalm 79 keeps the verb and the food but hands the action to the enemy: "**they** gave the corpses of Your servants as food." The same move produces the verb of verse 1: in Psalm 44 God "sets" us as a reproach, and here the nations "set" Jerusalem in ruins. The verbs that an older lament assigned to God are taken from him one by one and charged to the nations. The poet concedes that God is angry, but he does not let God be the one who killed them.
If God has done nothing, the speaker cannot thank God and cannot quite accuse God, so he argues. That is why nearly every noun in the poem carries the suffix "Your": **Your** inheritance, **Your** holy temple, **Your** servants, **Your** faithful, **Your** jealousy, **Your** compassion, **Your** name three times, **Your** arm, **Your** people, **Your** pasture. The disaster is described as damage to God's property. The poem reads less like a cry of grief than like a claim filed by a tenant on behalf of an absent owner.
## Blood left uncovered
That legal framing explains why the poem cannot let the bodies be buried. In the first audience's world an exposed corpse was not just a horror. It was the formal penalty for breaking an oath. Esarhaddon's succession treaty (672 BCE) threatens the vassal who breaks faith: may Ninurta "give your flesh to eagles and vultures to feed upon." Deuteronomy 28:26 puts the same curse into Israel's own covenant. Psalm 79:2 describes that sentence carried out, and its victims are called "Your servants" and "Your faithful." The ancient hearer would have registered the contradiction at once: the punishment for traitors had fallen on the loyal. This is why the poem has to confess at its center, and also why the confession is so oddly worded. The rabbis felt the strain. Sanhedrin 47a splits the parallel pair: "Your faithful" were the truly pious, while "Your servants" were sinners who became servants by dying. A Maccabean author felt it from the other direction. When the sixty Hasideans were murdered in 161 BCE, 1 Maccabees 7:17 quoted the psalm as fulfilled scripture, but only from its second half: "the flesh of Your *ḥăsîdîm*." The pun works only in Hebrew, and he left out the line about the "servants."
The blood carries the argument further. The Torah requires that the blood of a hunted animal be poured out and covered: וְשָׁפַךְ אֶת־דָּמוֹ וְכִסָּהוּ בֶּעָפָר, "he shall pour out its blood and cover it with dust" (Lev 17:13). Lamentations Rabbah sets that verse beside this psalm. Even a deer's blood is covered, and here human blood lies open "and there is none to bury." Ibn Ezra hears the killers' reasoning in the phrase "like water": they poured it out like water, which belongs to nobody, on the assumption that no one would come to claim it. The psalm makes that assumption its target. Ezekiel 24:7–8 describes the mechanism. Jerusalem's own bloodguilt was set "on the bare rock, not poured on the ground to cover it with dust," precisely "to raise up wrath, to take vengeance" (לְהַעֲלוֹת חֵמָה לִנְקֹם נָקָם). Blood left uncovered keeps calling for an answer. Psalm 79 uses the same three terms in the same causal chain but turns it around. The blood "poured out" in verse 3 becomes the warrant for "pour out Your wrath" in verse 6 and for "vengeance for the poured-out blood" in verse 10. The bodies the poem cannot bury become evidence that it will not let anyone cover up.
## The bargain at the center
At the hinge of the poem, verse 5 asks two things at once. "How long" assumes there is an end, and "forever" fears there is none. The rabbis explained the question with Lamentations 4:11, כִּלָּה ה׳ אֶת־חֲמָתוֹ, "the Lord has spent His fury," which goes on to say He poured out His anger and kindled a fire in Zion. Rashi uses it to answer why a poem about the destruction is headed מִזְמוֹר, "a song," and not "a lament." God spent His anger on wood and stones and did not make an end of His children. Malbim draws the conclusion the psalm itself is reaching for. Anger that has been spent ought to stop, and whatever still burns belongs somewhere else. Lamentations ends on the same turn: Zion's punishment is finished, and the iniquity of Edom is now remembered.
The center is shaped by another psalm. Psalm 89 ends with a king's lament: "Remember, O Lord, the reproach of Your servants, which I carry in my bosom," and "Where are Your former kindnesses?" Psalm 79 takes those words and turns each one around. "Remember" becomes "do not remember" (אַל־תִּזְכָּר). "Former kindnesses" become "former iniquities" (עֲוֹנֹת רִאשֹׁנִים). Reproach carried "in my bosom" is to be returned "into **their** bosom" (אֶל־חֵיקָם). This psalm has no king left to absorb the insult, so the insult goes straight to God: "the reproach with which they reproached **You**, O Lord." It even keeps Psalm 89's divine name, Adonai, at that point.
This is the place to say plainly what it is like from the inside. When people cannot bury their dead, what wears them down is less the enemy than their own uselessness. The last thing that could have been done was not done, and they no longer trust their own right to ask for anything. The speaker's request is small and specific: let Your compassion "come to meet us" before the record of our sins arrives. The verb יְקַדְּמוּנוּ elsewhere describes snares of death confronting someone (Ps 18:6). Here it asks mercy to get there first.
## The psalm in other mouths
Because the poem's case rests on "Your inheritance," it has passed easily into the hands of anyone who feels robbed. In 1213 Innocent III's crusade bull *Quia maior* ordered this psalm, *Deus venerunt gentes*, to be sung daily at Mass across Latin Christendom for the recovery of Jerusalem. In the same centuries the Jews of the Rhineland put verse 10 into *Av HaRachamim*, their memorial for communities massacred by crusaders, and verses 6–7 into the Haggadah, said as the door is opened for Elijah. The same words were being said on both sides of the slaughter, with each side claiming to be the one dispossessed. In 1589 William Byrd, a Catholic in Elizabeth's England, published a large setting of verses 1–4 that is widely heard as his lament for Edmund Campion, hanged, drawn and quartered at Tyburn in 1581. Malbim notes that the psalm moves from נִבְלַת, the intact "corpse," to בְּשַׂר, "flesh," because the bodies of the faithful had been cut into pieces. A recusant composer seems to have caught exactly that detail.
At the Seder the verses fit a second way. The psalm is full of the Exodus: "inheritance" and "pasture" come from the Song of the Sea, and so does "the greatness of Your arm." Its central petition, "let it be made known among the nations" (יִוָּדַע בַּגּוֹיִם), echoes the refrain of the plagues, "and Egypt shall know." Pharaoh had said, "I do not know the Lord." The psalm describes "nations that do not know You." On the night that tells of the first exodus, Jews recite the psalm that asks for another.
## From the gravedigger to the teller
Listen finally to the sounds. The empty participle קוֹבֵר, *qō-VĒR*, "one who buries," closes verse 3. The poem then answers it with a chain of words ending in the same stressed *-ēr*: מַהֵר, *ma-HĒR*, "hasten" (v. 8), כַּפֵּר, *khap-PĒR*, "atone" (v. 9), הוֹתֵר, *hō-THĒR*, "spare" (v. 11). The chain ends on the last verb of the psalm: נְסַפֵּר, *nə-sap-PĒR*, "we will recount." Verse 11 is borrowed from Psalm 102:21, where the condemned are to be "released" (לְפַתֵּחַ). The poet replaced that with the rare הוֹתֵר, a word that also means "leave over," keep a remnant alive, and that fits the chain. The verse is modeled on "the one who buries," and the person absent from verse 3 is replaced by a community that promises to tell. The Hebrew has no "then" in the last verse, only וַאֲנַחְנוּ, "and we," spoken against "they": the nations who devoured and the neighbors who mocked. The flock that was "food" in Psalm 44 is now the flock of God's "pasture." The generations broken by the slaughter are put back as לְדוֹר וָדֹר, "from generation to generation."
Goya's caption names what survivors are usually made to do: bury the dead and stay silent. Psalm 79 leaves the blood uncovered because it is evidence, and it makes telling the duty of the survivors. Jewish liturgy kept that last verse and sets it, by itself, in the evening service of many communities, where it is said night after night by people who mostly no longer know it was the last line of a poem about unburied dead.

#### Full psalm
The Edot HaMizrach siddur prints the whole psalm in two places. The first is Tikkun Chatzot, the midnight lament, in its section called Tikkun Rachel. There Psalm 79 follows directly on Psalm 137, so the curse on "daughter of Babylon" runs straight into מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף, אֱלֹקִים בָּאוּ גוֹיִם בְּנַחֲלָתֶךָ, "A psalm of Asaph. God, nations have come into Your inheritance." After it comes Lamentations 5, זְכֹר ה׳ מֶה־הָיָה לָנוּ, "Remember, O Lord, what has befallen us." The second is the weekday Shacharit section of special psalms for the Song of the Day, where a rubric assigns it to one fast: בשבעה עשר בתמוז אומרים מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף, "On the Seventeenth of Tammuz one says: A psalm of Asaph." That day commemorates the breach of Jerusalem's walls, and the psalm opens with the nations already inside. The pairing is old. Tractate Soferim (18:3) sets Psalm 79 and Psalm 137 for the Ninth of Av, and *Ma'avar Yabbok* asks that the same two psalms be said after meals in "these days of judgment." In every one of these settings the psalm keeps its plain sense, as a lament over the Temple.
#### Key verses
The psalm's best-known lines in the prayer book are the two at its center. Vv. 8–9 close the weekday Tachanun, morning and afternoon, in the Ashkenaz, Sefard (Hasidic) and Chabad rites and in the Edot HaMizrach Vidui: אַל תִּזְכָּר לָנוּ עֲוֹנוֹת רִאשׁוֹנִים מַהֵר יְקַדְּמוּנוּ רַחֲמֶיךָ כִּי דַלּוֹנוּ מְאֹד, "Do not remember against us the iniquities of those before us; let Your compassion hurry to meet us, for we have been brought very low." Then comes עָזְרֵנוּ אֱלֹקֵי יִשְׁעֵנוּ, "Help us, God of our salvation," and the reader begins Kaddish. The same pair ends every day of the Lithuanian and Polish Ashkenaz selichot and the Edot HaMizrach selichot. On Yom Kippur it stands in the run of verses after the Thirteen Attributes. A plea that rests on God's name rather than on Israel's merit has become the standard last word before Kaddish.
V. 10 is put to three uses. In *Av HaRachamim*, the Shabbat memorial for martyred communities said after the Torah reading, it is quoted whole as the proof from the Writings, after proofs from the Torah and the Prophets. *Avinu Malkeinu* condenses it: אָבִינוּ מַלְכֵּנוּ נְקוֹם לְעֵינֵינוּ נִקְמַת דַּם־עֲבָדֶיךָ הַשָּׁפוּךְ, "Our Father, our King, avenge before our eyes the poured-out blood of Your servants." The litany *Aseh lema'an* includes "act for the sake of the poured-out blood of Your servants."
Vv. 6–7, שְׁפֹךְ חֲמָתְךָ, "Pour out Your fury," are said at the Seder as the door is opened and Elijah's cup is poured. All the Haggadot searched follow the psalm's wording, not that of Jeremiah 10:25.
V. 13 has a separate liturgical life. Weekday Maariv in Ashkenaz and Sefard closes its sequence of verses beginning *Baruch Hashem le'olam* with וַאֲנַחְנוּ עַמְּךָ וְצֹאן מַרְעִיתֶךָ נוֹדֶה לְּךָ לְעוֹלָם, "And we, Your people and the flock of Your pasture, will thank You forever." The Ashkenaz and Sefard machzorim repeat the verse in Maariv at the close of Yom Kippur, and Edot HaMizrach says it in Shacharit after Hannah's Prayer. Taken out of the psalm, the vow of praise works as a doxology of its own, and nothing around it recalls the unburied dead in v. 3.
The uses of vv. 1, 2, 6, 11 and 12 are taken up in the notes on those verses.
#### Practical Kabbalah
*Shimush Tehillim*, the medieval manual of psalm-magic (first printed at Sabbioneta in 1551), prescribes this psalm for casting down or subduing one's enemies. The association is easy to follow. The psalm asks for wrath to be poured on the nations (v. 6), for vengeance to be seen before one's own eyes (v. 10), and for a sevenfold repayment (v. 12). A manual that sorts psalms by practical use would put it in the column for hostile neighbors.

## Psalm 79

1. מִזְמ֗וֹר לְאָ֫סָ֥ף אֱֽלֹקִ֡ים בָּ֤אוּ גוֹיִ֨ם ׀ בְּֽנַחֲלָתֶ֗ךָ טִ֭מְּאוּ אֶת־הֵיכַ֣ל קׇדְשֶׁ֑ךָ שָׂ֖מוּ אֶת־יְרוּשָׁלַ֣͏ִם לְעִיִּֽים׃‎		A psalm of Asaph.
O God, heathens have entered Your domain,
defiled Your holy temple,
and turned Jerusalem into ruins.
2. נָתְנ֡וּ אֶת־נִבְלַ֬ת עֲבָדֶ֗יךָ מַ֭אֲכָל לְע֣וֹף הַשָּׁמָ֑יִם בְּשַׂ֥ר חֲ֝סִידֶ֗יךָ לְחַיְתוֹ־אָֽרֶץ׃‎		They have left Your servants’ corpses
as food for the fowl of heaven,
and the flesh of Your faithful for the wild beasts.
3. שָׁפְכ֬וּ דָמָ֨ם ׀ כַּמַּ֗יִם סְֽבִ֘יב֤וֹת יְֽרוּשָׁלָ֗͏ִם וְאֵ֣ין קוֹבֵֽר׃‎		Their blood was shed like water around Jerusalem,
with none to bury them.
4. הָיִ֣ינוּ חֶ֭רְפָּה לִשְׁכֵנֵ֑ינוּ לַ֥עַג וָ֝קֶ֗לֶס לִסְבִיבוֹתֵֽינוּ׃‎		We have become the butt of our neighbors,
the scorn and derision of those around us.
5. עַד־מָ֣ה ה׳ תֶּאֱנַ֣ף לָנֶ֑צַח תִּֽבְעַ֥ר כְּמוֹ־אֵ֝֗שׁ קִנְאָתֶֽךָ׃‎		How long, O LORD, will You be angry forever,
will Your indignation blaze like fire?
6. שְׁפֹ֤ךְ חֲמָתְךָ֗ אֶֽל־הַגּוֹיִם֮ אֲשֶׁ֢ר לֹא־יְדָ֫ע֥וּךָ וְעַ֥ל מַמְלָכ֑וֹת אֲשֶׁ֥ר בְּ֝שִׁמְךָ֗ לֹ֣א קָרָֽאוּ׃‎		Pour out Your fury on the nations that do not know You,
upon the kingdoms that do not invoke Your name,
7. כִּ֭י אָכַ֣ל אֶֽת־יַעֲקֹ֑ב וְֽאֶת־נָוֵ֥הוּ הֵשַֽׁמּוּ׃‎		for they have devoured Jacob
and desolated his home.
8. אַֽל־תִּזְכׇּר־לָנוּ֮ עֲוֺנֹ֢ת רִאשֹׁ֫נִ֥ים מַ֭הֵר יְקַדְּמ֣וּנוּ רַחֲמֶ֑יךָ כִּ֖י דַלּ֣וֹנוּ מְאֹֽד׃‎		Do not hold our former iniquities against us;
let Your compassion come swiftly toward us,
for we have sunk very low.
9. עׇזְרֵ֤נוּ ׀ אֱלֹ֘קֵ֤י יִשְׁעֵ֗נוּ עַֽל־דְּבַ֥ר כְּבֽוֹד־שְׁמֶ֑ךָ וְהַצִּילֵ֥נוּ וְכַפֵּ֥ר עַל־חַ֝טֹּאתֵ֗ינוּ לְמַ֣עַן שְׁמֶֽךָ׃‎		Help us, O God, our deliverer,

Save us and forgive our sin,
for the sake of Your name.

reprieve those condemned to death,
as befits Your great strength.
12. וְהָ֘שֵׁ֤ב לִשְׁכֵנֵ֣ינוּ שִׁ֭בְעָתַיִם אֶל־חֵיקָ֑ם חֶרְפָּ֘תָ֤ם אֲשֶׁ֖ר חֵרְפ֣וּךָ אֲדֹנָֽי׃‎		Pay back our neighbors sevenfold
for the abuse they have flung at You, O Lord.
13. וַאֲנַ֤חְנוּ עַמְּךָ֨ ׀ וְצֹ֥אן מַרְעִיתֶךָ֮ נ֤וֹדֶ֥ה לְּךָ֗ לְע֫וֹלָ֥ם לְד֥וֹר וָדֹ֑ר נְ֝סַפֵּ֗ר תְּהִלָּתֶֽךָ׃ {פ}‎		Then we, Your people,
the flock You shepherd,
shall glorify You forever;
for all time we shall tell Your praises.

## Verse-by-Verse Commentary
**Verse 1**
מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף; אֱלֹקִים, בָּאוּ גוֹיִם בְּנַחֲלָתֶךָ, טִמְּאוּ אֶת־הֵיכַל קָדְשֶׁךָ; שָׂמוּ אֶת־יְרוּשָׁלַםִ לְעִיִּים.
> A psalm of Asaph. God, nations have come into Your inheritance; they have defiled Your holy temple; they have made Jerusalem into heaps of rubble.
The heading מִזְמוֹר, "a song," over a poem about massacre has bothered readers for two thousand years. One answer looks past the midrash to how the music was performed. Meiri says the word names the instruments, not the mood, and that some instruments gladden while others make the heart tremble. He quotes a master musician telling his pupil, עורר מיתר הדאגה, "wake the string of worry." On this view a *mizmor* can be sung on any string, including grief.
The opening verb is plain: בָּאוּ, "they came." Prose would have said "invaded" or "besieged." The Alshich (*Romemot El*) asks why the verse bothers to report the entry at all, since the defiling and the ruin are what matter. He answers from the Pesikta. The Babylonians did not take the First Temple by their own strength. An angel set his feet on the wall and breached it, and he cried, "Let the enemies come and enter a house whose master is not in it," so that they could not boast, "a conquered city you conquered, a slain people you slew." Four angels with torches then set the fire. On this reading "they came" is the whole of what the nations accomplished: they walked in through a door already opened. Keep the verb in mind. Ten verses later the psalm uses it once more, for something that comes in the opposite direction.
The last word, לְעִיִּים, "into heaps," appears elsewhere only in Micah's oracle of doom: צִיּוֹן שָׂדֶה תֵחָרֵשׁ וִירוּשָׁלַםִ עִיִּין תִּהְיֶה, "Zion shall be plowed as a field, and Jerusalem shall become heaps" (Mic 3:12). The spelling here, however, is not Micah's. Micah has the Aramaic-looking plural עִיִּין. Our psalm has עִיִּים, the form in Jeremiah 26:18, where the same oracle is quoted a century later at Jeremiah's own trial. That context matters. In Jeremiah 26 the elders cite Micah to save Jeremiah's life. Micah threatened ruin, they say, and King Hezekiah "feared the Lord and entreated the Lord, and the Lord relented of the evil He had spoken" (26:19). In that telling Micah's "heaps" were a sentence called off by repentance. Read through Jeremiah's version, the psalm's first line reports that the threat once called off has now been carried out. That may be part of why a poem of outrage still makes room for confession in vv. 8–9. Hezekiah's remedy was known, and the community attempts it after the event.
The Greek translator did not see heaps at all. He wrote εἰς ὀπωροφυλάκιον, "into an orchard-watchman's hut," the word the Septuagint uses for Isaiah's "lodge in a cucumber field" (Isa 1:8), the image of Zion left standing alone. Ibn Ezra adds a darker reading of עִיִּים: the enemy dug Jerusalem up looking for hidden treasure.
The Greek tragedians knew the same scene from the god's side. Euripides opens *The Trojan Women* (415 BCE) with Poseidon standing over burning Troy, which he helped build:
> ἔρημα δʼ ἄλση καὶ θεῶν ἀνάκτορα
> φόνῳ καταρρεῖ …
> λείπω τὸ κλεινὸν Ἴλιον βωμούς τ᾽ ἐμούς·
> ἐρημία γὰρ πόλιν ὅταν λάβῃ κακή,
> νοσεῖ τὰ τῶν θεῶν οὐδὲ τιμᾶσθαι θέλει.
>
> Groves stand forsaken and temples of the gods run down with blood …
> I am leaving Ilium, that famous town, and my altars;
> for when dreary desolation seizes on a town,
> the worship of the gods decays and tends to lose respect.
(trans. E. P. Coleridge)
Both poems show a temple running with blood, and both rest on the same rule: a ruined city cannot honor its god. Poseidon draws the obvious conclusion and leaves. The psalm uses the same rule as an argument for God to stay, since God's honor is the thing that has been laid in heaps.
The opening verse is also said apart from the psalm. In the selichot for the Tenth of Tevet (Ashkenaz), for Asara B'Tevet and 20 Sivan (Sefard), and on the fourth of the Ten Days in the Lithuanian rite, it begins a run of "O God" pleas after the Thirteen Attributes: "For You, Lord, are good and forgiving… O God, nations have come into Your inheritance… O God, arrogant men have risen against us."
**Verse 2**
נָתְנוּ אֶת־נִבְלַת עֲבָדֶיךָ מַאֲכָל לְעוֹף הַשָּׁמָיִם, בְּשַׂר חֲסִידֶיךָ לְחַיְתוֹ־אָרֶץ.
> They have given the corpses of Your servants as food to the birds of the sky, the flesh of Your faithful ones to the wild beasts of the earth.
נִבְלָה, "carcass," belongs mainly to the law of animals: the carcass of an unclean beast that defiles whoever touches it (Lev 11; Deut 14:8). Its grammatical form here is singular, נִבְלַת עֲבָדֶיךָ, "the carcass of Your servants." A crowd of the dead has become one heap of carrion.
The second half quietly changes its source. Deuteronomy's curse gives the dead to "the beasts of the earth," בֶהֱמַת הָאָרֶץ, a word that usually means livestock. The psalm writes לְחַיְתוֹ־אָרֶץ, using the archaic form חַיְתוֹ found in the creation story (Gen 1:24) and in Psalm 104, where "every beast of the field" drinks from God's springs. That makes the predators wild and the phrasing old, as if the world's oldest animals had come to collect. Malbim sharpens the contrast: these are not field animals but scavengers fed with "flesh cut from the corpses of the faithful."
The Bible preserves one counter-scene, and the psalm's emptiness is clearer beside it. After David handed over Saul's grandsons to be impaled, their mother Rizpah spread sackcloth on the rock "from the beginning of the harvest until water fell on them from heaven, and she did not let the birds of the sky rest on them by day, nor the beasts of the field by night" (2 Sam 21:10). For a whole season one woman did, single-handed, what this verse says nobody did. Rizpah's watch is what the phrase "the birds of the sky… the beasts of the earth" leaves unsaid: there was no one to drive them off. It also explains why the next verse ends on an absence.
Radak reports his father's view of who "Your faithful" were. Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah were pious men of Jerusalem who were exiled, not killed. Others like them hid in their houses and could not show themselves in the streets. Some of the pious were saved from death and some were not, "all according to the judgment given by God." It is a sober answer. Radak does not claim that every victim was a sinner or that every one was a saint.
The verse has a long life in the selichot. The Lithuanian rite places it whole among the pleas of its seventh day. A selichah for the Fast of Gedaliah turns it into a lament: טָמוּם וְעָמוּם וּבָלָה בְּשַׂר חֲסִידֶיךָ, "they hid them and darkened them, and the flesh of Your faithful wasted away."
**Verse 3**
שָׁפְכוּ דָמָם כַּמַּיִם סְבִיבוֹת יְרוּשָׁלָםִ, וְאֵין קוֹבֵר.
> They have poured out their blood like water all around Jerusalem, and there is none to bury.
Say the first half aloud: `shā-fə-KHŪ dhā-MĀM kam-MA-yim sə-viy-VŌTH yə-rū-shā-LĀM`. The *m* sounds pile up, **m**āM, ka**mm**ayi**m**, yerushalaa**m**, so that the line itself becomes liquid. Then the verse stops on two short words that have no *m* in them: `wə-'ĒYN qō-VĒR`. The ring of blood "all around Jerusalem" takes the place of the city's walls in this verse, and in the next verse the same word, סְבִיבוֹת, "all around," will describe the neighbors who mock.
Why was there nobody to bury them? The Alshich gives the grim practical answer. Any Jerusalemite who went out to bury the dead would himself be killed, and his blood would be poured out around the city too. On this reading the two halves of the verse are cause and effect: the ring of blood is what keeps the gravediggers inside.
Two poets from opposite ends of the classical world wrote about the same absence and drew opposite conclusions. In his epic of civil war (written in the 60s CE), Lucan describes Caesar refusing the Pompeian dead a pyre at Pharsalus, and the narrator answers him with a famous consolation:
> libera Fortunae mors est; capit omnia tellus
> quae genuit; caelo tegitur qui non habet urnam.
>
> Death is free from Fortune; the earth takes in all that it begot;
> he who has no urn is covered by the sky.
(*Pharsalia* 7.818–819)
The line is a Stoic shrug: the sky is a big enough coffin for anyone. Du Fu, writing in China around 750, closes his "Ballad of the Army Carts" with no such comfort:
> 君不見青海頭，古來白骨無人收。
> 新鬼煩冤舊鬼哭，天陰雨濕聲啾啾。
>
> Has my lord seen the shores of the Kokonor?
> The white bones lie there in drifts, uncollected.
> New ghosts complain and old ghosts weep,
> under the lowering sky their voices cry out in the rain.
(trans. David Lunde)
無人收, "no one gathers," is almost exactly וְאֵין קוֹבֵר, "and there is none to bury." Lucan makes the missing burial bearable. Du Fu leaves it unbearable and lets the ghosts complain to the rain. The psalm does neither. It records the missing burial so that it can be used as evidence.
The verse was later worked into the laments for the destruction. A strophe of the Edot HaMizrach kinah for Tisha B'Av night, whose refrain is עֵינִי עֵינִי יוֹרְדָה מַּיִם, "my eye, my eye runs down with water," reads:
> זְכוֹר ה׳ לִיהוּדָה וְאֶפְרַיִם.
> אֲשֶׁר שֻׁפַּךְ דָּמָם כַּמַּיִם.
> סְבִיבוֹת יְרוּשָׁלַיִם.
>
> Remember, O Lord, Judah and Ephraim,
> whose blood was poured out like water
> all around Jerusalem.
The refrain's water of tears is answered by the psalm's water of blood. The Sefard El Maleh for fast days blends the phrase with 2 Samuel 14:14 into "their blood was poured out like water poured on the ground," which cannot be gathered up again.
**Verse 4**
הָיִינוּ חֶרְפָּה לִשְׁכֵנֵינוּ, לַעַג וָקֶלֶס לִסְבִיבוֹתֵינוּ.
> We have become a reproach to our neighbors, a scorn and a derision to those around us.
For the first time the poem says "we," and the "we" has nothing to report except how it looks to others. The rarest word in the verse is קֶלֶס, "derision." Its verb describes the boys who jeered at Elisha, וַיִּתְקַלְּסוּ־בוֹ, "they mocked him: Go up, baldhead!" (2 Kgs 2:23), and Ezekiel uses it for the nations who "mock you, defiled of name" (Ezek 22:5). In later Hebrew a word spelled the same way, קִלּוּס, came to mean the opposite: praise, laudation. Some lexicographers trace both senses to one root meaning "to stamp the feet," something done in contempt and in applause. Whatever the history, the overlap suits a poem that begins with the neighbors stamping in scorn and ends with "we will recount Your praise." Hold on to that until verse 13.
Rashi glosses קֶלֶס as a byword, people talking about us, and cites Deuteronomy's curse that Israel will become לְמָשָׁל, "a proverb," among the nations. The Alshich draws a social distinction from the two halves of the verse. The near neighbors, who trample us daily, do not even bother to mock us, because we are simply a disgrace to them. Only those a little farther off still find us worth laughing at. It is a precise observation of how contempt works: indifference sits closest.
Wilfred Owen's "Anthem for Doomed Youth" (1917) imagines dead soldiers whose only funeral is the noise of the guns:
> What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?
> — Only the monstrous anger of the guns.
> Only the stuttering rifles' rapid rattle
> Can patter out their hasty orisons.
> No mockeries now for them; no prayers nor bells;
> Nor any voice of mourning save the choirs,—
> The shrill, demented choirs of wailing shells;
> And bugles calling for them from sad shires.
Owen's "mockeries" are the church's own rites, which he calls hollow beside such deaths. The psalm's mockery is literal, the neighbors' laughter. The structure is the same, though. In both, the only sound that rises over the unburied is a parody of mourning: shells "wailing" in Owen, neighbors jeering in the psalm. The real mourners are absent. Owen's dead die "as cattle," and the psalm's dead were left as carrion, נִבְלָה.
**Verse 5**
עַד־מָה ה׳ תֶּאֱנַף לָנֶצַח; תִּבְעַר כְּמוֹ־אֵשׁ קִנְאָתֶךָ.
> How long, O LORD — will You be angry forever, will Your jealousy burn like fire?
This is the only time the four-letter divine name appears in the psalm, which otherwise belongs to a stretch of the Psalter that prefers "God." It arrives already inside a quotation. The whole line is adapted from Psalm 89:47, עַד־מָה ה׳ תִּסָּתֵר לָנֶצַח, תִּבְעַר כְּמוֹ־אֵשׁ חֲמָתֶךָ, "How long, O Lord, will You hide forever, will Your fury burn like fire?" The name came with the line. What the poet changed is more telling. Psalm 89 complains of a God who hides (תִּסָּתֵר). Psalm 79 complains of a God who is angry (תֶּאֱנַף, a verb built from אַף, "nose," the face reddening). A hidden God may simply be absent. An angry God is present and involved. The psalmist chose the second complaint, and that makes his next request possible: anger can be aimed somewhere else, and absence cannot.
The second swap is from חֵמָה, "fury," to קִנְאָה, "jealousy." Ibn Ezra sees the problem at once. God's jealousy is the jealousy of a spouse, kindled "because Israel served other gods." Then, he asks, וְלָמָּה לֹא תְקַנֵּא בְּזָרִים, "why are You not jealous against the strangers?" Jealousy in a marriage is aimed at the unfaithful partner. The psalm wants it turned outward, at the intruders. Verse 6 makes that move in its first word.
Radak and Meiri hear the "how long" against the Babylonian exile, which was promised to last only seventy years and still felt endless. They cite Zechariah, the prophet of the return: עַד־מָתַי אַתָּה לֹא־תְרַחֵם אֶת־יְרוּשָׁלַםִ, "How long will You not have mercy on Jerusalem?" (Zech 1:12). Read the next two verses of Zechariah and the psalm gets something like an answer. The angel is told to proclaim, קִנֵּאתִי לִירוּשָׁלַםִ וּלְצִיּוֹן קִנְאָה גְדוֹלָה, "I am jealous for Jerusalem and for Zion with a great jealousy," and God is furious with "the nations at ease" (1:14–15). In Zechariah the same jealousy has changed direction: it is now for Jerusalem and against the nations, which is what this verse is asking for.
**Verses 6–7**
שְׁפֹךְ חֲמָתְךָ אֶל־הַגּוֹיִם אֲשֶׁר לֹא־יְדָעוּךָ, וְעַל מַמְלָכוֹת אֲשֶׁר בְּשִׁמְךָ לֹא קָרָאוּ.
> Pour out Your fury on the nations that do not know You, and upon kingdoms that do not call on Your name.
כִּי אָכַל אֶת־יַעֲקֹב, וְאֶת־נָוֵהוּ הֵשַׁמּוּ.
> For he has devoured Jacob, and they have laid waste his pasture.
Fire becomes liquid. The jealousy that "burns" in verse 5 is now חֵמָה, which can be "poured." The word ranges from "heat" through "venom" to "rage," and here it is something that can be aimed.
These two verses almost repeat Jeremiah 10:25, and the differences between the two texts matter. Jeremiah curses מִשְׁפָּחוֹת, "families, clans." The psalm curses מַמְלָכוֹת, "kingdoms." Radak explains the change historically. Once Judah fell, Israel itself no longer had a kingdom, even though the exile of the ten tribes had left the Davidic kingdom standing. A people that has lost its own kingship prays against kingdoms. Jeremiah's version also has a double verb, אֲכָלֻהוּ וַיְכַלֻּהוּ, "they devoured him and finished him off." The psalm has only "devoured." It may be a matter of transmission, since no one can say which text borrowed from which. Still, the psalm happens to lack the verb of annihilation, and the rabbinic reading of the poem holds that the children were not annihilated. Verse 11 will ask for the survivors to be kept alive.
The number shifts inside verse 7, from singular אָכַל, "he devoured," to plural הֵשַׁמּוּ, "they laid waste." The Greek and some Hebrew manuscripts smooth it into a plural. The commentators prefer to explain it. Meiri reads the singular as "the wicked king at the head of the oppressors," and the plural as the whole people that followed him. Radak calls the first verb the general statement and the second the particulars. The Alshich hears Esau, a single ancestor, who "devoured Jacob" by stripping him of his wealth, and his sons who then destroyed the Temple. Grammar that seems irregular to a grammarian is, to these readers, the poem telling a ruler apart from his army.
The last word reaches back to Exodus. The Mekhilta comments on the Song of the Sea's וְאַנְוֵהוּ, "I will glorify Him" (Exod 15:2). Rabbi Yossi ben Dormaskit reads it as "I will make Him a נָוֶה, a dwelling," that is, a Temple, and his proof is this verse: וְאֶת־נָוֵהוּ הֵשַׁמּוּ, "and they have laid waste His dwelling." For him the psalm's ruined pasture is the Song's promised sanctuary, and the Song's promise stands behind the psalm's complaint.
A Jewish poet of modern times wrote the curse out to its end. Shaul Tchernichovsky's "Baruch of Mainz" (1902) is the monologue of a Rhineland Jew in 1096, forced to the font and bereaved, who sets the city on fire. Speaking to one of his dead (the imperatives are feminine), he watches it burn:
> הָהּ, מַה נּוֹרָא פֹּה הַלַּיְלָה, חֹשֶׁךְ יְשׁוּפֶנּוּ.
> לִבִּי דַּוָּי, בְּשָׂרִי יִכְאַב, שֶׁן־הַקֹּר תֹאכְלֶנּוּ.
> וּבָעִיר – שָׁם חֹם־הַכִּבְשָׁן... שׁוּרִי, רְאִי שָׁם אוֹרָה!
> יָדַי, יָדַי שָׁלְחוּ אֵשׁ זוֹ הֶעֱלוּ הַמְּדוּרָה,
> וְיַעַל בָּהּ הַגּוֹי הַנֶּאֱלָח, חָסְנוֹ וְחַטֹּאתָיו,
> וְהָיָה שִׁמְמוֹת עַד, וְעָנוּ אֹחִים בְּאַרְמְנוֹתָיו!
>
> Ah, how terrible the night is here; darkness grinds it down.
> My heart is faint, my flesh aches, the tooth of cold devours it.
> And in the city — there, the furnace heat… Look, see the light there!
> My hands, my hands sent out this fire, raised the pyre,
> and the loathsome gentile goes up in it, his wealth and his sins,
> and it shall be a desolation forever, and howling creatures shall answer in its palaces!
Every word of the psalm's request is here: "devours," "desolation" (שִׁמְמוֹת, from the root of הֵשַׁמּוּ), the fire. One thing has changed. The psalm says שְׁפֹךְ, "**You** pour." Tchernichovsky's Baruch says יָדַי, יָדַי, "**my** hands, my hands." The psalm hands vengeance to God and leaves the matter there. The modern poem shows what happens when a man stops waiting for God to act and acts himself, and it does not pretend that this brings him peace.
Redirection has a cost the psalm does not look at. In April 1927, with the Mississippi in record flood, the authorities dynamited the levee at Caernarvon, fourteen miles below New Orleans, to take pressure off the city. The water did not disappear. It went into St. Bernard and Plaquemines Parishes, among poorer people downstream. "Pour out Your fury elsewhere" asks for a spillway in the same way, and every spillway floods somebody. The psalm decides who that should be by one criterion: those "who do not know You," who "do not call on Your name." The criterion is theological, and the violence that would justify it does not appear until the כִּי, "for," of verse 7. A midrash (Tanchuma Buber, Nitzavim) gives the criterion a moral reason. When suffering comes on the nations they "kick against it and do not recognize the name of the Holy One," whereas Israel, under the same suffering, says "I call on the name of the Lord."
The Edot HaMizrach rite turns the phrase into an antithesis in an alphabetical petition said in the weekday Vidui, on the Fast of Gedaliah, the Tenth of Tevet, the Fast of Esther and the Seventeenth of Tammuz, in the selichot, and through the Yom Kippur services: יָהּ, דַּע אֶת יִשְׂרָאֵל אֲשֶׁר יְדָעוּךָ, מַגֵּר אֶת הַגּוֹיִם אֲשֶׁר לֹא יְדָעוּךָ, "O Lord, know Israel who know You; overthrow the nations who do not know You." Jeremiah shares the phrase, so the debt may not be to this psalm alone. The Polish selichot for the sixth day include vv. 6–7 whole. The Lithuanian selichot for Erev Rosh Hashanah, and a kinah of Edot HaMizrach for Tisha B'Av day, end on the verse about devouring Jacob. The kinah keeps the psalm's singular אָכַל.
**Verse 8**
אַל־תִּזְכָּר־לָנוּ עֲוֹנֹת רִאשֹׁנִים; מַהֵר יְקַדְּמוּנוּ רַחֲמֶיךָ, כִּי דַלּוֹנוּ מְאֹד.
> Do not remember against us the iniquities of those before us; let Your compassion hurry to meet us, for we have been brought very low.
עֲוֹנֹת רִאשֹׁנִים can be read two ways: "former iniquities," our own earlier sins, or "iniquities of the former ones," the sins of our ancestors. Radak takes the first ("the sins they committed in the days of their youth"). Ibn Ezra notes that most interpreters read it as an adjective and then disagrees: "the right reading in my eyes" is that the iniquities belong to אָבוֹת רִאשֹׁנִים, "the first fathers." His reason is a matter of craft. He hears יְקַדְּמוּנוּ, "let it come first to meet us," answering רִאשֹׁנִים, "the first ones." The ancestors' sins were first in time, and the psalm asks God's mercy to be first in speed. Malbim works the same pair as a sequence in a courtroom. A man struck down for his own sins gets no pity, since he is guilty. So the psalm asks for compassion to arrive before the record of sins is read. Once mercy has spoken first, the sins can be atoned for afterward, which is the order verse 9 follows.
The line has a near-twin in Isaiah, אַל־תִּזְכְּרוּ רִאשֹׁנוֹת, "do not remember the former things" (Isa 43:18). There God tells Israel to forget the past, while here Israel asks God to. A midrash (Shemot Rabbah 31:1) brings the two together in a parable about debt. A man who had borrowed from a moneylender and forgotten the loan comes years later to acknowledge it. The lender says, "Why did you remind me? That first debt was long since gone from my heart." That is how God treats people who repent: when they come to bring up their old debts, He says "do not remember the former things."
An unexpected modern parallel shows exactly how modest the request is. The American Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970) bars credit bureaus from reporting "accounts placed for collection or charged to profit and loss which antedate the report by more than seven years" (15 U.S.C. §1681c). The debt is not cancelled, and the file still exists. The bureau simply may not read it out. "Do not remember against us" asks the same thing of God, and the plea is not "we are innocent." The statute even uses the psalm's other number, seven, which returns in verse 12.
דַּלּוֹנוּ, "we have been brought low," is used of Israel at a known low point: וַיִּדַּל יִשְׂרָאֵל מְאֹד מִפְּנֵי מִדְיָן, "Israel was brought very low before Midian, and the children of Israel cried to the Lord" (Judg 6:6). The Greek renders it ἐπτωχεύσαμεν, "we have become beggars." The Alshich builds a defense on that word. The first two questions put to a soul at judgment, he says, are "did you set times for Torah?" and "did you deal honestly?" Poverty answers both. "If there is no flour there is no Torah," and a starving man does not check whether his bread is honestly come by. The Chida (*Chomat Anakh*) goes further: exiles are like the drunk, whom the law exempts from prayer. Both commentators turn "we are very low" from a complaint into a legal plea of diminished capacity.
The verse, with v. 9, closes Tachanun daily (see the liturgical section). It also appears inside *Vehu Rachum*, the long Monday-and-Thursday supplication, in adapted form: מַהֵר יְקַדְּמוּנוּ רַחֲמֶיךָ בְּיוֹם צָרָתֵנוּ, וְאִם־לֹא לְמַעֲנֵנוּ לְמַעַנְךָ פְעַל, "Let Your compassion hurry to meet us on the day of our distress; if not for our sake, act for Yours." That second clause anticipates the next verse of the psalm. In the Yom Kippur Shacharit, a piyyut of "day… today…" lines quotes it as a prooftext: הַיּוֹם סְלַח לַעֲוֹנֵינוּ וְחֵטְא אַל תִּזְכֹּר, "Today forgive our iniquities and do not remember sin," followed by "as it is written… אַל תִּזְכָּר לָנוּ עֲוֹנוֹת רִאשֹׁנִים." The Baal Shem Tov's prayer for the Days of Awe puts the plea in its barest form: "we spread our hands before the Throne of Glory and say: we have not even a poor man's offering… only empty hands. מַהֵר יְקַדְּמוּנוּ רַחֲמֶיךָ כִּי דַלּוֹנוּ מְאֹד."
**Verse 9**
עָזְרֵנוּ אֱלֹקֵי יִשְׁעֵנוּ עַל־דְּבַר כְּבוֹד־שְׁמֶךָ; וְהַצִּילֵנוּ וְכַפֵּר עַל־חַטֹּאתֵינוּ לְמַעַן שְׁמֶךָ.
> Help us, God of our salvation, for the sake of the glory of Your name; rescue us and atone for our sins, for Your name's sake.
Malbim's lexical note catches the restraint in the first word. עֵזֶר, "help," is less than יְשׁוּעָה, "salvation." The one who saves does everything, while the one who helps only joins in. The verse sets the two words side by side: "**help** us, God of our **salvation**." You have always been our savior, Malbim paraphrases, so now at least help. After eight verses of disaster the community asks for the lesser thing, and frames even that as God's own past.
וְכַפֵּר, "and atone," is the vocabulary of the priesthood (the high priest "atones" with the blood of the purification offering, Lev 16), but here God is the subject, there is no priest, and the altar has been defiled. The verse had a near neighbor in the scroll. The psalm placed immediately before this one, recalling the wilderness, says: וְהוּא רַחוּם יְכַפֵּר עָוֹן וְלֹא־יַשְׁחִית, "But He, being compassionate, atones for iniquity and does not destroy" (Ps 78:38). What Psalm 78 tells about the past, Psalm 79 asks for in the present tense. The two lines also ended up neighbors in the prayer book: וְהוּא רַחוּם is how the evening service begins, and this verse is how Tachanun ends.
The Alshich offers a reading that later Jewish history made very literal. He takes עַל־דְּבַר כְּבוֹד־שְׁמֶךָ not as "for the sake of Your name's glory" but as "on the matter of Your name's glory," a death undertaken to sanctify the Name. If in exile we must die for Your name, "help us, God of our salvation, and strengthen our hearts, and we will give ourselves to death on account of the glory of Your name, like Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah." Then: "and rescue us," as You rescued them from the furnace. If we are spared, how will our sins be atoned? "Atone for our sins," because we were willing to die. The liturgy holds this reading without naming him. Avinu Malkeinu places עֲשֵׂה לְמַעַן בָּאֵי בָאֵשׁ וּבַמַּיִם עַל קִדּוּשׁ שְׁמֶךָ, "act for the sake of those who went through fire and water to sanctify Your name," directly before its paraphrase of verse 10. The Chabad bedtime Shema ends each of its four paragraphs accepting, symbolically, the court's four death penalties with עַל דְּבַר כְּבוֹד שְׁמֶךָ.
The Chida hears the Name differently. He draws on the Zohar's teaching that sin weighs down the "wings of the Shekhinah," the indwelling divine presence, and on that basis reads "atone for our sins for Your name's sake" as "for the sake of the Shekhinah, so that it will have room." Forgiveness then frees the Shekhinah as well as Israel. He adds that this explains the rabbinic saying, "whoever passes over his grievances, all his sins are passed over." By forgiving his fellow, a person lifts that weight from the Shekhinah, and measure for measure, his own sins are lifted.
The argument from the god's own interest is as old as prayer. At their father's tomb in Aeschylus' *Libation Bearers* (458 BCE), Agamemnon's children make the same bargain with Zeus:
> Nor, if all withered, shall this royal stock,
> On sacrificial days, support thine altars.
> O foster it, and raise, from low estate,
> A house which now seems fallen utterly.
(trans. Anna Swanwick)
The difference is in what each argues from. The Greek heirs point to the altars God will lose: let us die and you go unfed. The psalm cannot argue from altars, since its altar is defiled. It argues from God's name, which the nations can still hear mocked when there is no altar at all.
In the Monday and Thursday Tachanun, the line just before this verse is וּבְכָל־זֹאת שִׁמְךָ לֹא שָׁכָחְנוּ, נָא אַל תִּשְׁכָּחֵנוּ, "and despite all this we have not forgotten Your name; please do not forget us." That echoes Psalm 44's protest of innocence, כׇּל־זֹאת בָּאַתְנוּ וְלֹא שְׁכַחֲנוּךָ, "all this befell us, yet we have not forgotten You" (Ps 44:18). Psalm 79 borrowed Psalm 44's line of shame but not its claim of innocence, and confessed instead. The liturgy puts the two back side by side: we sinned, and we did not forget. The verse's phrase became a fixed formula of piety, used in the prayers after Tashlich, in the Rosh Hashanah annulment of vows (הַמָּקוֹם בְּרַחֲמָיו יַעְזְרֵנוּ עַל דְּבַר כְּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ), and even in the Lag BaOmer songs of the Sefard siddur, where עָזְרֵנוּ אֱלֹקֵי יִשְׁעֵנוּ opens lines that end "by the merit of Bar Yochai's celebration."
**Verse 10**
לָמָּה יֹאמְרוּ הַגּוֹיִם, אַיֵּה אֱלֹקֵיהֶם; יִוָּדַע בַּגּוֹיִם לְעֵינֵינוּ, נִקְמַת דַּם־עֲבָדֶיךָ הַשָּׁפוּךְ.
> Why should the nations say, "Where is their God?" Let the avenging of the poured-out blood of Your servants be made known among the nations before our eyes.
The taunt is quoted from a stock of formulas. Psalm 115:2 has it in nearly the same words, לָמָּה יֹאמְרוּ הַגּוֹיִם אַיֵּה־נָא אֱלֹקֵיהֶם, and Joel 2:17 sets it in the mouths of the priests weeping between porch and altar. Comparing the two psalms shows what kind of answer each wants. Psalm 115 answers with doctrine: וֵאלֹקֵינוּ בַשָּׁמָיִם, כֹּל אֲשֶׁר־חָפֵץ עָשָׂה, "Our God is in heaven; whatever He pleases, He does." Psalm 79 does not accept doctrine as an answer to a taunt. It wants a public event, "made known among the nations," and adds a phrase Psalm 115 lacks: לְעֵינֵינוּ, "before our eyes." Ibn Ezra explains the addition simply: "that we be alive before our death." The community wants to see the vindication in its own lifetime, not have it promised to its grandchildren. The Greek reads the opening לָמָּה as μή ποτε, "lest they say," which turns the rhetorical question into a warning about what will happen if God does nothing.
A small curiosity of the written text: the word for "among the nations" is spelled in the consonants as בגיים, with a yod where a waw is expected, and is read as בַּגּוֹיִם. Minchat Shai records it without comment. The same word written a few letters earlier, הַגּוֹיִם, has the normal spelling.
The rabbis read נִקְמָה, "vengeance," as something that belongs to God alone. Midrash Tehillim (149:4) cites this verse and adds: "And vengeance is not the vengeance of a human being, but 'God of vengeance, O Lord'" (Ps 94:1). The psalm asks God for it, and that keeps it out of human hands.
In 1655 the Duke of Savoy's troops massacred the Waldensians, Protestant villagers of the Alpine valleys of Piedmont. John Milton answered with a sonnet that takes up most of this psalm:
> Avenge, O Lord, thy slaughter'd saints, whose bones
> Lie scatter'd on the Alpine mountains cold,
> Ev'n them who kept thy truth so pure of old,
> When all our fathers worshipp'd stocks and stones;
> Forget not: in thy book record their groans
> Who were thy sheep and in their ancient fold
> Slain by the bloody Piemontese that roll'd
> Mother with infant down the rocks. Their moans
> The vales redoubl'd to the hills, and they
> To Heav'n. Their martyr'd blood and ashes sow
> O'er all th' Italian fields where still doth sway
> The triple tyrant; that from these may grow
> A hundred-fold, who having learnt thy way
> Early may fly the Babylonian woe.
The scattered bones, the groans, the sheep, the call to "avenge" and "forget not" are all the psalm's. Milton's multiplication, though, goes the other way. The psalm asks for the neighbors to be repaid "sevenfold." Milton asks for the martyrs' blood to be sown and to come up "a hundred-fold" as converts. He turns the logic of vengeance into the parable of the sower.
The Jewish answer in verse is less forgiving. *Maoz Tzur*, the Chanukah hymn, ends with a stanza built from this psalm:
> חֲשׂוֹף זְרוֹעַ קׇדְשֶׁךָ וְקָרֵב קֵץ הַיְשׁוּעָה,
> נְקֹם נִקְמַת דַּם עֲבָדֶיךָ מֵאֻמָּה הָרְשָׁעָה,
> כִּי אָרְכָה לָנוּ הַשָּׁעָה וְאֵין קֵץ לִימֵי הָרָעָה…
>
> Bare Your holy arm and bring near the end of salvation;
> avenge the vengeance of Your servants' blood from the wicked nation,
> for the hour has grown long for us, and there is no end to the days of evil…
In three lines it takes the psalm's vengeance for "Your servants' blood," its "how long" (אָרְכָה לָנוּ הַשָּׁעָה), and its divine "arm," which belongs to the next verse.
The Ashkenaz kinot for Tisha B'Av bring the phrase back as נִקְמַת דַּם עֲבָדֶיךָ לְעֵינֵינוּ תּוֹדִיעַ, "make known before our eyes the vengeance for Your servants' blood." The Sefard El Maleh for fast days recasts the taunt over a burned Torah scroll: לָמָה יֹאמְרוּ הַגּוֹיִם אַיֵּה יִרְאָתָם, "why should the nations say, Where is their fear of God?"
**Verse 11**
תָּבוֹא לְפָנֶיךָ אֶנְקַת אָסִיר; כְּגֹדֶל זְרוֹעֲךָ הוֹתֵר בְּנֵי תְמוּתָה.
> Let the groan of the prisoner come before You; by the greatness of Your arm keep alive those doomed to die.
Here is the verb the first verse used. בָּאוּ גוֹיִם, the nations "came" into God's land. Now תָּבוֹא, let the prisoner's groan "come" before God's face. The enemy entered the sanctuary, and the groan is to enter the presence. With the temple defiled, a sound becomes the only thing that can still come before God, and it needs no priest.
אֶנְקָה, a "groan," is a word for the lowest kind of human sound. Psalm 12:6 uses it for the cry of the destitute that finally gets God up from His seat: מִשֹּׁד עֲנִיִּים מֵאֶנְקַת אֶבְיוֹנִים, עַתָּה אָקוּם, "because of the plundering of the poor, because of the groan of the needy, now I will arise." The psalmist hopes for the same effect.
בְּנֵי תְמוּתָה, literally "sons of dying," is a phrase found elsewhere only in Psalm 102:21. Most commentators read it as "those condemned to death." Rashi reads it differently and very personally: בְּנֵי הַנֶּהֱרֶגֶת עָלֶיךָ, "the children of the one who was killed for Your sake." For him תְּמוּתָה is the dying mother, and her children are left behind. He supports the word from rabbinic usage, בְּשַׂר תְּמוּתָה, the flesh of an animal at the point of death (Kiddushin 21b–22a). The Greek, independently, reads the same way: τοὺς υἱοὺς τῶν τεθανατωμένων, "the sons of those put to death." On this reading the verse is not about prisoners waiting for execution but about orphans of the slain in verses 2–3, still in chains and still alive. The two readings may meet in the Babylonian situation itself, since the children of the dead were among the captives.
The Alshich offers one more reading. The "prisoner" is the Messiah, who, as the Talmud tells (Sanhedrin 98a), sits bound among the sick at the gate of Rome, binding and unbinding his wounds one at a time so that he will be ready the moment he is called. On this reading the groan that "comes before You" is that of the redeemer himself, waiting in chains alongside his people.
"By the greatness of Your arm" makes the reprieve a display of strength, not a verdict of innocence. History has a cold example of how that works. On 22 December 1849, the young Fyodor Dostoevsky and other members of the Petrashevsky circle were taken to Semyonovsky Square in St. Petersburg, read their death sentences, and lined up before a firing squad. The first three were tied to the stakes. At the last moment a messenger brought the Tsar's pardon, which had been arranged beforehand. Nicholas I wanted a reprieve that showed his power, not their innocence. The psalm makes the same kind of request without irony: it does not ask God to find the condemned not guilty. It asks God to "keep alive" (הוֹתֵר, leave over, leave a remnant) as a demonstration of what His arm can do.
The selichot use this verse as a link in a chain of "let it come" pleas. In the Polish rite on the first day: תָּבוֹא לְפָנֶיךָ תְּפִלָּתֵנוּ וְאַל תִּתְעַלַּם מִתְּחִנָּתֵנוּ… תָּבוֹא לְפָנֶיךָ אֶנְקַת אָסִיר, "Let our prayer come before You and do not hide from our plea… Let the groan of the prisoner come before You." The verse is also said in the Lithuanian selichot (the first day, the Fast of Gedaliah, the fourth of the Ten Days), in the Polish selichot (the first and third days, and the third and fifth of the Ten Days), and in the Sefard selichot for the "First Monday." The chain shows that the community saying these selichot counts itself among the prisoners.
**Verse 12**
וְהָשֵׁב לִשְׁכֵנֵינוּ שִׁבְעָתַיִם אֶל־חֵיקָם, חֶרְפָּתָם אֲשֶׁר חֵרְפוּךָ אֲדֹנָי.
> And pay back to our neighbors sevenfold into their bosom the reproach with which they have reproached You, Lord.
The sound piles up: `wə-hā-SHĒV lish-khē-NĒY-nū shiv-ʿā-THA-yim`. The sh sounds of hā**SH**ēv, li**SH**khenenu, **SH**ivʿatayim make "return" and "sevenfold" sound like one thing. The figure in the second half doubles itself as well: חֶרְפָּתָם אֲשֶׁר חֵרְפוּךָ, "their reproach that they reproached You." The insult is said twice so it can be sent back once.
"Sevenfold" has a history in the Bible, and it is not a comfortable one. Cain is avenged sevenfold, and Lamech boasts he will be avenged seventy-seven (Gen 4:15, 24). Closer still, in the covenant curses of Leviticus God promises to punish Israel itself שֶׁבַע עַל־חַטֹּאתֵיכֶם, "sevenfold for your sins" (Lev 26:18, and three times more in the chapter). The psalm takes the measure of covenant punishment that fell on Israel and asks that it be paid to the neighbors instead. Malbim finds a less vindictive logic in the number. Reproach of God is not a matter between equals. "There is no common measure between You and them," so repaying the insult in kind would not be enough. Sevenfold here means "times without number," because the injury was against someone without limit.
אֶל־חֵיקָם, "into their bosom," takes the word for the fold of a garment, where one carries what one keeps. The idiom has a particular home. Jeremiah praises the God who is מְשַׁלֵּם עֲוֹן אָבוֹת אֶל־חֵיק בְּנֵיהֶם, "who repays the iniquity of the fathers into the bosom of their children" (Jer 32:18). It is the same preposition, אֶל, which this verse uses unusually, and Isaiah threatens Israel in the same terms: "I will repay into their bosom… your iniquities and the iniquities of your fathers together" (Isa 65:6–7). These are formulas for laying the ancestors' debt on Israel. Set beside verse 8, the psalm's request becomes clear: do not remember the iniquities of those before us, and pay the neighbors back into their bosom instead. The account is not closed. It is sent to someone else.
A midrash reads "into their bosom" anatomically and terribly. In Tanchuma Buber (Ki Teitzei), Rabbi Judan bar Gadya says the neighbor Amalek mutilated the circumcised and threw the flesh skyward, saying "Here, take what You chose." The psalm's "reproach with which they reproached You" was, on this reading, a taunt aimed directly at God, and the bosom is the place of the covenant itself.
The oldest such prayer in Western literature makes the same request on very different grounds. In the first book of the *Iliad*, the priest Chryses, dishonored by Agamemnon, walks alone by the sea and prays to Apollo:
> κλῦθί μευ ἀργυρότοξʼ, ὃς Χρύσην ἀμφιβέβηκας
> Κίλλάν τε ζαθέην Τενέδοιό τε ἶφι ἀνάσσεις,
> Σμινθεῦ εἴ ποτέ τοι χαρίεντʼ ἐπὶ νηὸν ἔρεψα,
> ἢ εἰ δή ποτέ τοι κατὰ πίονα μηρίʼ ἔκηα
> ταύρων ἠδʼ αἰγῶν, τὸ δέ μοι κρήηνον ἐέλδωρ·
> τίσειαν Δαναοὶ ἐμὰ δάκρυα σοῖσι βέλεσσιν.
>
> Hear me, Silver Bow, who stand guard over Chryse
> and holy Killa, and rule Tenedos in strength,
> Smintheus: if ever I roofed a pleasing temple for you,
> or if ever I burned for you the fat thighs
> of bulls and goats, fulfil this wish for me:
> let the Danaans pay for my tears with your arrows.
(*Iliad* 1.37–42)
It is the same request, repay my enemies for my injury, and the arrows fly. Chryses argues from his record: I roofed your temple, I burned your thighbones. The psalmist cannot. His temple's roof is gone and its fat is not burning. He has already confessed his sins. All he has left is the argument that the insult was to God: חֵרְפוּךָ, they reproached **You**. In Homer the god avenges a servant who has done his duty. In the psalm a servant who has failed asks God to defend His own honor.
The Polish selichot place the whole verse among the pleas for the fourth of the Ten Days, after "Return, O Lord, our captivity." An Ashkenazi kinah for Tisha B'Av, with the refrain הַטֵּה אֱלֹקַי אָזְנְךָ, "Incline Your ear, my God," asks God to hear כָּל חֶרְפָּתָם אֲשֶׁר חֵרְפוּךָ, "all their reproach that they reproached You."
**Verse 13**
וַאֲנַחְנוּ עַמְּךָ וְצֹאן מַרְעִיתֶךָ, נוֹדֶה לְּךָ לְעוֹלָם; לְדוֹר וָדֹר נְסַפֵּר תְּהִלָּתֶךָ.
> And we, Your people and the flock of Your pasture, will thank You forever; from generation to generation we will recount Your praise.
The verb נוֹדֶה, "we will thank," comes from a root that also means to confess. The same form, הוֹדָה, admits a sin and acknowledges a gift. Verses 8–9 confessed without using a verb, only "iniquities" and "sins." Now the root of confession finally appears, but as thanks. The verse also ends what verse 4 began. There the neighbors' קֶלֶס stamped in derision, and here the people promise תְּהִלָּה, praise, the meaning that the same root would later take.
צֹאן מַרְעִיתֶךָ, "the flock of Your pasture," with "You" as the owner, appears in only one other place: the first verse of the twin psalm, לָמָה אֱלֹקִים זָנַחְתָּ לָנֶצַח, יֶעְשַׁן אַפְּךָ בְּצֹאן מַרְעִיתֶךָ, "Why, God, have You rejected us forever? Why does Your anger smoke against the flock of Your pasture?" (Ps 74:1). That single verse contains both ends of our psalm: "anger forever," which Psalm 79 sets at verse 5, and "the flock of Your pasture," which it saves for the last line. Psalm 74 opens with the flock under God's smoke. Psalm 79 ends with the flock singing. The next psalm in the scroll opens by calling on רֹעֵה יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Shepherd of Israel" (Ps 80:2), as if answering.
Malbim distinguishes the two time-words. לְעוֹלָם, "forever," is continuous time, the thanks of a whole lifetime, which we give ourselves. לְדוֹר וָדֹר, "generation to generation," is time divided between people, the praise handed down to those who come after. The first is a vow about our lives, the second about our children's. Radak adds a note against triumphalism: "whether in exile or when we leave the exile, we will thank You forever." The vow does not wait for the outcome.
In the summer of 1942, as reports of the destruction of Polish Jewry reached Palestine, Natan Alterman wrote "From All the Nations," a bitter liturgy of chosenness:
> כי אתה בחרתנו מכל העמים,
> מנֹרוגים, מצ'כים, מבריטים.
> ובצעוד ילדינו אלי גרדומים,
> ילדים יהודים, ילדים חכמים,
> הם יודעים כי דמם לא נחשב בדמים —
>
> For You have chosen us from all the nations,
> from Norwegians, from Czechs, from Britons.
> And as our children march toward the gallows,
> Jewish children, clever children,
> they know their blood is not counted as blood —
Alterman keeps the prayer book's phrase of election, "You have chosen us from all the nations," and lets it be the reason for the slaughter. Psalm 79 says "we are Your people and the flock of Your pasture" as grounds for rescue and as a pledge of praise. Alterman's children "know their blood is not counted as blood," the opposite of the psalm's insistence that poured-out blood must be avenged. The modern poem keeps the psalm's last move, naming oneself God's own, and turns it into an accusation. Read together, the two show how much the psalm's closing line takes on trust.
Midrash Tehillim (106:2) also sets this verse beside one about generations: "They will not hide from their children, telling the next generation the praises of the Lord" (Ps 78:4). The psalm two places earlier promised to pass the story on, and this verse promises it again. In the Simchat Torah hakafot of the Sefard siddur, the whole verse stands in a chain just before רֹעֵה יִשְׂרָאֵל הַאֲזִינָה, "Shepherd of Israel, give ear," which keeps the scroll's own order. A piyyut in the Yom Kippur Musaf repetition (Ashkenaz and Sefard) asks to be answered "for we are Your people and the flock of Your pasture." The Polish selichot for the fifth of the Ten Days follow the same words with an image the psalm would recognize: כְּדַלִּים וּכְרָשִׁים דָּפַקְנוּ דְלָתֶיךָ, "like the poor and the destitute we have knocked at Your doors." The דַּלּוֹנוּ of verse 8, "we have been brought very low," is still knocking at the door.
