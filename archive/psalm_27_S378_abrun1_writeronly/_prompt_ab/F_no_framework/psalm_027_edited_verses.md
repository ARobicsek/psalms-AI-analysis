**Verse 1**

לְדָוִד ׀ ה׳ אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי — מִמִּי אִירָא? ה׳ מָעוֹז־חַיַּי — מִמִּי אֶפְחָד?

> Of David. The LORD is my light and my rescue — of whom shall I be afraid? The LORD is the stronghold of my life — of whom shall I be in dread?

The second question is not a repetition; it is a step down into the body. יָרֵא, "to fear," is the wide word — reverence, caution, dread. פָּחַד is narrower and more physical, the word used of the panic that seizes a camp at night. He asks the intellectual question first and the visceral one second.

And the answer is hidden inside the question. Listen: 'ō-**R**IY, "my light," and 'iy-**R**Ā', "I fear," echo each other in sound. Hebrew poets often exploit this kind of phonetic closeness, and here it does real work: the word for the thing that ends fear is made to sound like the word for fear.

מָעוֹז is fortress vocabulary — Klein derives it from עזז, "to be strong," with the prefix מָ that turns a verb into a *place*: the place of strength. Radak takes the metaphor and then dismantles it, in a passage that reads like someone who has thought hard about sieges:

> כי הנשגב במגדל עוז עדין הוא ירא ופוחד פן יצורו עליו איביו ויכבשו המגדל ההוא, או יצורו עליו עד שימות שם ברעב ובצמא — "for one secure in a tower of strength is still afraid and in dread, lest his enemies besiege him and capture that tower, or besiege him until he dies inside it of hunger and thirst."

That is why the psalm cannot stop at מָעוֹז. A tower has two failure modes, and the psalmist knows both. By verse 5 he will have traded the fortress for a booth of branches — an object with no defensive properties whatsoever, and therefore none of the tower's specific liabilities.

The Septuagint declines the flat identification. Where the Hebrew says God *is* my light, the Greek reads κύριος φωτισμός μου, "the Lord is my illumination," rather than the bare predicate "my light." The Greek translator, faced with a metaphor that makes God a thing, recast it as something God does for the speaker.

**Verse 2**

בִּקְרֹב עָלַי מְרֵעִים לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי — צָרַי וְאֹיְבַי לִי — הֵמָּה כָשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ׃

> When evildoers drew near against me to eat my flesh — my foes and my enemies, they are the ones — they stumbled and fell.

"To eat my flesh" turns the attackers into animals. The idiom is unmistakable in its home habitat: Jeremiah summons the beasts to a carcass with לְכוּ אִסְפוּ כָּל־חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה הֵתָיוּ לְאָכְלָה, "come, gather all the beasts of the field, bring them to devour" (Jer 12:9). Hezekiah, sick, describes God as כָּאֲרִי כֵּן יְשַׁבֵּר כָּל־עַצְמוֹתָי, "like a lion, so He breaks all my bones" (Isa 38:13). And having made them predators, the psalmist takes away their footing: כָשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ, `khā-shə-**LŪ**` `wə-nā-**FĀ**-lū` — the two verbs rhyme on the same plural ending, and the effect is of one motion continued, a trip becoming a sprawl. Jeremiah's rhetorical question makes the point about finality: הֲיִפְּלוּ וְלֹא יָקוּמוּ, "do they fall and not get up?" These do not get up.

The syntax of צָרַי וְאֹיְבַי לִי is awkward, and Malbim's solution is to hear two motives in one verse:

> אם כוונתם להנאתם, כמו גנבים ושודדי לילה, ועז"א לאכל את בשרי … אם כונתם מחמת איבה ומשטמה, ועז"א צרי ואויבי הבאים לי בשבילי — "if their aim is their own gratification, like thieves and night-robbers, that is 'to eat my flesh'; if their aim is hatred and malice, that is 'my foes and enemies who come *to me* — on my account.'"

On this reading the psalm distinguishes predators who want something from predators who want *you*, and refuses to be more frightened of either. Malbim adds a detail worth keeping: הגם שאני לא אעשה נגדם דבר כי איני יודע מי המה, "though I do nothing against them, since I do not know who they are." The psalmist never identifies his attackers. He does not need to.

**Verse 3**

אִם־תַּחֲנֶה עָלַי מַחֲנֶה — לֹא־יִירָא לִבִּי; אִם־תָּקוּם עָלַי מִלְחָמָה — בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ׃

> Should a camp encamp against me, my heart will not fear; should war rise up against me, in this I am confident.

`'im-ta-ḥa-**NEH** ʿā-**LAY** ma-ḥa-**NEH**` — the verb and its object are the same word, differently pointed: תַּחֲנֶה, "should it encamp," and מַחֲנֶה, "a camp." Hebrew loves this figure, and here it makes the action and its agent inseparable, so that the camp *is* its own encamping, a self-generating threat. Meiri, ever the grammarian, pauses to note that מַחֲנֶה here takes feminine agreement, as in Genesis 32:9.

Then בְּזֹאת אֲנִי בוֹטֵחַ, "in this I am confident." In *what*? The commentators split, and the split matters. Rashi points backward: בְּמַה שֶׁאָמַר לְמַעְלָה ה׳ מָעוֹז חַיַּי, "in what was said above: the LORD is the stronghold of my life" — a reading Radak and Meiri share. Malbim points forward, to the verse not yet spoken:

> בזאת אני בוטח, במה שיאמר, והוא, כי אחת שאלתי מאת ה' … רק שאלה אחת שאלתי בעבר, ואותה אבקש תמיד בעתיד, כי בשאלה זו כלולים כל השאלות — "'in this I trust' — in what he is about to say, namely: one thing I have asked of the LORD … one request I asked in the past, and that I keep asking in the future, for all requests are contained in this one."

Commit to Malbim. A forward-pointing "this" is the more interesting Hebrew and the better poem: the psalmist reaches the summit of his bravado, and instead of naming what he is brave about, he leaves the pronoun hanging for one beat and then answers it with something nobody expects — a request to sit in a building. The suspense is the point, and Rashi's reading dissolves it.

**Verse 4**

אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־ה׳, אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ: שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־ה׳ כָּל־יְמֵי חַיַּי, לַחֲזוֹת בְּנֹעַם־ה׳ וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ׃

> One thing I have asked of the LORD, that is what I seek: my dwelling in the house of the LORD all the days of my life, to gaze on the LORD's pleasantness and to inquire in His palace.

The verse names three distinct activities, and the last two are genuinely obscure.

**לַחֲזוֹת** is not ordinary seeing. חָזָה is the prophet's verb — it takes חָזוֹן, "vision," as its object; Balaam is מַחֲזֵה שַׁקַּי יֶחֱזֶה, "one who sees the vision of the Almighty." With the preposition **בְּ** attached to its object, as here, BDB notes it means to look on *intently, with gratification* — the same construction as the lover's gaze in the Song. So the request is for prolonged, gratified, quasi-visionary looking.

**נֹעַם** occurs six times in the Bible and is startlingly aesthetic for a divine attribute — Proverbs uses it of wisdom's דַּרְכֵי־נֹעַם, "pleasant ways," and אִמְרֵי־נֹעַם, "pleasant words," and Zechariah names a shepherd's staff with it. Applied to God only here and in Psalm 90:17, וִיהִי נֹעַם אֲדֹנָי אֱלֹקֵינוּ עָלֵינוּ, "let the pleasantness of the Lord our God be upon us." It is a word for charm, agreeableness, the quality that makes something a pleasure to be near. Whatever the psalmist wants to look at, it is beautiful rather than terrifying.

**וּלְבַקֵּר** is the hard one, and Rashi preserves an eleventh-century philological quarrel:

> לֵירָאוֹת שָׁם בְּכָל בֹּקֶר וָבֹקֶר. כָּךְ פֵּרְשׁוֹ דּוֹנָשׁ, וּמְנַחֵם חִבְּרוֹ עִם ״לֹא יְבַקֵּר בֵּין טוֹב לָרָע״ — "to appear there every single morning. So Dunash explained it. Menachem, however, connected it with 'he shall not inquire between good and bad' (Lev 27:33)."

Dunash ben Labrat heard בֹּקֶר, "morning"; Menachem ben Saruq heard the root בקר, "to examine." Menachem was right, and the decisive evidence is cultic: when Ahaz commissions his new altar, the old bronze one is left לִי לְבַקֵּר, "for me to inquire by" (2 Kgs 16:15) — technical language for seeking an oracle. What the psalmist wants in the temple is not sightseeing but *consultation*: to look, and to get an answer. Which is exactly what a man with false witnesses ranged against him (v. 12) would want.

The whole verse has a twin. Psalm 23 ends וְשַׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־ה׳ לְאֹרֶךְ יָמִים, "and I shall dwell in the house of the LORD for length of days," with כָּל־יְמֵי חַיָּי, "all the days of my life," in the same breath just before. Our psalm shares both phrases — and changes the mood. Psalm 23 states it as an outcome; Psalm 27 asks for it as a favor. Same words, opposite confidence. In some Jewish practice the difference has been quietly softened: Psalm 23's version of the line gets sung before Kiddush on Friday night, while Psalm 27's version has to wait for Elul.

Augustine, reading the Latin *unam petii*, took the "one thing" to be the beatific vision — the direct sight of God in eternity, with every other petition folded into it. That is more than the Hebrew says and less than the Hebrew wants: the psalmist is asking for something he expects inside his own lifetime, כָּל־יְמֵי חַיַּי, and he expects to get answers there.

**Verses 5–6**

כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְּסֻכֹּה בְּיוֹם רָעָה, יַסְתִּרֵנִי בְּסֵתֶר אָהֳלוֹ, בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי׃

> For He will conceal me in His booth on an evil day, He will hide me in the covert of His tent, on a rock He will raise me high.

וְעַתָּה יָרוּם רֹאשִׁי עַל אֹיְבַי סְבִיבוֹתַי, וְאֶזְבְּחָה בְאָהֳלוֹ זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה, אָשִׁירָה וַאֲזַמְּרָה לַה׳׃

> And now my head will be raised over my enemies around me, and I will sacrifice in His tent sacrifices of blast-and-shout; I will sing and make music to the LORD.

Verse 5 ends three consecutive clauses with the same object: `yits-**PNĒ**-niy` … `yas-ti-rē-**NIY**` … `yə-rō-mə-**MĒ**-niy`. Concealed *me*, hidden *me*, lifted *me*. The ear registers the suffix before the mind parses the verbs, and the effect is of a single body being handled three times.

The סֻכָּה is worth dwelling on. It is a shelter of interwoven boughs — the word Leviticus uses for the festival booths, the word Isaiah uses for a watchman's hut in a vineyard as an image of Zion reduced to nothing, the word Job uses for the frailest thing a man can build. And its cognates reach into Canaanite religion: Ugaritic *sk* belongs to the vocabulary of divine dwellings, the pavilion-tents in which El and Baal are described as residing on the divine mountain, and Akkadian *sukku* is a shrine. Psalm 18 puts God's own סֻכָּה in the storm clouds. So the psalmist is asking to be tucked inside the deity's tent — not into a bunker, but into the place where the god himself is. It is the least defensible structure in the Hebrew architectural vocabulary, and that is the argument.

Rashi will not let the image float. Citing *Seder Olam*, he anchors it in a specific royal crisis:

> שֶׁהַמִּקְרָא הַזֶּה נֶאֱמַר עַל יוֹאָש בֶּן אֲחַזְיָה שֶׁהִסְתִּירָתוֹ יְהוֹשֶׁבַע אֲחוֹתוֹ בַּעֲלִיַּת בֵּית קָדְשֵׁי הַקֳּדָשִׁים — "that this verse was said concerning Joash son of Ahaziah, whom Jehosheba, sister of Ahaziah, hid in the attic of the Holy of Holies."

The reference is to 2 Kings 11:3, וַיְהִי אִתָּהּ בֵּית ה׳ מִתְחַבֵּא שֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים, "and he was with her in the house of the LORD, hiding, six years." Rashi has found a case where the temple literally functioned as a hiding place for a hunted claimant to the throne — which is precisely the institution modern scholars reconstruct behind this psalm, the sanctuary as legal asylum. An infant in a temple attic for six years is not a metaphor about spirituality.

Then בְּצוּר יְרוֹמְמֵנִי, "on a rock He will raise me high" — and verse 6 keeps the vertical going with יָרוּם רֹאשִׁי, "my head will be raised." Compare the same verb in Psalm 13:3: עַד־אָנָה יָרוּם אֹיְבִי עָלָי, "how long shall my enemy be raised over me?" One verb, two psalms, and the only question is whose head is up.

The sacrifices are זִבְחֵי תְרוּעָה — and תְּרוּעָה is a word that refuses to choose. BDB lists it as the war-cry at Jericho, the alarm-blast of the trumpets on the march, the shofar of Yom Kippur, *and* the shout of religious joy at the Ark's return. Malbim exploits the double sense precisely:

> שהלוחם ומנצח את אויביו יזבח זבחי תרועה … אבל אנכי שאנצח ע"י שאהיה באהל ה' א"כ ישיבת האהל היא תרועת המלחמה ונצחונה — "the warrior who defeats his enemies offers sacrifices of *teru'ah*, thanksgiving for the war-blast and its victory. But I, who win by being in the LORD's tent — for me, sitting in the tent *is* the war-blast and the victory."

The military noun is retained and its referent swapped. What used to be the trumpet is now the pew.

Here the Greek makes a decision the Hebrew was careful not to make. MT's יָרוּם is a future form — "my head *will be* raised," a hope. The Septuagint reads ὕψωσεν, "He raised," a completed act. A psalm still waiting for its vindication has, in Greek, already received it; the translator granted the victory in advance and lost the ache of וְעַתָּה, "and now," which in Hebrew means *any moment now*.

Liturgically, verse 6 has been turned inside out. Its עַל אֹיְבַי, "over my enemies," reappears in Ashkenazi *Tachanun* with the pronoun changed: עוֹרְרָה גְבוּרָתְךָ וְקִנְאָתְךָ עַל־אֹיְבֶיךָ הֵם יֵבשׁוּ וְיֵחַתּוּ מִגְבוּרָתָם, "rouse Your might and Your zeal against *Your* enemies; may they be shamed and shattered of their strength." The individual's foes become God's.

**Verse 7**

שְׁמַע־ה׳ קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא, וְחָנֵּנִי וַעֲנֵנִי׃

> Hear, LORD — my voice, I call! — and be gracious to me and answer me.

The verse is a hinge, and it behaves like one: for the first time in the psalm God is addressed rather than described, and the grammar goes slightly loose in the process. קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא is two phrases set side by side with no connective — "my voice, I call" — which is why translators supply "when." Ibn Ezra notes the gap and offers two repairs: either a relative word is missing, or the sense is "this honored Name that I call and invoke." Neither is elegant. The roughness is what a shift from composure to appeal sounds like.

This is the verse the Rosh Hashanah *chazzan* borrows when he presents his own credentials to the court, in the Ashkenazi *piyyut* before the extended Kedushah: שָׁפַכְתִּי שִׂיחַ לְבַקֵּשׁ עֲתִירָה. שְׁמַע ה׳ קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא: מְדַבֵּר בִּצְדָקָה חוֹנֵן וּמִתְרַצֶּה, "I have poured out speech to seek entreaty. Hear, O LORD, my voice when I call — You who speak righteously, gracious and appeasable." In the Edot HaMizrach Selichot for Yom Kippur eve it is set in a chain of verses that all begin with the same demand, each one asking again for the same audience: שְׁמַע־ה׳ וְחָנֵּנִי … שְׁמַע־ה׳ קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא … שָׁמַע ה׳ תְּחִנָּתִי, "Hear, LORD, and be gracious to me … Hear, LORD, my voice when I call … The LORD has heard my supplication." The last verb is a perfect. The chain is built to end with the request granted.

**Verse 8**

לְךָ אָמַר לִבִּי: בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי! אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ ה׳ אֲבַקֵּשׁ׃

> On Your behalf my heart has said: "Seek My face!" Your face, LORD, I seek.

The verse has a logical problem that every commentator feels: the quoted command, בַּקְּשׁוּ פָנָי, "seek *My* face," must be God's — nobody else has a face worth seeking that way — but its speaker is לִבִּי, "my heart." Rashi solves it by taking לְךָ as "in Your stead": the heart speaks as God's deputy, relaying His summons, and the psalmist obeys. He proves the usage from Job — הֵן אֲנִי כְפִיךָ לָקֵל, "behold, I am like your mouth toward God," and אִם־לָקֵל תְּרִיבוּן, "will you contend on God's behalf" — where the preposition means *on behalf of, in place of*.

The Radak on this verse comes with an interruption. Somewhere between the manuscript and the printed page, the publisher lost patience and inserted his own commentary under his own name, signing himself שלמה הקטן בן מאיר הגדול, "Solomon the small, son of Meir the great":

> מאד נשתוממתי על המראה כי אין גם אחד מן הפרשים הרבים מפרש הפסוק הזה כראוי, אשר הוא בעיניהם כלו מקשה, והוא באמת זהב טהור — "I was greatly astonished at what I saw: not one of the many commentators explains this verse properly. In their eyes it is entirely difficult, and it is truly pure gold."

His solution is to make the verse a dialogue in two voices: לך לבי אמר (השם): בקשו פני. ולבו משיב: את פניך ה׳ אבקש — "*To you, my heart*, God said: seek My face. And his heart replies: Your face, LORD, I seek." God addresses the heart; the heart answers. It is the reading that best explains why אֲבַקֵּשׁ appears in the first person at the end of a verse that began with a plural imperative — there are two speakers, and they have just agreed.

What the verse does not tell you is where that divine command comes from. One striking parallel lies in Hosea, and the context is bleak: אֵלֵךְ אָשׁוּבָה אֶל־מְקוֹמִי עַד אֲשֶׁר־יֶאְשְׁמוּ וּבִקְשׁוּ פָנָי, "I will go and return to My place until they acknowledge their guilt and seek My face" (Hos 5:15). The command "seek My face" is issued *by a God who has withdrawn it*. That may explain the sequence here: verse 8 quotes the summons, and verse 9 immediately begs that the withdrawal which motivates such summonses not happen.

The idiom also has an Akkadian parallel. *amāru pān ili*, "to see the face of the god," was technical language for visiting a temple and beholding the cult statue. Israel had no statue, and the phrase helps explain how what survives here can mean securing an audience with the King.

**Verse 9**

אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי, אַל־תַּט בְּאַף עַבְדֶּךָ — עֶזְרָתִי הָיִיתָ — אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי וְאַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי, אֱלֹקֵי יִשְׁעִי׃

> Do not hide Your face from me; do not turn Your servant aside in anger — my help You have been — do not cast me off and do not abandon me, God of my rescue.

Four negations, and the sound is relentless: `mim-men-**NIY**` … `'al-tit-**TSHĒ**-niy` … `wə-'al-ta-ʿaz-**VĒ**-niy`. The same -*ni* ending, "me," lands again and again; the verbs change and the object does not.

Meiri makes one compression worth keeping: the second plea is not a new request but a restatement of the first, since הסתר הפנים וריחוקם הוא האף והחמה, the hiding and distancing of the face *is* the anger. There is no separate divine wrath to fear; the averted face is the wrath.

The clause אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי appears in only one other psalm, verbatim: Psalm 102:3, אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי בְּיוֹם צַר־לִי, "do not hide Your face from me in the day of my distress." And Psalm 13:2 has the same thought in the interrogative: עַד־אָנָה תַּסְתִּיר אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי, "how long will You hide Your face from me?" Three psalms, one clause, three time-signatures. Psalm 13 speaks from inside a hiding already underway. Psalm 102 speaks on the day the trouble arrives. Psalm 27 speaks before the hiding has happened — and that is one reason it feels different from a lament. It is not protesting an absence; it is trying to prevent one.

The modern poet who lives longest inside this verse is R. S. Thomas, an Anglican priest in rural Wales who spent forty years writing about a God who declines to appear. "Via Negativa," from 1972, begins:

> Why no! I never thought other than

> That God is that great absence

> In our lives, the empty silence

> Within, the place where we go

> Seeking, not in hope to

> Arrive or find.

Thomas and the psalmist share a vocabulary — seeking, absence, a face that may not be there — and take opposite positions on it. Thomas has made peace: the seeking is the whole of the religious life, and arrival is not the point. The psalmist regards that arrangement as the catastrophe he is writing to avert. He wants to arrive; he wants the face; he has ordered his entire poem around the request. Reading them together clarifies what is at stake in the fourfold אַל: the psalmist is not a mystic of absence but a courtier who knows exactly what happens to a petitioner the king stops looking at.

**Verse 10**

כִּי־אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי, וַה׳ יַאַסְפֵנִי׃

> For my father and my mother have abandoned me, and the LORD will gather me in.

Father-and-mother is a fixed pair in West Semitic poetry, attested in Ugaritic as *ʾab* // *ʾum*, and it functions here as a way of saying *everyone*: name the two bonds nobody breaks, declare them broken, and there is nothing left to lose. The verbs are pointedly out of step — עֲזָבוּנִי is done, יַאַסְפֵנִי is not yet. Human failure in the past tense, divine gathering in the future.

אָסַף is the surprising choice. It is a harvest word and a burial word — the idiom for dying is וַיֵּאָסֵף אֶל־עַמָּיו, "he was gathered to his people," and Job calls death בֵּית מוֹעֵד לְכָל־חָי, "the meeting-house of all the living." Zechariah, however, uses the semantic neighbor for something warmer: אֶשְׁרְקָה לָהֶם וַאֲקַבְּצֵם כִּי פְדִיתִים, "I will whistle to them and gather them, for I have redeemed them" (Zech 10:8) — a shepherd calling in strays. Here the verb means neither death nor harvest but adoption: God performing the office of kin for someone who has none.

Rashi will not read the abandonment as hypothetical, and his solution is genuinely shocking:

> בִּשְׁעַת תַּשְׁמִישׁ לַהֲנָאָתָן נִתְכַּוְּנוּ; כֵּיוָן שֶׁגָּמְרוּ הֲנָאָתָן, זֶה הוֹפֵךְ פָּנָיו אֵילָךְ וְזֶה הוֹפֵךְ פָּנָיו אֵילָךְ — "at the time of intercourse they intended their own pleasure. As soon as their pleasure was over, this one turns his face one way and that one turns her face the other way." And then: הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא שׁוֹמֵר אֶת הַטִּפָּה וְצָר אֶת הָעֻבָּר — "the Holy One guards the drop and forms the fetus."

Rashi has relocated the abandonment to the moment of conception, and in doing so has made the verse universal rather than autobiographical: every human being was left by both parents at the instant of coming into existence, and formed by God in the interval. Note the mechanism of the reading — Rashi seizes on the *turning of faces*. Two parents turn their faces away; the psalmist has just spent a verse begging God not to. The midrash is built on the psalm's own governing image.

The verse's afterlife is a long record of people finding themselves in it. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks tells of a congregant who came to him puzzled by it during Elul: "Which Jewish parent had ever rejected his or her own son? And why did we say this verse at this time of the year?" Sacks answered with Ishmael — a child of two parents' desperation, sent into the desert, heard by God under a bush. Elsewhere he returns to the verse again and again as the ground of a single claim: "God believes in us even if we don't believe in ourselves." Seven separate references across his corpus, all to this one half-verse. It is the line that a modern reader, given the whole psalm, keeps stopping at.

**Verses 11–12**

הוֹרֵנִי ה׳ דַּרְכֶּךָ, וּנְחֵנִי בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי׃

> Teach me Your way, LORD, and lead me on a level path, because of those who watch me.

אַל־תִּתְּנֵנִי בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי, כִּי קָמוּ־בִי עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, וִיפֵחַ חָמָס׃

> Do not give me over to the appetite of my foes, for false witnesses have risen against me — and a witness of violence.

בְּאֹרַח מִישׁוֹר, "on a level path," occurs nowhere else. Psalm 143:10 has the nearly identical בְּאֶרֶץ מִישׁוֹר, "in a land of levelness," and a long line of critics — BDB among them — propose emending that verse to match this one. Whatever the textual history, the request is concrete: after verse 2, in which the enemies כָשְׁלוּ וְנָפָלוּ, "stumbled and fell," the psalmist is asking for ground on which *he* will not do the same.

שׁוֹרְרָי is from שׁור, "to watch, lie in wait" — Klein relates it to Akkadian *šāru*, "slander." These are not soldiers but observers.

Ibn Ezra's gloss on the verse is five words long and it reorganizes the logic:

> אמר אחד מחכמי לב: הרוצה להתגבר על שונאיו — יוסיף בעבודת השם — "One of the wise of heart said: he who wishes to prevail over his enemies should increase his service of God."

The petition "because of my watchers" is not asking for a route that evades them. It is asking for more instruction, on the theory that the way to defeat surveillance is to become more thoroughly obedient. Which is exactly the strategy verse 4 announced.

Verse 12 puts us in a courtroom. עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר, "false witnesses," is Deuteronomic legal vocabulary — Deut 19:18 provides for the case וְהִנֵּה עֵד־שֶׁקֶר הָעֵד, "and behold, the witness is a false witness," and the Decalogue prohibits it outright: לֹא־תַעֲנֶה בְרֵעֲךָ עֵד שָׁקֶר.

Then וִיפֵחַ חָמָס, on which the lexicons offer two derivations and no confidence: Klein lists it as a word that appears only here, meaning "breathing, puffing out," from either יפח or a form of פוח. The traditional rendering makes it a verb — "one who breathes out violence." But Ugaritic supplies a noun *ypḥ*, "witness," and that may explain the line: noun paired with noun, עֵדֵי־שֶׁקֶר // יְפֵחַ חָמָס, "witnesses of falsehood" // "a witness of violence." Proverbs uses the same collocation twice — יָפִיחַ כְּזָבִים עֵד שָׁקֶר, in Prov 6:19 and again in 14:5 — which on this reading would also mean "a witness of lies, a false witness." On that reading, the balancing of the two halves is decisive, and the psalm's courtroom is better served by witnesses than by respiration.

בְּנֶפֶשׁ צָרָי is usually softened to "the will of my foes." But נֶפֶשׁ is also throat and appetite — Rashi glosses בְּתַאֲוַת שׂוֹנְאַי, "in the desire of those who hate me" — and the concrete sense reaches back to verse 2, where the same enemies came לֶאֱכֹל אֶת־בְּשָׂרִי, "to eat my flesh." Do not hand me over to their gullet. The psalm's first image and its last threat are the same image.

Radak identifies what the perjury actually alleges, and it changes the stakes from criminal to theological:

> שהם מעידים בי ומדברים בי דברי חמס לאמר אני רשע ואיש דמים ואין לי חלק ביי' — "they testify against me and speak violent words about me, saying that I am wicked, a man of blood, and that I have no portion in the LORD."

Not a property dispute. The witnesses are testifying that he does not belong in the house he has spent the whole psalm asking to live in. Radak cites the taunt of Psalm 71:11 as their formula: אֱלֹקִים עֲזָבוֹ, "God has abandoned him" — which is the very thing verse 9 begs God not to do.

**Verse 13**

לׅׄוּׅׄלֵׅ֗ׄאׅׄ הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת בְּטוּב־ה׳ בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים׃

> Had I not trusted that I would see the goodness of the LORD in the land of the living —

The sentence stops. לוּלֵא introduces a condition and always, elsewhere, gets its consequence: לוּלֵא חֲרַשְׁתֶּם בְּעֶגְלָתִי לֹא מְצָאתֶם חִידָתִי, "had you not plowed with my heifer, you would not have found out my riddle" (Judg 14:18); the answering clause is sometimes marked with כִּי עַתָּה or אָז. Here BDB simply notes the entry with an exclamation and leaves it: *if I had not believed…!*

Rabbi Sacks named the device and gave it its best gloss. The Greeks called it *aposiopesis*, "becoming silent" — a sentence broken off mid-course because the speaker cannot go on. His illustration is Lear, raging at the daughters who have humiliated him and unable to finish either of his threats:

> I will have such revenges on you both

> That all the world shall — I will do such things —

> What they are yet, I know not; but they shall be

> The terrors of the earth!

Lear breaks off twice from excess of fury, and the effect is that the audience supplies the atrocity he cannot articulate; the unspoken revenge is worse than any revenge he could name. The psalmist breaks off from excess of dread, and the mechanism is identical in reverse: he will not say what he would have become without belief, and so the reader says it for him. Both poets have discovered that the strongest thing a sentence can do is refuse to end. And in both cases the recovery is immediate and slightly desperate — Lear turns on the storm, the psalmist turns to the imperative of verse 14.

The scribes registered their own unease. In the Masoretic text every letter of לׅׄוּׅׄלֵׅ֗ׄאׅׄ carries a dot — above *and* below, the only such word in the Hebrew Bible. Such marks often flag a reading present in some manuscripts and absent in others, and the Septuagint appears to confirm exactly that: it has no conditional at all, only πιστεύω, "I believe." Delete לוּלֵא and the verse becomes a flat affirmation. Keep it and the verse becomes a scream cut off. The Greek translator worked from a text, or made a decision, that spared him the problem.

The Talmud reads the dots as psychology rather than philology. Berakhot 4a, cited by the Torah Temimah, asks how David could call himself pious given this verse, and answers:

> למה נקוד על לולא — אמר דוד לפני הקב"ה: רבש"ע, מובטח אני בך שאתה משלם שכר טוב לצדיקים לעתיד לבוא, אבל איני יודע אם יש לי חלק ביניהם אם לאו, שמא יגרום החטא — "Why is there a dot over *lulei*? David said before the Holy One: Master of the Universe, I am confident in You that You reward the righteous in the world to come — but I do not know whether I have a share among them or not; perhaps sin will cause otherwise."

The derivation runs through the dots themselves: a marked word is an uncertain word, so the uncertainty is transferred from the manuscript to the speaker. Note what this does to the verse's grammar — the dots let the sages read the confidence and the doubt simultaneously, which is precisely what an unfinished conditional achieves. The scribal apparatus becomes a psychological instrument.

אֶרֶץ חַיִּים, "land of the living," is used elsewhere almost exclusively of *this* world as opposed to the Pit — Ezekiel deploys it seven times in his dirges over the nations, each time meaning the world above ground: אֲשֶׁר נָתְנוּ חִתִּית בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים, "who spread terror in the land of the living," now lying with the uncircumcised. On the plain sense the psalmist wants vindication before he dies. Radak takes the opposite view, reads it as the world to come — and, characteristically, feels obliged to defend the geography:

> ואף על פי שאין לנשמה מקום וכל שכן ארץ, אלא לפי שנמשל טוב עולם הבא לגן עדן שהוא מקום בארץ, להבין השומעים נאמר דרך משל — "even though the soul has no place, let alone a *land* — rather, because the good of the world to come is likened to the Garden of Eden, which is a place on earth, it is said by way of metaphor so the hearers will understand."

A rationalist accepting an afterlife but refusing to let it have real estate.

**Verse 14**

קַוֵּה אֶל־ה׳, חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ, וְקַוֵּה אֶל־ה׳׃

> Wait for the LORD; be strong, and let Him make your heart bold — and wait for the LORD.

Who is being addressed? The verse suddenly speaks to a "you," and the candidates are all plausible: a priest answering the petitioner, the psalmist turning on himself, a wisdom teacher instructing a class. Radak's answer is the least dramatic and the most likely: ומרוב בטחוני ואמונתי ביי' אני אומר כנגד עצמי כל היום: קוה אל יי', "out of the abundance of my trust I say to myself, all day long: wait for the LORD." A man talking himself down.

חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ is Joshua-language, echoing the repeated חֲזַק וֶאֱמָץ, "be strong and resolute," spoken by Moses to Joshua and by God to Joshua at the border of the land, and again by David to Solomon before the building of the Temple. In every one of those cases the imperative precedes an action — conquer, apportion, build. Here it precedes nothing but waiting. The military formula is retained and its content removed; the courage is for holding still. And note the small grammatical shift: not וֶאֱמָץ, "and be resolute," but וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ — "and let Him make your heart bold," with God as the one doing the strengthening, exactly as Ibn Ezra reads it. Even the courage is a gift.

The verb itself carries an image. Klein derives קוה from a root meaning to twist or stretch — קַו is a cord, Aramaic קַוָּא a thread, and the Syriac cognate covers enduring and remaining. Waiting, in this vocabulary, is being under tension. The psalm ends with the rope pulled taut, and then pulled again: `qa-**VĒH**` … `wə-qa-**VĒH**`, the same word bracketing the verse.

The Talmud takes the repetition as a legal instruction. Rashi, citing Berakhot 32b: וְאִם לֹא תִתְקַבֵּל תְּפִלָּתְךָ, חֲזוֹר וְקַוֵּה, "and if your prayer is not accepted, go back and hope again." The derivation is exact — the verse says "hope" twice, so a second hoping is prescribed after the first has failed. R. Ḥama b. Ḥanina's formulation in the Talmud is even blunter: אם ראה אדם שהתפלל ולא נענה, יחזור ויתפלל, "if a man sees that he prayed and was not answered, let him pray again."

This is the verse of Psalm 27 that Jews say most often, and almost none of them are saying Psalm 27 when they say it. Across all major rites it introduces *Ein Keloheinu* — weekday Shacharit, Musaf on Shabbat and festivals and the High Holidays: קַוֵּה אֶל ה׳ חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ וְקַוֵּה אֶל ה׳: אֵין קָדוֹשׁ כַּה׳ כִּי אֵין בִּלְתֶּךָ וְאֵין צוּר כֵּאלֹקֵינוּ, "Wait for the LORD, be strong and let your heart take courage, and wait for the LORD. There is none holy like the LORD, for there is none besides You, and there is no rock like our God." A man's private instruction to his own heart has become the doorway into the congregation's declaration that there is nobody else. Which is, at bottom, what he was telling himself.
