### The One Thing, Asked Twice

Read verse 3 and then verse 9, and you will think you are hearing two different people.

אִם־תַּחֲנֶה עָלַי מַחֲנֶה לֹא־יִירָא לִבִּי, "should a whole camp pitch camp against me, my heart will not fear" — this from a man who has just declared God his light, his rescue, his fortress. Six verses later the same voice is pleading: אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי, "do not hide Your face from me," and then twice more, אַל־תִּטְּשֵׁנִי וְאַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי, "do not cast me off, do not abandon me." Since Gunkel it has been standard to call this two psalms stitched together: 27A, a song of trust; 27B, a lament, glued on by an editor with more piety than taste.

But notice what the psalmist is actually afraid of. The army is hypothetical — אִם, "should," "if." The hiding of the face is not hedged at all. He does not say "if You hide Your face." He says: don't. The threat he brushes aside is armed and external; the threat he cannot brush aside is a change of expression on the face of God. Once you see that, the two halves stop looking like two psalms and start looking like one request, made twice — once in the indicative, once in the imperative.

The architecture is straightforward:

- **vv. 1–3** — fearlessness declared, God spoken *about*

- **v. 4** — the one request: to dwell, to gaze, to inquire

- **vv. 5–6** — why: the sanctuary is both hiding place and place of thanks

- **v. 7** — the turn to direct address

- **vv. 8–9** — face-seeking, and the terror of the hidden face

- **v. 10** — parents fail; God gathers

- **vv. 11–12** — guidance, and the courtroom

- **v. 13** — a sentence that breaks off

- **v. 14** — the exhortation, doubled

### Light Is a Face

The two halves are welded at the level of a single idiom, and the weld is invisible in English.

ה׳ אוֹרִי, "the LORD is my light," is nearly unique as a direct statement about God — Micah says the LORD *will be* a light to him, Isaiah calls Him אוֹר יִשְׂרָאֵל, "the light of Israel," but the flat statement here has no close twin. What it does have is a home idiom. In biblical Hebrew, divine light is often *facial*. The priestly blessing asks יָאֵר ה׳ פָּנָיו אֵלֶיךָ, "may the LORD make His face shine toward you" (Num 6:25). Psalm 44, distinguishing military from theological causes of victory, says: לֹא בְחַרְבָּם יָרְשׁוּ אָרֶץ … כִּי־יְמִינְךָ וּזְרוֹעֲךָ וְאוֹר פָּנֶיךָ, "not by their sword did they take the land … but Your right hand, Your arm, and the light of Your face."

So when this psalm says "the LORD is my light," it has already implied "the LORD's face is turned toward me" — and when it later says "do not hide Your face," it is asking that the light of verse 1 not go out. Verse 1 and verse 9 are the two poles of one metaphor. The confidence and the dread are not two moods awkwardly juxtaposed; they are the front and back of the same claim. And the tradition heard it: in the Sefardic rite, when the priests reach יִשָּׂא ה׳ פָּנָיו אֵלֶיךָ, "may the LORD lift His face toward you," the congregation answers with this psalm's אַל־תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנִּי. Blessing and terror, antiphonally, in the same breath.

### The Psalm Keeps Its Word

Verse 4 makes a promise most poems would break: אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־ה׳ אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ, "one thing I have asked of the LORD; that is what I seek." The phrase אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. And the psalm keeps its word — it never asks for a second thing.

The proof is a verb. אֲבַקֵּשׁ, "I seek," appears exactly twice in the psalm: at verse 4, of the one request, and at verse 8, אֶת־פָּנֶיךָ ה׳ אֲבַקֵּשׁ, "Your face, LORD, I seek." Identical form, one in each half. The confident section names the desire; the petitionary section voices it. Everything else is subordinate. The guidance in verse 11 is asked לְמַעַן שׁוֹרְרָי, "because of my watchers" — instrumentally. The rescue from false witnesses in verse 12 is asked so that he can go on standing where he wants to stand. The enemies never become the subject of the request.

Radak saw the mechanism, and his reading is uncomfortably modern. Why would a man confident of rescue ask for anything at all? Because, Radak says, war eats attention: לבו טרוד בהם ומתעסק בצרכי הגוף ואין לו פנאי להתעסק בצרכי הנפש, "his heart is preoccupied with them and busy with the needs of the body, and he has no leisure to attend to the needs of the soul." What David asks is not survival but quiet — that God still the wars so that he will have *time*. On this reading, the enemies of Psalm 27 are dangerous chiefly because they are distracting. That is not a threat medieval piety invented; it is the threat anyone recognizes who has tried to pray while being sued.

The psalm's spatial imagery follows the same logic. Verse 1 offers a military noun, מָעוֹז, "stronghold." Verse 5 abandons it: God will hide him בְּסֻכֹּה, "in His booth" — a structure of interwoven branches, the flimsiest shelter in the Hebrew vocabulary, the thing Isaiah uses as an image of Zion's vulnerability. A fortress protects by thickness. A booth protects only because of who is inside it with you. The poem trades masonry for proximity, which is precisely the exchange verse 4 announced.

Leonard Bernstein, composing the *Chichester Psalms* in 1965, wanted a movement that set serenity against violence and built it by pitting Psalm 23 in a boy's voice against Psalm 2's raging nations. He was reaching for an effect Psalm 27 already contains — with the difference that in Bernstein the two texts interrupt each other, while here they are the same speaker, and the serenity has already accounted for the rage.

### Where It Ends

The last verse gives the psalm's answer, and it is not resolution. קַוֵּה אֶל־ה׳, "wait for the LORD" — the root קוה carries the sense of twisting or stretching; the noun קַו is a cord, and Aramaic and Syriac cognates give threads and looms. To wait, in this vocabulary, is to be a rope under tension. The psalm ends with the cord pulled taut and doubled: קַוֵּה … וְקַוֵּה, said twice, because once was apparently not convincing.

And the verse before it does not finish. לוּלֵא הֶאֱמַנְתִּי לִרְאוֹת בְּטוּב־ה׳ בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים, "had I not trusted that I would see the LORD's goodness in the land of the living—". Then nothing. The clause that should complete it never comes, and the scribes marked their unease by dotting every letter of לוּלֵא, the only word in the Hebrew Bible dotted above *and* below.

Strip off the fortress, the booth, the rock, the level path, the courtroom. What is left is a man who has already been left — by the two people in the world who were not supposed to leave — asking one thing of the only one remaining. Not victory. Not even rescue. Just: keep your face where I can see it.

#### Full psalm

Psalm 27 is recited in full, twice daily, from the first of Elul through Hoshana Rabbah — after Shacharit and after Mincha or Maariv — across Ashkenazi, Hasidic, and Mizrachi/Sephardic rites, typically following *Aleinu* and the closing Kaddish. It is the season's signature text, and the custom is startlingly young: it is absent from the Talmud and from the *Shulchan Aruch*, surfacing only in eighteenth-century sources (the *Shem Tov Katan* of 1706, Jacob Emden's *Siddur Beit Yaakov* of 1745), with a probable line of descent through the *Chemdat Yamim* — a work long suspected of Sabbatean sympathies. Adopted by the Baal Shem Tov's circle and then far more widely, it is a case of liturgical usefulness quietly overruling pedigree.

The rationale is a homily on verse 1 read against the calendar: אוֹרִי, "my light," is Rosh Hashanah, when deeds are illuminated; וְיִשְׁעִי, "and my salvation," is Yom Kippur; and כִּי יִצְפְּנֵנִי בְּסֻכֹּה, "for He will hide me in His booth," is Sukkot. The three festivals of the season are read out of a single verse and its sequel — which is also why the psalm stops on Hoshana Rabbah rather than at the end of Tishrei.

#### Key verses and phrases

**v. 14** is the most widely recited line, serving across all major rites as the introduction to *Ein Keloheinu* — in weekday Shacharit and in Musaf on Shabbat, festivals, and the High Holidays: קַוֵּה אֶל ה׳ חֲזַק וְיַאֲמֵץ לִבֶּךָ וְקַוֵּה אֶל ה׳: אֵין קָדוֹשׁ כַּה׳ כִּי אֵין בִּלְתֶּךָ, "Hope in the LORD, be strong and let your heart take courage, and hope in the LORD. There is none holy like the LORD, for there is none but You." Private exhortation becomes the hinge into communal affirmation.

**v. 9** is the verse of fast days. In *Aneinu*, inserted into the weekday Amidah, the plural voice takes it over: אַל תֵּפֶן אֶל רִשְׁעֵנוּ וְאַל תַּסְתֵּר פָּנֶיךָ מִמֶּנּוּ, "turn not to our wickedness, and hide not Your face from us." It appears in Selichot, in the Sephardic and Mizrachi *El Erech Apayim* before the weekday Torah reading, and — most pointedly — as the congregation's responsive line during the Priestly Blessing in the Sephardic rite, answering the priests' request that God lift His face with a request that He not withdraw it.

**v. 7** is voiced by the prayer leader on Rosh Hashanah in the Ashkenazi *piyyut* (liturgical poem) introducing the expanded Kedushah: שָׁפַכְתִּי שִׂיחַ לְבַקֵּשׁ עֲתִירָה. שְׁמַע ה׳ קוֹלִי אֶקְרָא, "I have poured out my speech to seek entreaty. Hear, O LORD, my voice when I call." In the Edot HaMizrach Selichot for Yom Kippur eve, it sits inside a chain of "hear" verses, each pressing the same imperative.

**vv. 4–5** are repurposed for the dead. The Mizrachi *Hashkavah* memorial prayer asks that God יַסְתִּירֵהוּ בְּצֵל כְּנָפָיו וּבְסֵתֶר אָהֳלוֹ לַחֲזוֹת בְּנוֹעַם ה׳ וּלְבַקֵּר בְּהֵיכָלוֹ, "hide him in the shadow of His wings and in the shelter of His tent, to gaze upon the pleasantness of the LORD and to visit His temple." A living man's plea for asylum becomes a soul's itinerary — a reading verse 13's "land of the living" resists, and which the medievals nonetheless licensed.
